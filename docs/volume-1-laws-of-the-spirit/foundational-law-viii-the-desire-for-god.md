# Foundational Law VIII: The Desire-for-God Law

This law belongs right next to the Drawing-Near Reciprocity Law (FL.VII). Drawing-Near tells you how it works: when a person moves toward God, He moves toward them. But what makes a person move toward God in the first place? What is the heart actually doing when it starts the seeking that Drawing-Near describes? This law gives the answer. There is a longing built into the heart that points toward God before you even know what it is. That longing is the inward sign that you were made for Him, and it is the engine of the moving-toward-Him that Drawing-Near describes. No longing, no seeking; no seeking, and there is nothing for God to draw near to.

C. S. Lewis pointed to it in Surprised by Joy — that "inconsolable secret," an ache that turned out to be a signpost toward the country we were made for. Augustine put it at the very start of his Confessions — "You have made us for yourself, O Lord, and our heart is restless until it rests in you." The Eastern church called it *eros* for God (in Maximus the Confessor and Gregory of Nyssa); the Western church called it *desiderium*, "desire"; and the contemplative writers down the centuries called it the engine of the inner life. The longing is the same; only the names change. And Scripture names it across both Testaments through at least nine different writers.

## The Scriptural Ground
The heart's longing for God is named all across both Testaments, in many different voices and by many writers. The Old Testament psalms carry the raw feel of it; the wisdom books name its shape; the New Testament letters spell out what it means; the Gospels bless it; and Revelation closes the whole Bible with the invitation it holds out.

Eccl. 3:11 (ESV): “He has made everything beautiful in its time. Also, he has put eternity into man’s heart, yet so that he cannot find out what God has done from the beginning to the end.” The key claim: God has set eternity inside the human heart. A creature with limits has been given room for the limitless. The result is a heart that can't be fully satisfied with limited things alone, because limited things are not what it was made for.

Ps. 42:1–2 (sons of Korah, ESV): “As a deer pants for flowing streams, so pants my soul for you, O God. My soul thirsts for God, for the living God. When shall I come and appear before God?” Ps. 63:1 (David, ESV): “O God, you are my God; earnestly I seek you; my soul thirsts for you; my flesh faints for you, as in a dry and weary land where there is no water.” Here you feel it — thirst as a real ache in body and soul, not some vague wish, and aimed at one specific Person whose face the psalmist longs to see.

Isa. 26:9 (ESV): “My soul yearns for you in the night; my spirit within me earnestly seeks you.” The seeking grows out of the yearning; the yearning has to be there first before any seeking begins.

Matt. 5:6 (ESV): “Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall be satisfied.” The Sermon on the Mount calls this hunger-and-thirst blessed; the follower who longs this way is living right inside that Beatitude. Phil. 3:8, 14 (ESV): “Indeed, I count everything as loss because of the surpassing worth of knowing Christ Jesus my Lord… I press on toward the goal for the prize of the upward call of God in Christ Jesus.” Paul’s longing is named, pointed, and costly; the pressing-on in v. 14 is simply the longing of v. 8 put into action.

Heb. 11:13–16 (ESV): “These all died in faith, not having received the things promised, but having seen them and greeted them from afar, and having acknowledged that they were strangers and exiles on the earth… they desire a better country, that is, a heavenly one. Therefore God is not ashamed to be called their God, for he has prepared for them a city.” The longing points forward and homeward: the heart was made for a homeland not in this world, and the sense of being a stranger here is the inward sign that you were made for there.

1 John 3:2–3 (ESV): “Beloved, we are God’s children now, and what we will be has not yet appeared; but we know that when he appears we shall be like him, because we shall see him as he is. And everyone who thus hopes in him purifies himself as he is pure.” John ties the longing to what we will one day see: we hope to see Him as He is, and that hope itself makes us purer. The longing actually shapes us.

Rev. 22:17 (ESV): “The Spirit and the Bride say, ‘Come.’ And let the one who hears say, ‘Come.’ And let the one who is thirsty come; let the one who desires take the water of life without price.” The Bible's closing words are themselves an invitation to everyone who is thirsty and longing. It is the longing that brings a person to the water.

## The Mechanism
Three things about how this law works.

First, the longing is built in — part of the heart’s design from the start, not something you pick up along the way. Eccl. 3:11 says it most plainly: God has set eternity inside the human heart. Room for the limitless has been built into a creature made to bear the image of a limitless God, and so the heart can't rest in limited things alone, because limited things are not what it was made for.

Second, the longing is at work before you are even aware of it. Most people feel “something missing,” “a longing I can’t pin down,” or what Lewis called “the inconsolable secret” — without knowing what it is actually for. That quiet, below-the-surface pull is the law already working: the heart’s design tugging you toward the One it was made for, whether or not you have consciously turned that way yet. Putting a name to what the longing is really for — Christ, the Beauty it was after all along — is itself a step of growth, one the formation work in Vol 2 helps along. The person who pays attention to her longing finds that formation work doing exactly what it is meant to do.

Third, there is a telltale sign that sets this longing apart from stand-in desires. Stand-in desires (food, sex, achievement, comfort) fade once you get the thing — a fed appetite is a quiet appetite. The longing for God does the opposite: it grows the more you come to know Him, because the One you are coming to know is without limit. You can see this pattern plainly in Phil. 3:8, 14 (Paul already knows Christ, counts everything else as loss, and still presses on for more) and Heb. 11:13–16 (the faithful saw the promises from far off, welcomed them, and kept going as strangers and exiles seeking the better country). The fact that it deepens instead of fading is the giveaway: a longing that grows the closer you get is the longing for God; a longing that fades once it is satisfied is something else.

## Why This Is a Foundational Law
Nine passages from at least nine different writers — Solomon, the sons of Korah, David, Isaiah, Matthew, Paul, the writer of Hebrews, John in 1 John, and John in Revelation — across both Testaments and several kinds of writing (wisdom, lament, prophecy, gospel, letter, and the visions of Revelation). The core idea — the heart was made for God; the longing is proof of it; the longing is what drives the seeking — is uncontested across mainstream Christian traditions. Augustine’s opening line in the Confessions is the great Western statement of it; Gregory of Nyssa and Maximus the Confessor say it in the Eastern language of *eros*; the Reformed tradition calls it the God-ward bent of the image of God, now bent out of shape by sin; the contemplative writers down the centuries call it the engine of the inner life; and C. S. Lewis’s Surprised by Joy and The Weight of Glory are its clearest modern statements.

It is also easy to see at work, and well-documented. A person whose longing for God has been woken up — named, paid attention to, pointed toward the One it is really for — lives differently from a person whose longing has been shut down (numbed), swapped out (the false self’s cheap substitutes, which is the turf of FL.III Heart-Throne), or explained away (the religious-performance pattern that doesn’t actually want God). Formation Companions see this firsthand: people who name their longing find that naming it changes how it works.

This law pairs with FL.VII Drawing-Near Reciprocity (Drawing-Near is the longing actually put into motion) and with FL.III Heart-Throne (Heart-Throne names what the heart puts on its throne; this law names what the heart was made to want before idol worship steered it elsewhere). And with FL.XIII Pure-Heart Vision the whole arc comes full circle: longing stirs up seeking, the seeking is met by God drawing near, His drawing near makes the heart pure, and the pure in heart see God — which is what the longing was reaching for all along.

**Proposed Law (Mixed Structural-Operational, Wide Consent): The human heart, made for God, produces a longing for Him as a built-in part of its design (Eccl. 3:11). This longing is there before you know what it is for, outlasts every attempt to fill it with something else, and works as the inward sign that you were made for life with God in Christ. Coming to recognize what the longing is really for, and aiming it there, happens in stages: from a vague inner ache, to realizing no created thing can fill it, to recognizing Christ as the Beauty it was after all along, to the longing growing deeper as you draw nearer (1 John 3:2–3; Phil. 3:8, 14). In practice: the person who pays attention to her longing and aims it at the One it is for finds the other Foundational Laws (Drawing-Near, Pure-Heart Vision, and the rest) working as they should; the person whose longing has been shut down, swapped out, or numbed finds those same laws sitting idle. The law runs between you and God.**

***Certainty: Clearly Taught.**** The backing from many writers is extremely strong (nine writers across both Testaments). Acceptance is just about universal across mainstream Christian traditions; they differ only in how they explain the longing’s link to creation, sin, grace, and the life to come — the basic claim that the longing exists and works is uncontested. It is also well-documented in pastoral, contemplative, and modern theological writing. The one open question is mostly about how exactly the longing-is-built-in claim relates to the doctrine of the image of God, which traditions explain differently without changing how the law works.*
