# Foundational Law XXXVI: The Eschatological Glory Law

This law is about how the future God has promised pulls on our hearts right now. At the end of all things, God will complete everything — His glory fully revealed, His people gathered home. This law is about the way that coming glory reaches back into the present and draws God's people forward.

This law belongs to a family of laws about how the world to come shapes us now, and it touches a particular part of that. (The Suffering-as-Formation Loop is about how the End gives meaning to our suffering; the Attention Economy of the Soul is about where we fix our attention; the Worship Alignment Law is about how worship gets aimed.) This law is about the heart's longing for what God will complete — the way looking ahead to that coming glory shapes our hope, our willingness to sacrifice, our pursuit of purity, and where we set our sights right now.

The New Testament names this pull again and again. Paul: "this light momentary affliction is preparing for us an eternal weight of glory beyond all comparison" (2 Cor. 4:17). Hebrews: "they sought a better country, that is, a heavenly one... for there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God" (Heb. 11:16; 4:9). John: "we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is; and everyone who thus hopes in him purifies himself" (1 John 3:2–3). Peter: "set your hope fully on the grace that will be brought to you at the revelation of Jesus Christ" (1 Pet. 1:13). In each case, the coming glory is reaching into the present and pulling the heart forward.

## Scriptural Ground

At least four New Testament writers teach this pull of the world to come on the present life — Paul across several letters, the writer of Hebrews, John (in 1 John and Revelation), and Peter — with deep roots in the Old Testament, in Isaiah's visions of the end and the Psalms' longing for God. That's far more than the catalog's minimum of two, and it puts this law near the center of Scripture.

Paul teaches it across several letters. Romans 8:18–25 lays the foundation: "For I consider that the sufferings of this present time are not worth comparing with the glory that is to be revealed to us. For the creation waits with eager longing for the revealing of the sons of God... For in this hope we were saved. Now hope that is seen is not hope. For who hopes for what he sees? But if we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it with patience." Three things stand out. First, present suffering looks different in light of the glory still to come — the coming completion changes what our trials mean. Second, the hope works precisely because we can't yet see it; a hope you can already see isn't this kind of hope at all — its whole force is the pull of what's still unseen. Third, the response it produces is patient waiting — the heart that looks ahead learns to endure present hardship with patience.

2 Corinthians 4:16–18 shows how it works: "So we do not lose heart. Though our outer self is wasting away, our inner self is being renewed day by day. For this light momentary affliction is preparing for us an eternal weight of glory beyond all comparison, as we look not to the things that are seen but to the things that are unseen. For the things that are seen are transient, but the things that are unseen are eternal." The chain is clear: by fixing our eyes on what's unseen and eternal rather than on what's visible and passing, we don't lose heart, and our inner self is renewed day by day. Looking ahead is the path; not losing heart is the result; and the "eternal weight of glory" is what we're looking toward.

2 Corinthians 5:1–10 brings in the body: "For we know that if the tent that is our earthly home is destroyed, we have a building from God, a house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens... So we are always of good courage. We know that while we are at home in the body we are away from the Lord, for we walk by faith, not by sight... So whether we are at home or away, we make it our aim to please him."

Philippians 3:13–14 gives the picture of it: "Brothers, I do not consider that I have made it my own. But one thing I do: forgetting what lies behind and straining forward to what lies ahead, I press on toward the goal for the prize of the upward call of God in Christ Jesus." The image is a runner straining toward the finish line — leaning forward, pressing on, every effort aimed at what's ahead. That's the posture of a heart pulled by what's coming.

Philippians 3:20–21 names where we truly belong: "But our citizenship is in heaven, and from it we await a Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ, who will transform our lowly body to be like his glorious body, by the power that enables him even to subject all things to himself." Our home isn't really here and now; it's there and still to come. A heart whose true home is heaven lives out of that home.

Colossians 3:1–4 gives the command that puts it into practice: "If then you have been raised with Christ, seek the things that are above, where Christ is, seated at the right hand of God. Set your minds on things that are above, not on things that are on the earth. For you have died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God. When Christ who is your life appears, then you also will appear with him in glory." Seeking the things above and setting your mind on them, day after day, is the very path this law calls for.

1 Thessalonians 4:13–18 brings the hope to bear on the hardest moment of all — grief at the death of those we love: "But we do not want you to be uninformed, brothers, about those who are asleep, that you may not grieve as others do who have no hope. For since we believe that Jesus died and rose again, even so, through Jesus, God will bring with him those who have fallen asleep... Therefore encourage one another with these words." Because Jesus died and rose, and will bring His people with Him at the end, Christians grieve — but not as those who have no hope.

2 Timothy 4:7–8 shows it at the end of a life: "I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith. Henceforth there is laid up for me the crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the righteous judge, will award to me on that day, and not only to me but also to all who have loved his appearing." A life shaped by this law is one that has, all along, "loved his appearing" — longed for the day Christ returns.

Hebrews teaches it across several chapters. Hebrews 11:13–16 shows the pull at work in the Old Testament saints: "These all died in faith, not having received the things promised, but having seen them and greeted them from afar, and having acknowledged that they were strangers and exiles on the earth. For people who speak thus make it clear that they are seeking a homeland. If they had been thinking of that land from which they had gone out, they would have had opportunity to return. But as it is, they desire a better country, that is, a heavenly one. Therefore God is not ashamed to be called their God, for he has prepared for them a city." Abraham, Sarah, and the rest were drawn forward by a homeland they had not yet seen. So this pull isn't a New Testament invention — it's how faith has always worked, in both Testaments.

Hebrews 12:1–2 shows it carrying us through hardship — and points to Jesus Himself as the great example: "Therefore, since we are surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses, let us also lay aside every weight, and sin which clings so closely, and let us run with endurance the race that is set before us, looking to Jesus, the founder and perfecter of our faith, who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising the shame, and is seated at the right hand of the God." It was "the joy set before him" — the glory He knew was coming — that carried Jesus through the cross. The same pull is meant to carry us.

Hebrews 13:14 says it in a single line: "For here we have no lasting city, but we seek the city that is to come." No place here is our lasting home; we're always looking for the city that's coming.

John teaches it in 1 John and Revelation. 1 John 3:2–3 shows how the hope makes us pure: "Beloved, we are God's children now, and what we will be has not yet appeared; but we know that when he appears we shall be like him, because we shall see him as he is. And everyone who thus hopes in him purifies himself as he is pure." Hoping to see Christ one day, and to be made like Him, moves a person to start becoming pure now. The purity isn't something separate; it's what the hope looks like, lived out in the present.

Revelation 21–22 paints the picture in full of what's coming: the new heavens and new earth (21:1); the holy city, the new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God (21:2); God dwelling with His people (21:3); every tear wiped away (21:4); the throne of God and of the Lamb (22:3); His servants worshiping Him and seeing His face (22:3–4); the river of the water of life and the tree of life (22:1–2); and the closing invitation: "The Spirit and the Bride say, 'Come.' And let the one who hears say, 'Come.' And let the one who is thirsty come; let the one who desires take the water of life without price" (22:17). This is the specific future the heart is meant to be drawn toward — not a vague "someday," but this.

Peter teaches it too. 1 Peter 1:3–9 packs it all together: "Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ! According to his great mercy, he has caused us to be born again to a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead, to an inheritance that is imperishable, undefiled, and unfading, kept in heaven for you, who by God's power are being guarded through faith for a salvation ready to be revealed in the last time. In this you rejoice, though now for a little while, if necessary, you have been grieved by various trials, so that the tested genuineness of your faith — more precious than gold that perishes though it is tested by fire — may be found to result in praise and glory and honor at the revelation of Jesus Christ. Though you have not seen him, you love him. Though you do not now see him, you believe in him and rejoice with joy that is inexpressible and filled with glory, obtaining the outcome of your faith, the salvation of your souls." A living hope, an inheritance kept safe in heaven, real joy even in the middle of trials, love and trust for a Christ we haven't yet seen — it's all here.

1 Peter 1:13 turns it into a command: "Therefore, preparing your minds for action, and being sober-minded, set your hope fully on the grace that will be brought to you at the revelation of Jesus Christ." Setting your hope on what's coming isn't a mood that drifts over you; it's something you do on purpose, with a ready mind.

2 Peter 3:11–13 ties it to holy living: "Since all these things are thus to be dissolved, what sort of people ought you to be in lives of holiness and godliness, waiting for and hastening the coming of the day of God, because of which the heavens will be set on fire and dissolved, and the heavenly bodies will melt as they burn! But according to his promise we are waiting for new heavens and a new earth in which righteousness dwells." Waiting and longing for the day of God makes a person want to live a holy and godly life now.

The Old Testament lays the groundwork. Isaiah 25:6–9 pictures the great feast at the end: "On this mountain the LORD of hosts will make for all peoples a feast of rich food, a feast of well-aged wine, of rich food full of marrow, of aged wine well refined. And he will swallow up on this mountain the covering that is cast over all peoples, the veil that is spread over all nations. He will swallow up death forever; and the Lord GOD will wipe away tears from all faces, and the reproach of his people he will take away from all the earth, for the LORD has spoken." Isaiah 65:17–25 promises the new heavens and new earth: "For behold, I create new heavens and a new earth, and the former things shall not be remembered or come into mind. But be glad and rejoice forever in that which I create; for behold, I create Jerusalem to be a joy, and her people to be a gladness." And the Psalms ache for it — Ps. 17:15: "As for me, I shall behold your face in righteousness; when I awake, I shall be satisfied with your likeness"; and Ps. 73:24–26: "You guide me with your counsel, and afterward you will receive me to glory. Whom have I in heaven but you? And there is nothing on earth that I desire besides you." All of this is the soil the New Testament's teaching grows out of.

Put together, the witness is strong — at least four New Testament writers teach it (Paul across at least seven letters, the writer of Hebrews, John in 1 John and Revelation, and Peter in 1 and 2 Peter), with deep Old Testament roots in Isaiah and the Psalms. Far more than the catalog's minimum of two, and clearly near the center of Scripture.

## Mechanism

Here is how it works. As the heart keeps looking ahead to what God will complete, that looking shapes our present life — our hope, our willingness to sacrifice, our pursuit of purity, and where we set our sights. We can look at it on three levels.

**Level 1 — what the heart is looking forward to.** This isn't a vague hope that things will get better someday; it's the specific future Scripture describes: God's glory revealed when all things are completed; the marriage supper of the Lamb; the new heavens and new earth; the resurrection of the body; God's people gathered to see Christ as He is; every tear wiped away; God dwelling with His people; the kingdom in its finished form. The specifics matter — a heart drawn toward this concrete picture is changed in ways a vague "someday" never could. We come to know this future through Scripture (especially Revelation 21–22, Isaiah 25 and 65, Jesus' teaching about the end, and the letters of Paul and Peter), and we keep it before us through a Scripture-shaped imagination, through worship that looks ahead to the marriage supper, through the Lord's Supper as a foretaste of what's coming, and through all the catalog's other formation paths used with the end in view.

**Level 2 — how the coming glory draws the heart forward.** It works by the heart coming to treat that promised completion as more real, more solid, and more weighty than anything we can see right now. When the future weighs more than the present, four things happen. Present hardship looks different — Paul's "light momentary affliction" shrinks beside the "eternal weight of glory beyond all comparison" (2 Cor. 4:17). Sacrifice becomes possible — Jesus, "for the joy that was set before him endured the cross" (Heb. 12:2); Paul, "forgetting what lies behind and straining forward to what lies ahead" (Phil. 3:13). Holiness gets its motive — "everyone who thus hopes in him purifies himself as he is pure" (1 John 3:3); "what sort of people ought you to be in lives of holiness and godliness" (2 Pet. 3:11). And over-investment in what's passing away gets corrected — "set your minds on things that are above, not on things that are on the earth" (Col. 3:2); "for here we have no lasting city, but we seek the city that is to come" (Heb. 13:14). Notice it doesn't work by taking away present hardship; it works by changing how the hardship looks under the weight of what's coming. The result is hope that holds through trial, willingness to sacrifice for what's ahead, a pull toward purity as we wait to see Him, and our sights set on things above — not a denial that present life is hard.

**Level 3 — how this forward-look is grown.** You don't get this orientation just by agreeing that the doctrines are true; it's grown over time, through practice. Several of the catalog's paths build it. Steady time in Scripture (V1.Exp1 Faith-Hearing) — especially in Revelation 21–22, the end-promises of Paul and Peter, and the Old Testament visions — deepens the picture in the heart. Worship together (FL.XXXII Communal Worship Heart-Alignment) — a gathered service that looks ahead to the marriage supper of the Lamb is a rehearsal now of that coming celebration. The Lord's Supper, in traditions that take it as a foretaste of the end, is a bodily rehearsal of what's coming. Teaching passed down (FL.XXVII Thick Practice Transmission) hands the orientation from one generation to the next. And hope held together through hard times (FL.XXII Endurance-Hope) leans on this very law as its ground. The forward-look grows over time, through lived practice, not by head-agreement alone — and all these paths together are how it's built.

And it runs both ways. The same pull toward a coming end works wherever the heart aims its hopes. Aimed at God's promised completion, it produces all the good fruit named above. Aimed at a rival "end" — a perfect society, the march of progress, a technological fix, the fully-realized self, the family name carried on, an institution that lasts forever — it produces the same pull forward, but in service of the wrong goal. The Mirror below traces this other direction.

## Why This Is a Foundational Law

This law meets all four tests for inclusion.

First, the scriptural backing is exceptional. At least four New Testament writers teach it (Paul across seven letters, the writer of Hebrews, John in 1 John and Revelation, and Peter in 1 and 2 Peter), with deep Old Testament roots in Isaiah and the Psalms — far more than the catalog's minimum of two, and running through the apostles, Hebrews, Peter, John, and the prophets.

Second, every mainstream Christian tradition accepts the core claim — that the coming glory pulls on the present life. Catholics hold it (the "beatific vision" — seeing God face to face — as the heart's final goal; worship and the sacraments as a foretaste of the end). The Orthodox hold it (the centrality of resurrection hope; worship that reaches ahead to the world to come). The Reformed hold it (the end as the fulfillment of God's covenant; the "already / not yet" shape of the kingdom). Lutherans hold it (the kingdom still to come). Wesleyans hold it (glorification as the finishing of God's work of making us holy). The Anabaptists hold it (an end-shaped discipleship). And the wider evangelical world holds it (Christ's second coming as the heart's hope). They differ on specific end-times questions — the thousand years, the timing of Christ's return for His church, exactly how the present and future kingdom relate, whether all will finally be saved — but they agree on the basic claim: the pull of the coming completion on the present life is real and deeply practical.

Third, the law clearly runs in one direction: keep your sights set on the world to come → hope holds through trial, sacrifice becomes possible, purity is pursued, and your eyes stay on things above; let that forward-look fade → and over-investment in what's passing away takes its place instead.

Fourth, it works as a principle. The principle is simply that the coming completion really does draw the heart forward and change how it lives. And the way that forward-look is built is a Scripture-shaped imagination of the end, worship that looks ahead to the marriage supper, the Lord's Supper as a foretaste, and a steady setting of the heart on "things above."

This law runs from a person up to God: the heart reaches toward the completion God is bringing, resting on His faithfulness to bring it about, and fixed on the specific future Scripture promises.

**Proposed Law: The Eschatological Glory Law. The completion God has promised — His glory fully revealed, His people seeing Christ as He is, the new heavens and new earth made, the marriage supper of the Lamb celebrated, God dwelling with His people forever — is the goal toward which the heart of God's people is drawn all through this present life. Keeping that coming glory in view bears real fruit now: hope that holds through trial, willingness to sacrifice for what's ahead, a pursuit of purity as we wait to see Him, and a heart set on "things above" rather than "things on the earth." When that forward-look is lost, the heart over-invests in what's passing away instead — and loses its hope under trial, refuses sacrifice, gives up on purity, and clings to the visible and temporary.**

***Mirror (stated form):*** *The dark, idol-ward face of this law shows up wherever the heart's forward-look is aimed at some other "end" instead of at the completion God has promised. There are many rival "ends." Political utopias (the perfect society won through political action; the revolution that will finally set things right). The march of culture (history bending toward justice on its own; the cultural shift that will produce a renewed humanity). Technological transformation (the breakthrough that will fix the human condition; the dream of transcending our limits through technology). The fully-realized self (the finished, "actualized" you at the end of the self-improvement journey). The family name carried on (one's legacy, or one's children and grandchildren, as the heart's ultimate concern). And lasting institutions (a church, a nation, or a company imagined to go on forever as the real goal). Each of these pulls the heart toward itself and produces effects that look like the real thing — hope grounded in the false vision, sacrifices made for the substitute goal, a life aimed at the substitute's "things above" — while chasing the wrong end; and the fruit, in the end, shows what was really being trusted. Scripture names these counterfeits. Revelation 18 pictures the doomed lasting-institution: "In a single hour your judgment has come... In a single hour she has been laid waste." Jesus names treasure stored on earth and where it leaves the heart: "for where your treasure is, there your heart will be also" (Matt. 6:19–21). Paul names the marks of a substitute hope: "their end is destruction, their god is their belly, and they glory in their shame, with minds set on earthly things" (Phil. 3:18–19), and the sad collapse of one who lost the forward-look: "Demas, in love with this present world, has deserted me" (2 Tim. 4:10). John draws the line plainly: "Do not love the world or the things in the world... the world is passing away along with its desires, but whoever does the will of God abides forever" (1 John 2:15–17). The other foundational laws name the related failures: FL.III Heart-Throne names what the heart enthrones in the present moment; FL.XIV Vanity-of-Substitutes names the emptiness of every substitute put in God's place; FL.XV Hardening names the refusal that hardens over time. The way back is to rebuild the forward-look through the paths above: steady time in the Scriptures about the end, worship that looks ahead to the marriage supper, the Lord's Supper as a foretaste of what's coming, and a community that keeps the coming completion before its eyes as the real goal the heart is being drawn toward.*

***Certainty: Clearly Taught.*** *Four or more New Testament writers back it (Paul across at least seven letters — Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Philippians, Colossians, 1 Thessalonians, and 2 Timothy; the writer of Hebrews across several chapters; John in 1 John and Revelation; Peter in 1 and 2 Peter), with deep Old Testament roots in Isaiah and the Psalms. Every mainstream Christian tradition accepts the core claim, even while they differ on specific end-times questions. The direction it runs is clear.*
