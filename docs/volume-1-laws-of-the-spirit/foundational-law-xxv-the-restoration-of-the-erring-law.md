# Foundational Law XXV: The Restoration-of-the-Erring Law

This twenty-fifth law names how the community responds when one of its members wanders off. When spiritually mature members go after the wandering or straying brother or sister — gently, and keeping an eye on their own weak spots — it brings the one who strayed back into the fellowship and rescues them from where the wandering was headed. This is a different law from FL.V Reciprocal Forgiveness (which is about the hurt party, between two people) and from FL.XVIII Bitter-Root Community Law (which is about how unresolved offenses harden across a whole community). Here the focus is the *wanderer*, with the mature member doing the going-after, and the shape of that going-after is what the law is about.

This law completes a set of three about the community's relational life, alongside FL.XVIII Bitter-Root Community Law and FL.XXIV Confession-in-Community Law. Together the three cover three different things a community has to deal with (an *offense* and how it spreads, an *inner weight* and how it gets released, and a *wanderer* and how she is pursued) and the three different responses each one calls for (the Matt. 18 steps, confessing in the presence of others, and going after the one who strayed).

## Scriptural Ground

The witness runs across at least four biblical authors, with Paul's statement the most precise. Paul names the law at Gal. 6:1: "Brothers, if anyone is caught in any transgression, you who are spiritual should restore him in a spirit of gentleness, keeping watch on yourself, lest you too be tempted." Paul's version is complete — it names who needs the going-after (the one caught in a sin), who does it (you who are spiritual), how (gently), what it produces (restoration), and the warning to the one doing it (keep watch on yourself, or you may be tempted too). That last warning is unusually specific for a New Testament command, which tells you that the rescuer's own openness to the same sin is part of how the law works.

James names how far this reaches at Jas. 5:19–20: "My brothers, if anyone among you wanders from the truth and someone brings him back, let him know that whoever brings back a sinner from his wandering will save his soul from death and will cover a multitude of sins." James names the full reach of the law — saving a soul from death, covering a multitude of sins — both of which go beyond just the one person restored, out to the wider circle the wandering had touched. The phrase "covering a multitude of sins" echoes Peter (1 Pet. 4:8's "love covers a multitude of sins"), brought here into the restoring context; going after the wanderer has good effects for the whole community, not only for the wanderer.

Matthew gives the parable at Matt. 18:12–14: "What do you think? If a man has a hundred sheep, and one of them has gone astray, does he not leave the ninety-nine on the mountains and go in search of the one that went astray? And if he finds it, truly, I say to you, he rejoices over it more than over the ninety-nine that never went astray. So it is not the will of my Father who is in heaven that one of these little ones should perish." Where Matthew puts this matters: the parable comes right before the Matt. 18:15–20 reconciliation steps that FL.XVIII Bitter-Root Community Law names. The two passages together — Matt. 18:12–14 (going after the wanderer) and Matt. 18:15–20 (the steps for handling an offense) — are Matthew's treatment of the community's relational life. The parable's heart — *it is not the will of my Father that one of these little ones should perish* — names the claim from God's side: going after the wanderer is what the Father wants, and failing to go is treated as against His will.

Paul gives a case of restoration actually completed at 2 Cor. 2:5–11. The Corinthian church had earlier disciplined a member (the situation behind 1 Cor. 5), and now Paul calls them to reaffirm their love for him: "For such a one, this punishment by the majority is enough, so you should rather turn to forgive and comfort him, or he may be overwhelmed by excessive sorrow. So I beg you to reaffirm your love for him." This names how the restoration gets finished — the community's renewed love is the form the completed restoration takes, and failing to renew it risks crushing the man under too much sorrow, which would itself finish off the downward path the wandering started. This passage names what Galatians doesn't: the community's ongoing work after the first pursuit and return, with the renewed love as the finishing touch.

Luke gives the fullest set of parables at Luke 15:1–32 — the three stories of the lost: the lost sheep (Luke 15:1–7, Luke's version of the Matt. 18:12–14 parable), the lost coin (Luke 15:8–10), and the prodigal son (Luke 15:11–32). All three hold the pattern this law names, and it comes through most powerfully in the prodigal's father — running toward his son while the boy is still a long way off, throwing his arms around him before the rehearsed confession can even be finished, calling for a feast that shows the joy of the restoration. Luke names the *attitude* this law requires: you go after the wanderer out of joy at getting them back, not out of judgment on their wandering. That attitude is essential — the very same going-after, done out of judgment instead, produces what Scripture treats as the law's dark face (the older brother's anger at the feast in Luke 15:25–32).

The Old Testament case is Nathan and David (2 Sam. 12:1–14): Nathan going after David following the affair with Bathsheba and the killing of Uriah, using a parable to make his point, with "you are the man" as the moment of confrontation. The story holds all the pieces — the active going-after, the spiritually mature pursuer (Nathan the prophet), the form it takes (the parable, an indirect approach that lets David's own conscience do the work), and the restoration completed (David's confession in v. 13 and the prophet's word confirming forgiveness). This Old Testament case is a supporting witness; the New Testament puts it more directly.

## Mechanism

The law works through the relationship the active going-after creates. By wandering, the person has slipped out from under the community's accountability and into a spot where the wandering can keep going uncorrected. That spot is held in place by the community staying neutral or looking the other way; the wandering is quietly allowed to continue because no one is going after the wanderer to bring them back. The law breaks that quiet permission by making active pursuit the community's response to someone wandering.

Scripture names the shape of the pursuit precisely, and the precision matters. It is *active* — not passive, not waiting for the wanderer to come back, not hoping from a distance. By the very nature of wandering, the person has lost the inner compass that would turn them around; the wandering itself is what that lost compass looks like, so the rescue has to start from the outside rather than wait for the wanderer to find their own way back. It is done *by spiritually mature members* — Paul's "you who are spiritual" is exact, because that maturity is what lets the pursuit work the way the law intends instead of doing the extra harm that immature pursuit usually does. It is done *gently* — the manner isn't optional; Paul names the gentleness as the form the pursuit must take. And it is done *with an eye on the rescuer's own weak spots* — the watchfulness is the law's way of recognizing that the rescuer is in the same boat as the wanderer.

What the pursuit produces is restoration — the wanderer back in the fellowship, the downward path stopped, the relationship mended. It doesn't always succeed; the Matt. 18:15–20 escalating steps that FL.XVIII Bitter-Root Community Law names deal with the unrepentant wanderer who refuses to be pursued, and this law doesn't promise restoration in every case. The promise — saving the soul from death and covering a multitude of sins — holds where the pursuit is received; the active pursuit is the bright-side form of the law, with the wanderer's accepting or refusing it deciding whether the restoration actually happens.

The attitude behind the pursuit deserves its own word. Paul's *gentleness* and Luke's *joy* (the father running to the prodigal, calling for the feast) together name the attitude the law requires. Pursuit driven by *judgment* instead — a mature member who goes after the wanderer to expose her, to prove himself right, to enforce the community's rules as his own — works in a completely different way and produces a different result. Luke's older brother, angry at the prodigal's feast, is the classic case of the judging attitude that Scripture treats as the law's dark face: he is in the rescuer's position (the one who stayed, the one who was faithful, the one the household is basically entrusted to), but judgment has crowded out joy at the homecoming, and the result is that he shuts himself out of the celebration. The attitude is essential: the very same going-after is the law working when it is done in gentleness and joy, and something else entirely (the judging thing Luke exposes) when it is done in judgment.

The watchfulness is the second feature that deserves its own word. Paul's "keeping watch on yourself, lest you too be tempted" is exact — the rescuer is in the same boat as the wanderer, and the very temptation that pulled the wanderer away is right there for the rescuer too, through the act of pursuing. How the rescuer is at risk depends on what the wanderer wandered into: someone pursuing a person broken by sexual sin is at risk of the same, through the closeness the pursuit requires; someone pursuing a person drifting from the faith is at risk of the same doubts, through the deep engagement it takes; someone pursuing a person stuck in bitterness is at risk of the same bitterness, through the offenses the pursuit will dredge up. This watchfulness isn't paranoia about one's own rottenness; it is clear-eyed realism about the conditions of the pursuit. The mature rescuer keeps that watch as part of how the pursuit is done, and it guards them from catching what the wanderer has.

## Why This Is a Foundational Law

The law meets all four tests for inclusion. First, the backing from many writers is strong: at least four biblical authors hold it (Paul, James, Matthew, Luke), with the 2 Corinthians account of a restoration completed as an extra witness from Paul and the Nathan-and-David case as the Old Testament one.

Second, every mainstream Christian tradition accepts it. Going after the wandering is part of every tradition's pastoral practice; they differ in the form it takes (formal church discipline among Catholics and the Reformed; spiritual direction in the contemplative tradition; small-group accountability among Wesleyans; evangelistic-pastoral pursuit in the evangelical traditions; lay pursuit in the Anabaptist and free churches), not in the underlying principle. The core claim — that mature members going gently after the wandering brings the straying one back — is held in common.

Third, the law clearly runs in one direction. Active pursuit done under the law's conditions produces restoration (where it is received), plus the saving-of-the-soul and covering-of-a-multitude-of-sins that James names. Abandonment lets the wandering keep going, with all the harm that follows for the wanderer and for the wider community.

Fourth, the law works as a principle. The principle (mature members pursuing the wandering, gently and watchfully, produces restoration) stands apart from any one form it takes (formal discipline, spiritual direction, small-group accountability, evangelistic pursuit). The catalog accepts the principle; the specific forms are explained and practiced differently across traditions.

This law lives in the community's life of action, because the pursuit is something you physically do: the mature member goes to where the wanderer is, speaks to her face to face, and stays present over time. The actually going-to-them is part of how the law works.

**Proposed Law: The Restoration-of-the-Erring Law. When spiritually mature members of the community actively go after the wandering or straying brother or sister — gently, and with an eye on their own weak spots — they bring the straying one back into the fellowship and rescue them from where the wandering was headed. The attitude (gentleness, and joy at the homecoming) is essential to how the law works, and the watchfulness over the rescuer's own weak spots is the law's way of recognizing that the rescuer is in the same boat as the wanderer.**

***Mirror (stated form):*** *The dark, idol-ward face of the Restoration-of-the-Erring Law is abandoning the wanderer — the community staying neutral, or pulling back in judgment, and letting the wandering run on with no one stepping in, so that the wanderer keeps sliding and the whole community shares in the abandoning. The classic case is the older brother in Luke 15:25–32: he is in the rescuer's position (the one who stayed, the one the household is basically entrusted to), but judgment has crowded out joy at the homecoming, and the result is that he shuts himself out of the celebration. Matt. 18:12–14's parable, told to the disciples about their attitude toward the straying, names the dark face directly — failing to go after them, treated as against the Father's will. The very same going-after is the law working when it is done in gentleness and joy, and something else entirely (the judging thing Luke exposes) when it is done in judgment; the attitude is essential.*

***Certainty: Clearly Taught.*** *Four main biblical authors back it (Paul, Gal. 6:1; James, Jas. 5:19–20; Matthew, Matt. 18:12–14; Luke, Luke 15:1–32), with the 2 Corinthians account of a restoration completed as an extra witness from Paul and the Nathan-and-David case (2 Sam. 12:1–14) as the Old Testament one. Every mainstream Christian tradition accepts the core claim (they differ on the specific form, but hold the underlying principle in common). The direction it runs is clear, and it works as a principle.*

**A word from your Granddaddy.** I hold this law in a tension I've never fully resolved: I do not know how to help someone who doesn't want to be helped. The picture that settles me is the rich young man who came to Jesus asking what he must do — and Mark says Jesus looked at him and loved him, and then let him walk away (Mark 10:21–22). If the Lord Himself let a loved man go, then so, sometimes, must I. I've reached out to people in love and humility, watching my own heart the way this law says to, and gotten nothing back — and I've had to let the reaching-out itself be enough: enough that they knew they were cared for, enough that the door stayed open. But it is a door, and only they can walk through it. This law engages the other person's will. They get to choose. And I've had to let them — whatever my opinion of what they should do — and leave the rest with God.
