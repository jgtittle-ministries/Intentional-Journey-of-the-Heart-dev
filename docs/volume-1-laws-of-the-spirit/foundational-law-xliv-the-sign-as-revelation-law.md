# Foundational Law XLIV: The Sign-as-Revelation Law

This law is about how Christ's miracles reveal spiritual truths. A miracle isn't just a wonder to prove a point, or a handy illustration tacked on afterward. The miracle actually *is* the spiritual reality, made visible. When Christ heals blindness, He doesn't merely illustrate spiritual sight — He really gives it, out in the open, where everyone can see. The physical miracle and the spiritual truth are one thing. And this works the same at every size, from one person to the whole world.

This truth runs all through John's Gospel especially — which calls Jesus' miracles "signs" — and it shows up across the rest of the New Testament too, but it had never been set down as a foundational law in its own right. This law sets it down.

It goes hand in hand with the Opening Miracle Frame at the front of the volume. That frame makes the bigger point: the spiritual world runs right through the natural world, so a miracle isn't nature being broken into from outside — it's the spiritual breaking into view for a moment. This law takes the next step and names exactly how that works: Christ's miracles reveal the spiritual realities they carry, to anyone who receives them as signs.

It works the same way at every size. In one person (the man born blind, in John 9, who comes step by step to say, "Lord, I believe"). In a small group (the first sign at the Cana wedding showing Christ's glory to His disciples, John 2:11). In a whole church (a congregation hearing the Gospel's accounts of the signs and being brought to faith). Across the generations (the same revealing at work in the sacraments and means of grace down the centuries). And at the end of all things (the full unveiling of everything the signs have only hinted at in this age).

## Scriptural Ground

John's Gospel is built around seven of these miracles, which he deliberately calls "signs" (the Greek word is *sēmeia*). They are: turning water into wine at Cana (John 2:1–11); healing the official's son at Capernaum (John 4:46–54); healing the paralyzed man at Bethesda (John 5:1–17); feeding the five thousand (John 6:1–15); walking on the sea (John 6:16–21); giving sight to the man born blind (John 9:1–41); and raising Lazarus (John 11:1–44). At the very first sign, John 2:11 lays out the pattern: "This, the first of His signs, Jesus did at Cana in Galilee, and manifested His glory. And His disciples believed in Him." Three things to notice: it's the *first* (the signs come as an ordered series); it *manifested His glory* (the sign reveals something about who He is); and the disciples *believed* (the sign is meant to be received).

John 6:26 shows the other side — the crowd that missed the point: "Truly, truly, I say to you, you are seeking Me, not because you saw signs, but because you ate of the loaves." They took the surface of the sign (free bread) but missed what it revealed — that Jesus is the bread of life. In the long teaching that follows (John 6:22–59), Jesus presses that deeper meaning, and many can't take it: "after this many of His disciples turned back and no longer walked with Him" (John 6:66). A crowd chasing the spectacle won't stay once the real meaning is pressed home.

John 12:37 shows where rejecting the signs leads — the same hardening Isaiah foresaw: "Though He had done so many signs before them, they still did not believe in Him, so that the word spoken by the prophet Isaiah might be fulfilled: 'Lord, who has believed what he heard from us, and to whom has the arm of the Lord been revealed?'" Refusing the signs over and over hardens a heart, just as refusing God in general does (the pattern FL.XV Hardening describes).

John 20:30–31 states why he wrote down these signs at all: "Now Jesus did many other signs in the presence of the disciples, which are not written in this book; but these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in His name." Even the written record of the signs carries their revealing power to readers who never saw them in person — to us.

Matthew, Mark, and Luke show the same thing, especially when people demand a sign on the spot. At Matt. 12:38–42 (also Luke 11:29–32), the scribes and Pharisees ask for a sign, and Jesus refuses, pointing only to "the sign of Jonah" still to come. He won't hand out miracles to people who want them as mere proof. At Matt. 16:1–4 (and Mark 8:11–13) the same thing happens again. As Jesus says at Mark 8:12, "Why does this generation seek a sign? Truly, I say to you, no sign will be given to this generation." A heart that comes looking for spectacle doesn't receive the signs at all.

Hebrews shows the signs continuing through the apostles, at Heb. 2:3–4: "It was declared at first by the Lord, and it was attested to us by those who heard, while God also bore witness by signs and wonders and various miracles and by gifts of the Holy Spirit distributed according to His will." After Jesus, God kept confirming the message through signs and wonders done by the apostles.

Luke shows the same in Acts. In his Pentecost sermon, Peter calls Jesus "a man attested to you by God with mighty works and wonders and signs that God did through Him in your midst, as you yourselves know" (Acts 2:22). The early church prayed for signs and wonders to keep coming (Acts 4:30). And at Iconium, Paul and Barnabas "remained for a long time, speaking boldly for the Lord, who bore witness to the word of His grace, granting signs and wonders to be done by their hands" (Acts 14:3).

Paul names them too — the power "of signs and wonders, by the power of the Spirit of God" in his ministry (Rom. 15:18–19); "the signs of a true apostle were performed among you with utmost patience, with signs and wonders and mighty works" (2 Cor. 12:12); and signs serving as a revealing word for unbelievers (1 Cor. 14:22).

The Old Testament laid the groundwork. God promises, "the Lord Himself will give you a sign" (Isa. 7:14). Isaiah says even he and his children are "signs and portents in Israel" (Isa. 8:18). And the signs God worked through Moses in Egypt (Ex. 4:1–9; Ex. 7–11) revealed who the LORD is and pressed His demand to let His people go. Signs that reveal God go all the way back.

Six or more biblical writers teach it, across both Testaments — John (the seven signs and the teachings around them); Matthew, Mark, and Luke (the refusals of signs-on-demand); the writer of Hebrews; Luke again in Acts; Paul across Romans, 2 Corinthians, and 1 Corinthians; and Isaiah and Moses in the Old Testament.

## Mechanism

Here is how it works. A sign doesn't just point at a far-off reality from a distance — it actually shares in that reality. The water turned to wine at Cana shares in the great feast at the end of all things, for wine is the drink of that feast (Isa. 25:6's "wine on the lees, well refined"; Matt. 8:11's "many will come from east and west and recline at table"; Rev. 19:9's "marriage supper of the Lamb"). The multiplied loaves share in Jesus as the bread of life (John 6:35, "I am the bread of life"). Giving sight to the blind shares in the spiritual sight Christ brings (John 9:39, "for judgment I came into this world, that those who do not see may see"). Raising the dead shares in the resurrection Christ Himself is (John 11:25, "I am the resurrection and the life").

This sharing is what makes a sign different from a mere illustration or a piece of proof. The sign doesn't stand for some separate thing the way a picture stands for what it shows; the sign *is* that thing, present in the flesh. The Cana wine is real wine — and it really is the feast-wine of the kingdom. The multiplied bread is real bread — and it really is the bread of life. The blind man's new eyesight is real eyesight — and it really is the spiritual sight Christ gives. This is the same kind of relationship the church sees in the sacraments, where the bread and water of the Lord's Supper and baptism truly carry the realities they signify.

What you make of a sign depends on how you receive it. Someone who receives it *as a sign* sees through the miracle to what it reveals, and believes (John 2:11; the crowd's first response in John 6:14; the blind man coming step by step to "Lord, I believe"). Someone who receives it *as a spectacle* sees only the wonder and misses the meaning — and either demands yet another sign (Matt. 12:38–39) or explains it away (the charge that Jesus casts out demons by the devil, Mark 3:22; or the complaint that He broke the Sabbath, John 9:16). For the first kind of person, the sign deepens faith; for the second, it hardens the heart (John 12:37–43 ties sign-rejection to the very hardening Isaiah described).

Who does the work here? Christ does — He works the sign. Our part is only how we receive it: as a sign (and our faith deepens) or as a spectacle (and our heart hardens). We don't make the sign; Christ does. How we take it shapes what it does in us, but it doesn't create the sign. So, like FL.XLI Defilement-Cleansing, this is Christ's own work, with the person as the one who receives — here, the one who beholds the sign and either sees through it or doesn't.

## Why This Is a Foundational Law

This law meets all four tests for inclusion.

First, the scriptural backing is exceptional. At least six biblical writers teach it, and John's whole Gospel is built around it (the seven signs are the framework of the entire book). Far more than the catalog's minimum of two.

Second, every mainstream Christian tradition accepts the core claim — that Christ's miracles reveal who He is, rather than merely prove a point. They differ on whether such signs still happen today (some say they were mainly for the apostles' era; others say they continue) and on the finer details of how a sign carries its reality. But the basic claim — that a sign reveals its truth by truly sharing in it — is held across the board: Catholics (their teaching that a sacrament really contains the grace it signifies), the Eastern Orthodox (the icon truly sharing in the holy reality it depicts), the Reformed (the sacraments as visible signs of invisible grace, joined to the Word), Lutherans (the finite bread and wine truly bearing the infinite Christ), Anglicans, and Pentecostals (their theology of signs and wonders).

Third, the law clearly runs in one direction: a sign received with an open heart → reveals its truth → and deepens faith; a sign met with a closed heart → hardens it.

Fourth, it works as a principle — and Scripture even spells out the form. The principle is that a sign reveals its truth by sharing in it, and John lays out exactly how this works: his ordered series of seven signs, and his plain statement of why he wrote them down (John 20:30–31). Scripture gives us both the principle and the pattern.

This law runs from Christ toward the one who sees: Christ works the sign and reveals Himself through it, and the person responds back to Him — with faith, or with hardening. And it isn't merely a string of separate miracles; it's a standing way God works — the spiritual always shining through the physical — even though we glimpse it in single, dramatic signs.

**Proposed Law: The Sign-as-Revelation Law. Christ's miracles reveal spiritual realities by actually sharing in them — a sign is not a separate illustration or a piece of proof tacked on, but the spiritual truth itself made visible in the physical world. That revealing reaches the person who receives the miracle as a sign rather than as a mere spectacle; and the very same sign works opposite effects in the two — deepening faith in the one who receives it rightly, hardening the heart of the one who only gawks. This works the same way at every size, from one person to the whole world, and it comes to its fullness at the end, when everything the signs have only hinted at is unveiled in full.**

***Mirror (stated form):*** *The dark, idol-ward face of this law is taking a sign as mere spectacle — seeing the wonder but missing what it reveals. This ends one of two ways: demanding yet another sign (Matt. 12:38–39; Mark 8:11–13) or explaining the sign away (the charge that Jesus drove out demons by the devil, Mark 3:22; the complaint that He healed the blind man on the Sabbath, John 9:16). Jesus' refusals to perform miracles on demand show that He won't hand out signs to people who only want a show; and John 6 shows what happens when a sign is given but received only as spectacle — the crowd takes the free bread and misses the bread of life. The modern version appears wherever people hunt for miraculous proof instead of receiving a sign in faith. It takes several forms. There's the "give me proof" demand — the person who says he'd believe if only a convincing enough sign were given, but whose doubt outlasts every sign because his heart isn't really open. There's the thrill-seeker — chasing the spectacle of the miracle without caring what it reveals. There's the explain-it-away habit — reaching for any other explanation (psychological, social, natural) to avoid taking the sign as a sign. And there's magical thinking — treating signs as levers to control God rather than as Christ revealing Himself and His kingdom. The other laws name nearby pieces: FL.XV Hardening names the hardening that refusing the signs finally brings; FL.XIII Pure-Heart Vision names the clean heart that's able to see a sign for what it is; and FL.XXXV Trust-Substrate names the trusting heart in which sign-receiving faith can grow. The way back is to let the heart be reshaped through the catalog's heart-forming paths (FL.III Heart-Throne; FL.VIII Desire-for-God; FL.XIII Pure-Heart Vision; FL.XXXV Trust-Substrate; FL.XL Abiding-Fruitfulness).*

***Certainty: Clearly Taught.*** *Six or more biblical writers across both Testaments back it, and John's whole Gospel is built around it. Every mainstream Christian tradition accepts the core claim. The direction it runs is clear, and Scripture itself lays out both the principle and the pattern (John's ordered series of signs and his stated purpose for recording them). Like FL.XLI Defilement-Cleansing, this is Christ's own work, with our part being to receive it rightly.*
