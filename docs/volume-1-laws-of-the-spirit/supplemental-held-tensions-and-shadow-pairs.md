# Supplemental Exploration: Reading the Laws as Held Tensions — A Competing-Values Lens

## Why I Am Adding This Here

A reader who has just learned what qualifies as a Foundational Law in this volume is about to meet the catalog of command-shaped sentences. *Humble yourself. Draw near. Ask. Hear and obey. Sow and reap.* A reader used to one-direction instructions will naturally take each as a one-way command: do the named thing, and the named result follows. That reading is not wrong. But it is incomplete, and that gap costs you more in real, lived formation than it does in just reading the text.

I want to bring in a way of seeing here — one I picked up in organizational consulting work rather than in spiritual formation, but one that turns out to fit how many of these laws actually work. It is Kim Cameron and Robert Quinn's Competing Values Framework (Cameron & Quinn, 2006), built for sizing up the culture of an organization. Cameron and Quinn's core insight is not "here are four values" but something deeper: **every value worth having has an opposite pulling against it, and what goes wrong in an organization or a person is letting the tension collapse all the way to one side.** Health is not winning one value at the expense of its opposite. Health is holding the pair in tension on purpose, letting each side give its real good without letting it crush the other.

I have used this framework with leadership teams and organizations for many years. What I did not see, until recently, is how directly the same basic point applies to the spiritual life and to the laws this volume is cataloging.

## The Move

Many of the Foundational Laws read as one-way commands. They are written that way for good reason — the command form is how scripture itself states them, and the one-way cause-and-effect is exactly what makes them foundational laws in the first place. *Draw near to God, and He will draw near to you.* That is one direction. The verb is *draw*. The actor is the person.

But the law does not work that way in actual lived experience. The person who chases God with frantic intensity, never quieting enough to let God close the rest of the distance, is not shaped by the law — they are bent out of shape by overdoing their own pursuit. They are working harder at drawing near than the law actually asks. The opposite collapse is just as common: the person who has heard the promise that God will draw near and has settled into a passive waiting that never lifts a hand to draw near themselves. The law says both halves — *draw near, and He will draw near* — and the person who pulls one half loose from the other has not obeyed the law; they have swapped half of it for the whole.

This is what I mean by **held tension**. In practice the law is a tension between two paired values, each with its own typical way of going wrong. Those failures are themselves a diagnosis: when you see the pursuit-as-anxiety pattern, you know which side has collapsed; when you see the passive-waiting pattern, you know it is the other. Either way, the failure tells you what is missing, and what is missing is always the other half it was meant to be held with.

## Which Laws This Lens Applies To

Not every Foundational Law has this tension shape. Some are simply descriptions (*what is sown is what is reaped* — there is no opposite to hold it against). Some just state how a thing works at different sizes, with no competing pair. The lens fits most clearly the **command-form, paired-value laws** — the ones written with a human action and a divine response in the same sentence.

The clearest examples among them, and the ones I have worked through first, are:

- **[FL.IV — Humility-Exaltation](foundational-law-iv-the-humility-exaltation-law.md):** the tension between humble action and trusting that exaltation comes from God
- **[FL.VI — Hear-and-Obey](foundational-law-vi-the-hear-and-obey.md):** the tension between receptive hearing and active doing
- **[FL.VII — Drawing-Near Reciprocity](foundational-law-vii-the-drawing-near-reciprocity.md):** the tension between human pursuit and trust in divine response
- **[FL.X — Ask-Seek-Knock](foundational-law-x-the-ask-seek-knock.md):** the tension between persistent asking and the alignment that prevents asking-as-demand

Each of these four chapters now carries a **Shadow Pair** sub-section naming both ways it goes wrong. The shadow is the tell. When you see the over-acted shadow in a person's life (your own included), you know which side has collapsed. When you see the over-awaited shadow, you know it is the other. What the law is actually after is holding both at once.

## What I Am Not Doing With This Lens

I am not suggesting that the named laws are wrong, or that the command form of scripture is misleading. The one-way language in the texts comes first, theologically. *Humble yourself.* That is what the text says, and what the text says comes first. The shadow-pair reading does not replace the one-way reading; it sits alongside it, naming the common ways people stumble in real life — common enough to be worth naming as part of how the law works.

I am also not suggesting that this lens is original to me, or that Cameron and Quinn's framework was the source of any spiritual insight. The insight is in the texts. The lens is a way of articulating something the texts already contain, which I had not seen as clearly until I had spent a long time using a related framework in another domain. That is how some kinds of seeing work — you carry a tool around long enough that you start to see its applications everywhere, including in territory you did not originally develop it for.

## A Note from My Own Formation

I found this framework very useful in diagnosing and advising CEOs and other leaders in my consulting practice. The tools that Cameron and Quinn offered were very helpful in recognizing both where an organization and its leadership were at the current time, and where they wanted to be. The difference between those two could then be used to define a path and identify immediate next steps. Even the diagnostic results by themselves brought clarity to the individuals involved. When I did this with an organization's leadership team, the diagnostic process often revealed things that would start conversations they had never had before, but realized they needed to have. These tools can be adapted to recognize what is really going on and make visible things that can be acted on.

That same adaptive use is what I am proposing here. The competing-values structure that helps a leadership team see the tensions their organization is actually holding is, I think, the same structure that helps a person see the tensions their formation is actually holding — and in both cases, the diagnostic value is in the making-visible. What is named can be addressed. What stays inside an undifferentiated "I should be growing spiritually" stays unaddressable.

The same basic insight ran through a related project of mine: I have spent many years developing what I call the **Balanced Scorecard Process (BSCP)** — a method for surfacing, measuring, and balancing the competing values an organization is actually operating under, originally built to address the gap between contractually-met service-level agreements and visibly-unsatisfied customers in outsourcing relationships. The method's core move is, in its own language, what this volume's held-tensions reading is in spiritual-formation language: *the balancing is the practice*. As I wrote in an early BSCP draft, "as these metrics are built, the tension has to be maintained to keep the balance in the overall result… is productivity balanced against quality and schedule and budget commitments and measurements?" The math requires the categories to be *complete and independent* (orthogonal) — so that moving one does not automatically move another — which is just the working form of the competing-values claim that there is no single value an organization can over-invest in without paying for it on the other measures.

I did not arrive at the held-tensions reading of the Foundational Laws by reasoning my way down from Cameron and Quinn. I arrived at it because I had been doing this kind of work — in BSCP form, in client engagement after client engagement — for decades, and the spiritual-formation parallels eventually came into view because of years of hands-on practice, not in spite of it. The forms differ. The measures in BSCP are numbers; the read-outs in the Foundational Laws are things you observe. The "vital signs report" that sums up how a client is balancing has, in spiritual formation, no number-based equivalent yet. But the basic claim underneath — that health is a held tension between competing values, and that the failure tells you which side has collapsed — is the same claim in both settings.

For the reader interested in the operational depth, the BSCP work is being published as a separate volume at [**The Balanced Scorecard Process**](https://jgtittle-ministries.github.io/balanced-scorecard-process/). The two projects sit alongside each other in my own thinking: BSCP names how to do this work for organizations, this supplemental names how to read it in the spiritual life. Each tightens the other.

The four laws above are the pilot. If this lens proves useful in lived reading, it likely applies to others. The first such application has already landed: the Affective Taxonomy itself — presented in Vol 2 as a five-stage progression — is now read across the project as an *increasingly integrated holding of competing values rather than a ladder where each later stage replaces the one before*.
