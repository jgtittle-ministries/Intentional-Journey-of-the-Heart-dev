# Foundational Law XLI: The Defilement-Cleansing Reversal Law

This law is about how Christ reverses the flow of uncleanness. Under the Old Testament Law, uncleanness was catching: when a clean person touched an unclean one, the cleanness didn't spread to the unclean — the uncleanness spread to the clean. With Christ, it runs the other way. When Christ touches what is unclean, He doesn't catch its uncleanness; His cleanness flows out and makes the unclean clean. That reversal is the deep truth underneath the whole New Covenant way of making people clean and holy — baptism, the Lord's Supper, the church's care for the broken, and the believer's growth in holiness all rest on it. And it works the same at every size, from one person to a whole people.

Most of the laws in this catalog describe something we do — sow and reap, rest, obey, draw near. This one is different: it describes something Christ does. Wherever His presence touches what is unclean, the uncleanness is reversed into cleanness. The Gospels show it happening again and again, and Paul, Peter, and John all build on it, but it had never been set down as a foundational law in its own right. This law sets it down.

It works the same way at every size. In one person (Christ touching the leper, Matt. 8:3; the woman touching the hem of His robe, Mark 5:25–34). In small groups (a believer receiving the means of grace and being cleansed, not defiled). In a whole church (the church as Christ's cleansed body — Eph. 5:25–27). Across the generations (the cleansing handed down through the sacraments and the means of grace). And in the whole people of God (the pure bride presented at the marriage supper of the Lamb, Rev. 19:7–9).

## Scriptural Ground

The clearest pictures are the healings of lepers (Matt. 8:1–4; Mark 1:40–45; Luke 5:12–16). Under the Law (Lev. 13–14), a leper was unclean, and anyone who touched him caught the uncleanness; the leper had to live cut off from the community until a long cleansing ceremony was complete. Jesus turns this on its head: He reaches out, touches the leper, and the leper is made clean — and Jesus is not made unclean. The short exchange — "Lord, if You will, You can make me clean" / "I will; be clean" — shows both sides: the leper trusts that Jesus has the authority to cleanse him, and Jesus willingly does it. The cleanness flows from Him.

The double miracle of the bleeding woman and Jairus's daughter (Mark 5:21–43; Luke 8:40–56) shows the same reversal twice over, with two different kinds of uncleanness. The woman has bled for twelve years and is permanently unclean under the Law (Lev. 15); by the normal rule, her touch of Jesus' robe would pass her uncleanness to Him — yet instead she is healed, and Jesus knows it has happened ("Who touched My garments?"). Then Jairus's daughter has died, and touching a dead body makes a person unclean (Num. 19:11–22) — yet Jesus takes the dead girl by the hand and raises her, without being defiled. In both, His cleanness and life flow out; their uncleanness and death do not flow in.

Luke's account of the widow of Nain (Luke 7:11–17) shows it again — Jesus touches the open coffin carrying the widow's dead son to his burial. By the normal rule that would make Him unclean; instead, the young man is raised to life.

John gives a picture of the ongoing cleansing in the foot-washing (John 13:1–11). When Peter protests, "You shall never wash my feet," Jesus answers, "If I do not wash you, you have no share with Me." Being cleansed by Christ isn't an optional extra — it's the very thing that lets a person share in the new life He brings.

Paul names the deepest version of this reversal at 2 Cor. 5:21: "For our sake He made Him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in Him we might become the righteousness of God." There it is in one sentence: Christ takes our sin, and we receive His righteousness — the great exchange that drives everything else. At Eph. 5:25–27, the same cleansing reaches the whole church: "Christ loved the church and gave Himself up for her, that He might sanctify her, having cleansed her by the washing of water with the word, so that He might present the church to Himself in splendor, without spot or wrinkle or any such thing, that she might be holy and without blemish." At Titus 3:5: "He saved us, not because of works done by us in righteousness, but according to His own mercy, by the washing of regeneration and renewal of the Holy Spirit." And at 1 Cor. 6:9–11: "you were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and by the Spirit of our God."

Hebrews explains it in terms of the priesthood. It calls Christ "holy, innocent, unstained, separated from sinners, exalted above the heavens" (Heb. 7:26), and says, "how much more will the blood of Christ, who through the eternal Spirit offered Himself without blemish to God, purify our conscience from dead works to serve the living God" (Heb. 9:13–14). Christ is both the priest and the sacrifice — and His cleansing goes deeper than the old sacrifices ever could, reaching the conscience itself, not just the outside of a person.

John names the cleansing as something ongoing, at 1 John 1:7: "If we walk in the light, as He is in the light, we have fellowship with one another, and the blood of Jesus His Son cleanses us from all sin."

And Isaiah foresaw it centuries before, at Isa. 53:4–5: "Surely He has borne our griefs and carried our sorrows; yet we esteemed Him stricken, smitten by God, and afflicted. But He was pierced for our transgressions; He was crushed for our iniquities; upon Him was the chastisement that brought us peace, and with His wounds we are healed." The same exchange, promised long in advance — He takes what is ours, and we receive what is His.

Eight or more biblical writers teach it, across both Testaments — Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John (the healings and the foot-washing); Paul (2 Corinthians, Ephesians, Titus, 1 Corinthians); the writer of Hebrews (Heb. 7 and 9); John again in 1 John; and Isaiah foreseeing it in Isaiah 53. It runs through the Gospel stories, the apostles' letters, the priestly teaching of Hebrews, and the prophets.

## Mechanism

Here is how it works. Under the Old Testament Law, uncleanness flowed one way — from the unclean person to whoever touched him. When Christ is present, the same touch flows the other way: His cleanness flows out to the unclean and makes them clean.

This reversal depends entirely on Christ. It happens where He is present — in person, in the Gospel stories, and now, after His resurrection, through the means of grace. It is not something we make happen on our own. Apart from Christ, the old rule still holds: touch what is unclean and you catch its uncleanness — a person who plays with sin apart from Christ is defiled by it. Everything turns on whether you're acting from within Christ or on your own.

This reversal is the ground the whole New Covenant cleansing rests on. Baptism (Acts 22:16; Titus 3:5) works from it — being baptized is a person's entry into this reversed flow where Christ's cleansing reaches them, a cleansing received by faith. Whatever else is true of how the sign and the grace are joined — and the traditions weigh that differently — the cleansing is Christ's gift, taken hold of by faith, not a property of the water itself; the sign points to and seals the thing it signifies, and never works apart from Christ Himself received. The Lord's Supper (1 Cor. 10:16–17; 11:23–32) works from it — the bread and the cup are how we keep receiving that cleansing. And the church's care for the broken (anointing the sick with oil, Jas. 5:14–16; the "royal priesthood" of believers, 1 Pet. 2:9; clearing out the old "leaven," 1 Cor. 5:6–8) works from it too — when believers help cleanse one another, the cleansing power isn't their own; it's Christ's, flowing through them.

It's worth being clear about who does the work here. This is Christ's doing, start to finish; our part is simply to receive it — the leper coming to Jesus, the woman reaching for His hem, a believer receiving the means of grace, a believer helping cleanse another by Christ's power and not her own. That receiving matters, but it's receiving something Christ is doing, not helping Him do it. This is a little different from laws like FL.XXXIV Marriage Covenant or FL.XL Abiding-Fruitfulness, where Christ does the main work but we have a real part to play alongside Him. Here the cleansing is entirely His gift; we only open our hands to take it.

## Why This Is a Foundational Law

This law meets all four tests for inclusion.

First, the scriptural backing is exceptional. At least eight biblical writers teach it — Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John (the healings and the foot-washing); Paul (2 Corinthians, Ephesians, Titus, 1 Corinthians); the writer of Hebrews (Heb. 7 and 9); John again in 1 John; and Isaiah foreseeing it. Far more than the catalog's minimum of two.

Second, every mainstream Christian tradition accepts the core claim, even though few name it as a single law on its own. Catholics teach that Christ truly acts through the sacraments to cleanse (their phrase is *ex opere operato* — the grace works through the act itself, by Christ's power, not the minister's worthiness). The Eastern Orthodox teach that Christ, by becoming human, made human nature itself holy, and we are made like Him by sharing in His life. The Reformed teach union with Christ — both His righteousness counted as ours and the Spirit's ongoing work of making us holy, both flowing from being joined to Him. Lutherans teach that the infinite Christ truly comes to us in the finite bread and wine. Anglicans hold a full sacramental teaching. Pentecostals and charismatics live it out in healing and deliverance ministry. And the wider evangelical world holds it in the doctrines of being justified and made holy. What has been missing is simply naming it as one clear principle, rather than leaving it scattered across many separate truths about Christ.

Third, the law clearly runs in one direction: Christ's touch → the unclean is made clean; without Christ's touch → the old rule holds, and uncleanness still spreads the normal way.

Fourth, it works as a principle. The principle is simply that Christ's presence reverses the flow — His cleanness goes out to the unclean. And the way a person comes under that flow is by entering it through baptism, the ongoing Lord's Supper, and the other means of grace.

This law runs from Christ down to the unclean person, moving them from unclean to clean. And it isn't just a one-off event each time; it's a standing condition — an ever-present reversal that the whole New Covenant cleansing rests on, even though in the Gospels we see it in single, dramatic moments. Above all, it is Christ's own work.

**Proposed Law: The Defilement-Cleansing Reversal Law. Where Christ is present, the flow of uncleanness is reversed: when He touches the unclean, His cleanness flows out and makes them clean, rather than their uncleanness defiling Him. This reversal is the ground on which the whole New Covenant cleansing rests — baptism, the Lord's Supper, the church's care for the broken, and the believer's growth in holiness all draw on it. It depends entirely on Christ's presence, not on anything in us; our part is only to receive what He offers. It works the same way at every size, from one person to the whole people of God, and a person comes under it by entering through baptism, the Lord's Supper, and the other means of grace.**

***Mirror (Christ's work — the failure is refusing it):*** *This law works differently from most of the others. With them, the dark side is the same engine running the wrong way — the heart aimed at an idol instead of at God. But here the engine is Christ Himself, not us, so the failure isn't aiming His cleansing the wrong way; it's simply refusing to receive it. That refusal takes several forms. There's the try-to-clean-yourself form — scrubbing away at your own cleansing apart from Christ, the way the Pharisees did (Mark 7:1–13), which ends up all outward form and no real cleansing, the empty pattern FL.XL Abiding-Fruitfulness describes. There's the despair form — being unable to receive the offer because you don't believe Christ's cleansing is really for you (Heb. 10:38 calls this "shrinking back," and 1 John 1:8–9 gives the cure: confess, and He is faithful to cleanse). There's the wrong-mediator form — seeking cleansing through something other than Christ, whether the medieval buying of pardons, a modern therapy treated as salvation, or a religious system set up as a rival path. And there's the indifference form — never coming for cleansing at all, because you don't see your own uncleanness or don't know the cleansing is there for the taking. The way back, in every case, is to turn to Christ's offered cleansing and receive it — entering it through baptism, if you never have, or through the ongoing means of grace, if you've drifted from them. The other laws name nearby pieces of this: FL.II Confession-Restoration names receiving this cleansing through spoken confession; FL.XXIV Confession-in-Community names it in the gathered church; and FL.XXX Communal Soul-Care for the Wounded names the church holding a soul steady while the cleansing does its work.*

***Certainty: Clearly Taught.*** *Eight or more biblical writers across both Testaments back it (the four Gospel writers; Paul across 2 Corinthians, Ephesians, Titus, 1 Corinthians; the writer of Hebrews; John in 1 John; and Isaiah foreseeing it). Every mainstream Christian tradition accepts the core claim — Catholic, Orthodox, Reformed, Lutheran, Anglican, Pentecostal, and the wider evangelical world. The direction it runs is clear, and it holds the same at every size. This is one of the few laws whose work is wholly Christ's own, with our part being only to receive it.*

He came in, and His cleanness did what mine never could. I’ve watched Him do the same kind of thing for others in that exercise ever since — always amazing, and always personal.*
