# First Exploration: How to Get Faith


![](images/ear-listening.jpg)

## The Starting Point: Rom. 10:17
This is where I started the explorations, and it is still the clearest single law of how-things-work that I have found in scripture. Rom. 10:17 is a real if-then chain: it has a clear starting point, a clear result, and a clear way the one leads to the other.

***Rom. 10:17 (ESV)***

*"So faith comes from hearing, and hearing through the word of Christ."*

Let me lay it out as a law, with the arrows showing exactly what leads to what: the Word of Christ is what goes in. Hearing — real, attentive hearing that is willing to obey — is what turns it into faith. Faith (which I use throughout to mean trust, the Greek pistis) is what comes out. The chain runs one way: Word → Hearing → Faith. You cannot get to real trust by skipping the hearing, and you cannot get to the hearing by ignoring the Word.

## What "Hearing" Actually Means
The Greek in Romans 10 has two different words: akouo (just picking up a sound with your ears) and the kind of hearing that actually produces faith. Here is how I have come to understand the way the one becomes the other: a personal word to me about Christ, when I hear it ready to obey and ready to change, raises my level of trust (faith) and changes what I do. This is rhema (a living word spoken to me right now) rather than logos (the general, permanent, unchanging Word of God) — though the rhema always comes through the logos and lines up with it.

A word of caution about the rhema/logos difference I am using here. Grudem rightly points out in Systematic Theology that the New Testament does not actually use these two Greek words this neatly — both words show up for the written Word and for God speaking personally, with no clear pattern that would make them two separate categories. So I am not making a claim about the words themselves. I am describing a difference in how the Word reaches me and works on me: the general text of scripture versus that same scripture landing on me, personally, right now. It is not a second kind of word, and it does not carry its own separate authority. A line from scripture that hits you in your quiet time as if it were spoken straight to you is not a different kind of word; it is scripture arriving with your name on it. Grudem’s correction is a useful guardrail: the moment you start treating those personal impressions as if they outranked scripture or stood on their own, you have pushed the idea too far, and you are headed into dangerous territory.

The flip side matters just as much: if I hear but am not willing to obey, my heart can grow harder instead of softer. This is the parable of the Sower read as a loop that feeds itself — how I respond to the word I have heard sets the soil for the next word. Disobedience is not neutral; it clogs the channel.

## The Loop That Feeds Itself
What comes out of this exploration is not just a one-way chain but a loop that feeds itself. Faith, once it is there, makes me more willing to hear, which produces more faith. The other way around, a hard heart deadens hearing, which keeps faith from growing, which makes the heart harder still. The loop turns in both directions. Which way it turns depends mostly on whether I am willing to obey what I have already heard.

There is one more dimension of this worth naming, because it has changed how I pray with people. Dmitri Bilgere, drawing on years of men’s group facilitation, made an observation that I think is simply true: the moment of transformation is not when the tears come or the anger breaks. It is the moment when you allow yourself to risk believing that what the Spirit has been saying to you is actually real.

That sounds obvious until you sit with it. Think about what the Word → Hearing → Faith chain actually feels like from the inside. The Word arrives. Something in you responds — there is a flicker, a recognition. But then, at exactly the point where trust would be the next move, something in you holds back. You are still managing the risk. What Bilgere observed is that the actual transition point — the moment the chain completes and faith is genuinely generated — is the moment you drop that management and let the word land as true. Not intellectual assent. Not emotional performance. Just: I actually believe this, right now, for this moment.

The process itself — whether it is the “Any Doubts?” exercise, a TPM session, or quiet time with scripture — does not give you faith. It sets up the moment when you are finally willing to risk believing what was already true. The faith was there all along; you were holding back from it. The obedience part I described above is partly about this: being willing to obey is being willing to believe that what you heard was real enough to act on. That willingness is the doorway.

Here is the practical application: when the faith chain is stalled, the question is not “what more information do I need?” It is “what am I not yet willing to believe is true?” That is the door. The Spirit is standing at it.

And there is something to keep straight here so I do not take the credit: the very seeing that lets a word land as true is itself the Spirit's gift, not my achievement. Left to myself I am “not able to understand them because they are spiritually discerned” (1 Cor. 2:14); so the hearing this whole journey runs on is something I ask Him for, not something I manufacture (developed in [Vol 2, Exploration 7](../volume-2-knowing-to-doing/exploration-07-hearing-with-understanding.md)).

I connect this to the Sixth Exploration (the Obedience Channel): obedience to the last word I received is the condition for the channel to be open to receive the next word. The two explorations are closely linked.

## What faith does — the reception that releases (and its limits)

There is a second half to this law that took me years to see clearly. The chain *produces* faith; but once you have it, faith is not a passive feeling — it is the one real, chosen action that is genuinely *mine* in the whole exchange. The power is God’s; yet again and again in the Gospels that power is *released through* a person’s faith reaching for it. The centurion’s faith reaches across a boundary, and Jesus marvels and heals (Matt. 8:5–13). The Syrophoenician woman presses in, and Jesus answers, “Great is your faith!” (Mark 7:24–30). The woman with the hemorrhage only touches His robe — He had not turned to her — and the power goes out, and He tells her, “your faith has made you well” (Mark 5:25–34). The pattern is steady enough to lean on: where there is real faith, Christ does not ignore it. (Vol 1’s [FL.XLIII Cross-Boundary Faith-Access](foundational-law-xliii-the-cross-boundary-faith-access-law.md) names this from the access side, and [FL.XLI Defilement-Cleansing](foundational-law-xli-the-defilement-cleansing-reversal-law.md) names the far end where the work is wholly Christ’s and we only receive; Vol 3 makes the same point in its own language, treating trust as the `f()` factor that, set to zero, leaves the whole [spiritual-force](../volume-3-quantitative-framework/exploration-03-spiritual-force.md) equation at zero.)

But the same Gospels guard the point from a dangerous overreading. **Faith receives; it does not compel.** At Cana, Mary brings the need and Jesus answers, “My hour has not yet come” — and then acts anyway, on His own timing, not because He was forced (John 2:1–11). And God plainly moves where there was no prior faith at all: Lazarus was dead and could not believe; Saul was an enemy on the Damascus road; the Spirit fell on Cornelius before he had “done” anything. So the fuller truth is two-sided — genuine faith is never ignored, and God is never under our control. Faith is the necessary, chosen reception that opens the way; it is not a lever that obligates God, nor a meter that measures Him.

That two-sidedness matters most for the believer in a dry or unanswered season. If faith *released* the power and nothing visible came, it would be cruelly easy to conclude, “then I must not have had enough.” These stories teach no such thing. The delay is often the law’s normal shape (see [FL.VII Drawing-Near Reciprocity](foundational-law-vii-the-drawing-near-reciprocity.md) on the seasons when God seems to hide), and the outcome and its timing stay His. Do the believing; leave the rest with Him.

And it works at every size. When Israel reached the edge of the Promised Land, the spies’ fearful report spread, the people would not believe — and they did not enter (Num. 13–14). The real faith of Joshua, Caleb, and Moses did not outweigh a whole generation’s unbelief. Corporate faith opens a corporate door; corporate unbelief can shut one that stood wide open. The same law that runs in one trembling woman runs through a whole people.

## The Other Direction: Faith That Speaks

So far this is all about faith coming *in* — the Word heard, received, believed. But Paul, in this same chapter of Romans, names the other direction too: faith that has come in is meant to go back out, in words. Just before the verse this exploration is built on, he writes, "if you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved. For with the heart one believes and is justified, and with the mouth one confesses and is saved" (Rom. 10:9–10). Believing in the heart and confessing with the mouth are two halves of one thing. This is not the anxious "name it and claim it" that treats words as a lever on God; it is simply faith doing in the open what it has already done in secret — confessed, prayed aloud, sung, told to someone else. Much of the New Testament's faith is out-loud faith. So the guardrails above (keep the personal word under Scripture; never let an impression outrank the text) have a constructive partner: once the Word has landed and become trust, *speak* it. Saying it out loud is one of the ordinary ways faith grows — in the one who speaks and in the ones who hear.

And this confessing is not one-directional. Jesus attaches a staggering promise to it: “everyone who acknowledges me before men, I also will acknowledge before my Father who is in heaven” (Matt. 10:32; cf. Luke 12:8). The word we say for Him in front of others, He says for us in front of the Father — our small, sometimes shaky confession met by His own. The dark side is named too — “whoever denies me before men, I also will deny before my Father” (Matt. 10:33) — but this is not a one-strike verdict. Peter denied Him three times and was restored to a public, threefold confession (John 21:15–17); Paul, once a violent persecutor, was met with mercy (1 Tim. 1:13). The warning is against the settled refusal to own Him, not the frightened failure that grief carries back to His feet. What the promise asks is simply that the faith confessed in the heart not go silent when His name is in the room.

**Proposed Law (Operational): Faith (trust) is produced by hearing the Word of Christ with a genuine willingness to obey. How well you hear depends on whether you have obeyed before. Obedience keeps the channel open and strengthens the loop; disobedience clogs the channel and starts the heart hardening.**

**Certainty: Reasonably Inferred  ***Strong scriptural grounding. The exact way that willingness to obey turns the general Word into a personal one is confirmed in experience but not yet pinned to a single ESV verse. Community testing needed.*

***A word from your Granddaddy.*** *Your grandmother and I were teaching a Sunday-school class of sixth-grade girls, and they were all bent over a project around the table when one of them asked me — almost idly, not even looking up — “Mr. Tittle, do you believe the Bible is really true?” Something in that simple question stopped time. It felt as though I had all the room I needed to weigh it honestly, though it could only have been a second or two before I heard myself answer, “Yes. I do.” And here is what I want you to see: I wasn’t reaching for an argument or one more piece of evidence. In that quiet moment I simply let the question land — and discovered I already believed it, more surely than I had known. That is how faith so often comes: not by figuring out one more thing, but by being willing, right then, to believe what was already true. The faith was there all along; the Spirit was standing at the door, and a child’s honest question swung it open.*
