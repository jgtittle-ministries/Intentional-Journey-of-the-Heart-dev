# Foundational Law XXVII: The Thick Practice Transmission Law

![Bread and the cup on the Lord's Table — shared practices repeated across the years carry the faith in bodily form.](images/thick-practice-communion.jpg)

This twenty-seventh law names how shared practices pass faith down across the generations. Practices woven into the regular rhythm of life and repeated across generations — keeping the Sabbath, Passover and the seder, baptism and the ways it is remembered, the Lord's Supper and the Lord's table, steady praying together, steady reading of Scripture together, the cycles of catechism, the seasons of the church year — carry the heart of the faith across in a way that teaching with words alone never can. The practices hold that content in bodily form, and it is passed down across the generations as a person takes part in the practice, over and over, through the years when they are being formed.

What sets this law apart is how it works — the practice itself carries the content. The catalog already names four other ways faith passes across the generations: **FL.XXI Household Formation** by deliberate teaching (a parent puts the content into words); **FL.XVII Substitution-Cascade** by soaking-it-up (the child catches the parent's heart-orientation from the home's atmosphere); **FL.XIX Spirit Anointing Transmission** by handing-on and closeness (the anointing passed through laying-on of hands and nearness); and **FL.XXVI Doctrinal Calcification** at the level of belief (doctrines passed through how a community reads and hands on its teaching). This law works a fifth way: the practice's own shape, done in its proper form all through life, holds the content in bodily form, received as a person takes part again and again through their forming years.

## Scriptural Ground

The witness for this law runs across at least five biblical authors and both Testaments, and the echo between the two is unusually strong. Moses sets the pattern at Deut. 6:4–9, the Shema, with its instruction built into daily practice: "Hear, O Israel: The Lord our God, the Lord is one. You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your might… You shall teach them diligently to your children, and shall talk of them when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise… You shall write them on the doorposts of your house and on your gates." Moses holds two ways of passing it down side by side: the diligent teaching (the deliberate-teaching that FL.XXI Household Formation names) and the practices woven into daily life (sitting at home with the words present, walking along the road with them, lying down and rising with them, binding them on the hand, wearing them between the eyes, writing them on the doorposts and gates). The two work together in Moses' vision; this law picks out the second as a distinct way of passing faith down, with its own shape.

Moses sets up a deliberately instituted practice at Exod. 12, the founding of the Passover: "And when your children say to you, 'What do you mean by this service?' you shall say, 'It is the sacrifice of the Lord's Passover, for he passed over the houses of the people of Israel in Egypt, when he struck the Egyptians but spared our houses.'" The Passover shows exactly how this law works. The practice (the Passover meal, the unleavened bread, retelling the story of the rescue) is set up with explicit instructions to pass it on — it is to be kept on a regular rhythm across the generations, and the practice itself is the occasion for handing the content on. The content (the rescue from Egypt, the Lord passing over the houses, the covenant the rescue established) is passed down as a person keeps the Passover again and again through their forming years, with the practice holding the content in bodily form — and the child's question ("What do you mean by this service?") is the moment the practice turns from doing into explaining.

The book of Joshua gives a matching case at Josh. 4, with the memorial stones taken from the Jordan: "When your children ask their fathers in times to come, 'What do these stones mean?' then you shall let your children know, 'Israel passed over this Jordan on dry ground.'…" This parallels the Passover pattern — the memorial-stone practice is set up with explicit instructions to pass it on, with the child's question (just like the Passover question) as the turning point from doing into explaining. Joshua's case matters because it shows the pattern outside of worship-and-sacrifice: stacking memorial stones isn't a sacrifice or a religious rite, so this case proves the law works across everyday practices too, not just sacred ones.

Paul gives the great New Testament instituted practice at 1 Cor. 11:23–26: "…the Lord Jesus on the night when he was betrayed took bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke it, and said, 'This is my body, which is for you. Do this in remembrance of me.'… 'This cup is the new covenant in my blood. Do this, as often as you drink it, in remembrance of me.' For as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord's death until he comes." Paul shows the law at work in the central New Testament practice — the breaking of bread and the cup is set up with an explicit instruction to keep doing it ("Do this … as often as you drink it"), and taking part in the bread and cup holds the content in bodily form (the Lord's body, the Lord's blood, the new covenant, proclaiming His death until He comes). The "as often as you do this" matters: it is the regular, repeated keeping of the practice that does the work, not an occasional or once-in-a-while observance.

Luke shows the early church living by this law at Acts 2:42–47: "And they devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and the fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers." Luke names the four practices the early church was built around — the apostles' teaching (deliberate teaching), the fellowship (being present to one another), the breaking of bread (the Lord's Supper), and the prayers (praying together steadily). This matters because the practices were the very fabric of how the early church was formed; they weren't religious duties tacked onto the formation — they were how the formation happened, with the content received as people devoted themselves to the practices on a regular rhythm.

A fifth witness is at 2 Tim. 3:14–17, about how Timothy was formed: "But as for you, continue in what you have learned and have firmly believed, knowing from whom you learned it and how from childhood you have been acquainted with the sacred writings, which are able to make you wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus." Paul names this law at work in Timothy's own formation — Timothy has known the sacred writings *from childhood*, and that came through the practices of his household (the practices of his grandmother Lois and his mother Eunice, named at 2 Tim. 1:5). The phrase "knowing from whom you learned it" names the practice-and-relationship texture of how it was passed down; the formation happened through the household's practices across Timothy's growing-up years, with the content (the Scriptures, the wisdom that leads to salvation) carried by the practice's shape held in the warm presence of the people doing it with him.

A sixth witness, the writer of Ps. 78, names the principle in general: "Things that we have heard and known, that our fathers have told us. We will not hide them from their children, but tell to the coming generation the glorious deeds of the Lord… which he commanded our fathers to teach to their children, that the next generation might know them… and arise and tell them to their children, so that they should set their hope in God and not forget the works of God." The Psalm's version is general; it names the principle without naming any one particular practice. It sets the principle's reach — passing the heart of the faith down the generations through the practices of telling, remembering, and setting the works of God before the next generation.

The Old Testament witness keeps going through the wider observances — the feasts of the Lord (Lev. 23), the cycle of sabbaths and Jubilees (Lev. 25), the cycle of sacrifices through Exodus-Leviticus-Numbers, the three yearly pilgrimages (Deut. 16) — each one a built-in practice working by this law. The New Testament witness keeps going through baptism (Matt. 28:19–20, with its "teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you" naming both the teaching and the practice), the patterns of praying together (Acts 1:14; Acts 4:23–31; Acts 12:5; 1 Tim. 2:1–4), the Scripture-reading patterns (1 Tim. 4:13's "devote yourself to the public reading of Scripture"), and the wider handed-down practices (1 Cor. 11:2's "maintain the traditions even as I delivered them to you"; 2 Thess. 2:15's "stand firm and hold to the traditions that you were taught"). All together, the witness shows how far this law reaches — across both Testaments and a wide range of practices.

## Mechanism

The law works by the practice itself carrying the content, received as a person takes part in the practice again and again through their forming years. The working parts are: the practice's shape, the person's repeated taking-part, the rhythm of how often the practice comes around over a lifetime, the way the practice holds the content inside it, and the practice's resistance to having its content swapped out.

The practice's shape holds the content in bodily form. The Passover seder holds the story of the rescue; the Lord's Supper holds the covenant made new in Christ; baptism holds dying, being buried, and rising with Christ; keeping the Sabbath holds trust in God's provision; praying together holds the community turning toward God; reading Scripture together holds the Word as the community's main guide. The content isn't something that comes alongside the practice; the content is what the practice's shape *is*. Bread broken in the pattern of the Lord's body is what the Lord's Supper is; unleavened bread eaten in haste is what the Passover seder is; the water of baptism, with the person going under and coming up, is what baptism is. The practice's shape is the content put into bodily form — but the shape is the vessel, not the engine. It is the Holy Spirit who works through the practice, and faith that receives what He gives; apart from the Spirit's work and a receiving faith, even a rightly-kept practice can be taken "in an unworthy manner," to judgment rather than grace (1 Cor. 11:27–29). So it is the form *together with* the Spirit who fills it and the faith that receives it — not the bare form by itself — that makes the practice the carrier this law names. (How exactly the Spirit works through a given practice is one of the things the traditions weigh differently; that difference is left to each, as below.)

Taking part again and again through the forming years is how the content is received. You don't get it from a single time through; it comes through doing the practice over and over across a lifetime, each time fresh. The child who keeps the Passover seder year after year doesn't take in the rescue story from any one seder; it sinks in through the repeated keeping, settling deeper over the years. A single time through isn't the law working — the repeated taking-part across the forming years is.

Because the practice's shape carries the content, the Spirit can use it to plant faith before a person can put it into words. A child at the Passover seder may not be able to explain the covenant theology, but it can still enter them through the practice's form — the Spirit using it to plant and shape a faith still growing — years before words could. The form is the Spirit's vessel, not a mechanism that works of itself. This is what sets the law apart from FL.XXI Household Formation's teaching: teaching works through words; this works through a bodily form that can land decades before the words ever could.

Another feature is how the practice resists having its content swapped out. A practice done on a regular rhythm across generations holds up against the content-loss that the other ways of passing faith down are open to. FL.XVII Substitution-Cascade's soaking-it-up can be hijacked when the main adults in the home are pointed the wrong way; FL.XXVI Doctrinal Calcification's tradition of belief can harden and lose its content. A built-in practice, by contrast, is sturdier — its form lasts across generations even after the content has been hollowed out, and that lasting form keeps the shape within which the content can be recovered later. The "form without the power" that 2 Tim. 3:5 names is exactly this — the form keeps going after the power has left; and the way back is to recover the content the practice still holds, rather than to build some brand-new way of passing faith down.

The rhythm deserves a word too. How often a practice comes around — weekly, daily, monthly, yearly, by season — is part of its shape, not a side detail: the Sabbath's weekly return (Exod. 20:8–11), the Passover's yearly return (Exod. 12:14), the Lord's Supper's regular return ("as often as you eat this bread") are each part of what those practices are. The rhythm holds the content across a lifetime through its regular repeating; keep a practice off-rhythm — an occasional Lord's Supper, Passover, or Sabbath — and you get something different out of it. The rhythm is essential.

## Why This Is a Foundational Law

The law meets all four tests for inclusion. First, the backing from many writers is strong: five biblical authors hold it across both Testaments (Moses across Deuteronomy and Exodus; the writer of Joshua; Paul across 1 Corinthians and 2 Timothy; Luke at Acts 2:42–47; the writer of Ps. 78), with the wider Old Testament cycle of feasts and the New Testament instituted practices (baptism, the prayers, the Scripture readings, the handed-down apostolic practices) showing how far the law reaches across Scripture.

Second, the core claim is uncontested across mainstream Christian traditions. Catholics and Orthodox hold it with special weight in the sacraments and the liturgy — exactly the built-in practices the law names; Anglicans through the Book of Common Prayer; Lutherans and the Reformed through the "means of grace" (preaching, the sacraments, prayer, the Scripture readings); Wesleyans, adding the class meeting; Anabaptists and free churches as discipleship, adding believer's baptism and foot-washing; and today's spiritual-disciplines movement (Foster, Willard) under that heading. They differ on the *exact list* of practices and the *theology of how a practice works*, but all hold the core claim in common — built-in practices kept on a regular rhythm across generations pass the faith down.

Third, the law clearly runs in one direction. Built-in practices kept on a regular rhythm with their content alive pass that content down across generations; the same practices kept with their content hollowed out produce the "form without the power" the Mirror names; and no practices at all means no passing-down happens.

Fourth, the law works as a principle. The principle (built-in practices on a regular rhythm, with their content alive, pass the content down across generations) stands apart from any one practice's particular content. It applies to the sacraments the Catholic and Orthodox traditions hold central, the means-of-grace practices the Reformation traditions hold central, the class meeting the Wesleyans hold central, the believer's baptism the Anabaptists hold central, and the spiritual disciplines the Foster-Willard tradition holds central — the same way of working across all the different practices.

This law belongs at the generational level, in the realm of the body and action — because the practice itself is something the body does: taking part in the practice's bodily form, over and over, on its regular rhythm.

**Proposed Law: The Thick Practice Transmission Law. Built-in practices kept on a regular rhythm across generations — keeping the Sabbath, Passover and the seder, baptism and the ways it is remembered, the Lord's Supper and the Lord's table, steady praying together, steady reading of Scripture together, the cycles of catechism, the seasons of the church year — carry the heart of the faith across in a way teaching with words alone never can; the practices hold the content in bodily form, the Holy Spirit works through them and faith receives what He gives, and it is received across the generations as a person takes part in the practice again and again through their forming years, with the practice's regular rhythm essential to how it works.**

***Mirror (stated form):*** *The dark, idol-ward face of the Thick Practice Transmission Law is "form without the power" — the practice still being done while its content has been hollowed out, the outer shape carrying on after the life inside has left. 2 Tim. 3:5 names it: "having the appearance of godliness, but denying its power. Avoid such people." The Pharisees' empty practice-forms in the Gospel run-ins (Mark 7:1–13's tradition of the elders; Matt. 23:23's tithing mint and dill and cumin while neglecting the weightier matters of the law) are the New Testament cases of this. It happens when the practice's outer shape outlasts its living content; the form keeps going while the power leaves, and going on through the motions produces the opposite of what the law is meant to — the practice no longer passes the content down, its lasting shape gets filled with substitutes instead (looking godly, proving you are religiously correct, marking your social or cultural identity), and the people doing it are not formed by the content the practice was built to carry. The way back is to recover the content the practice still holds, keeping the practice's shape intact as the vessel the content can be poured back into.*

***Certainty: Clearly Taught.*** *Five or more biblical authors across both Testaments back it (Moses across Deuteronomy and Exodus; the writer of Joshua; Paul across 1 Corinthians and 2 Timothy; Luke at Acts 2:42–47; the writer of Ps. 78), with the wider Old Testament cycle of feasts and the New Testament instituted practices showing how far it reaches across Scripture. Every mainstream Christian tradition accepts the core claim (they differ on the exact list of practices and on the theology of how a practice works, but hold the underlying principle in common). The direction it runs is clear, and it works as a principle.*

**A word from your Granddaddy.** I grew up in a Disciples of Christ church, which isn't liturgical in the old sense — so this discovery came to me late. Soon after I came to Christ, your grandmother and I landed at Truro Episcopal, with the full prayer-book liturgy and three services every Sunday. I ran the soundboard once a month, which meant I'd hear Rite 2 three times in a single day — I nearly had it by heart. What struck me was how *complete* I felt when the service ended, as if we'd touched every base, all the way around. Years later, James K. A. Smith's *Desiring the Kingdom* gave me the bigger picture: worship is meant to mirror heaven, and its repetitions are meant to train our hearts to *desire* heaven. Across twenty-one years in the Air Force — nineteen different addresses — your grandmother and I have worshiped in all kinds of churches, and here's what we learned: every one of them has a liturgy, whether or not they ever open a prayer book. And one thing has held true every time. Ritual without the Spirit is deadly dull, hard to sit through; that same liturgy filled with the Spirit lifts you up and gives life. We've learned not to settle for anything less — and I hope you kids won't either.
