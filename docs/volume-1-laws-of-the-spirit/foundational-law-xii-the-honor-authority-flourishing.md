# Foundational Law XII: The Honor-Authority Flourishing Law

This law is the working-out of the commandment Paul calls “the first commandment with a promise.” Honoring authority that is set up rightly — your parents first of all, and then, by extension, governing and church leaders — makes you flourish; dishonoring it does the opposite. And the promise is right there in the text (Exod. 20:12: “that your days may be long in the land”). The commandment doesn't just tell you what to do; it states a cause and effect.

It starts as one specific commandment with one specific promise, and its cause-and-effect reaches past the original parent-and-child setting to cover governing leaders and church leaders too, on the testimony of Paul (Rom. 13) and Peter (1 Pet. 2).

## The Scriptural Ground
***Exod. 20:12 (ESV)***

*“Honor your father and your mother, that your days may be long in the land that the Lord your God is giving you.”*

***Deut. 5:16 (ESV)***

*“Honor your father and your mother, as the Lord your God commanded you, that your days may be long, and that it may go well with you in the land that the Lord your God is giving you.”*

***Eph. 6:1–3 (ESV)***

*“Children, obey your parents in the Lord, for this is right. ‘Honor your father and mother’ (this is the first commandment with a promise), ‘that it may go well with you and that you may live long in the land.’”*

***Rom. 13:1–4 (ESV)***

*“Let every person be subject to the governing authorities. For there is no authority except from God, and those that exist have been instituted by God. Therefore whoever resists the authorities resists what God has appointed, and those who resist will incur judgment. For rulers are not a terror to good conduct, but to bad. Would you have no fear of the one who is in authority? Then do what is good, and you will receive his approval, for he is God’s servant for your good.”*

***1 Pet. 2:17 (ESV)***

*“Honor everyone. Love the brotherhood. Fear God. Honor the emperor.”*

## The Mechanism
Four things stand out. First, the law comes with a spelled-out promise, and Paul says so. “That your days may be long” (Exod. 20:12) isn't just general blessing-talk; it's a specific promise tied to a specific commandment. Paul's aside in Eph. 6:2 — “this is the first commandment with a promise” — points this out directly, and that is what makes the command read like a law. It is worded as a command, but built into it is a cause-and-effect claim.

Second, the law works the same way at every size. The parent-and-child relationship is where it starts. The citizen-and-ruler relationship (Rom. 13) is the same thing one size up — community and government. 1 Pet. 2:17 stretches it across the whole social range: “Honor everyone… honor the emperor.” And the Eph. 6:3 promise, “that you may live long in the land,” reaches across the generations by the same pattern: parents honored in one generation means long life for the household down the line. One careful note on that promise: when Paul picks it up he drops the Exodus phrase “the land that the Lord your God is giving you” — the specific land of Israel — so the promise is carried forward but no longer pinned to that one national setting (the words are as readily rendered “on the earth”). It is a real promise, widened in Paul's hands; it is not a guarantee of a set lifespan or an estate for any one family. There is a double side to it: honoring these authorities is, at the same time, honoring God — because (Rom. 13:1) it is God who set them up — even as it plays out as honor between people.

Third, the law clearly has a flip side, even if it isn't always spelled out. Rom. 13:4 names the dark direction at the civic level: those who do wrong should be afraid, “for the ruler does not bear the sword in vain. For he is the servant of God, an avenger who carries out God’s wrath on the wrongdoer.” Dishonoring authority that is set up rightly isn't just a harmless lack of honor; it is a kind of rebellion, and what follows is the opposite of flourishing. Two things keep this from being read as blind submission to power. First, Paul sets his “submit to the authorities” inside a teaching on love: just before it he tells us never to avenge ourselves but to overcome evil with good (Rom. 12:19–21), and just after it that love is the fulfilling of the law (Rom. 13:8–10) — the honor he commands is part of an ethic of love, not a blank check. Second, the law is about authority that is rightly ordered, and working out which authority actually is rightly ordered is itself part of how the law works in real life — not a loophole that cancels it. When an authority commands what God forbids, honoring God comes first: the Hebrew midwives refused Pharaoh's order to kill the baby boys (Exod. 1:17), Daniel kept praying with his windows open against the king's decree, the prophets stood up to unjust kings, and the apostles answered the council, “We must obey God rather than men” (Acts 5:29).

Fourth, the flourishing this law promises is not always a smooth, this-world payoff. Right after “honor the emperor,” Peter tells servants to be subject even to harsh and unjust masters, and holds up Christ — who “when he suffered… did not threaten, but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly” (1 Pet. 2:18–23). Peter's own readers were honoring an emperor who would have many of them killed. So the promise is sure, but its fullest payment often lies past this life: honoring rightly-ordered authority can cost you in the short run and still be the road that flourishes, the way the cross was. Read the promise the way Scripture means it — the long, sometimes cruciform arc toward life, not a guarantee of comfort from every authority you honor.

## Why This Is a Foundational Law
Five passages from three writers (Moses, Paul, Peter), with Eph. 6:2 pointing out the commandment-with-a-promise shape in so many words. The link is uncontested across mainstream Christian traditions, with the well-known exception that you may have to refuse when an authority orders something against God's law (Acts 5:29). That exception is about where the law's limits are, not about which way it runs. And it is easy to see at work: households and societies that honor rightly-ordered authority tend to flourish over the long haul, and those that don't tend to break down — one of the most visible patterns in all of human life, for anyone willing to watch across enough decades for the built-in delay to show.

**Proposed Law (Operational, Wide Consent): Honoring authority that is rightly ordered — your parents first, and then governing and church leaders, on the testimony of Paul and Peter — makes the one who honors it flourish, after the usual delay; dishonoring it does the opposite, by the same cause-and-effect. The law comes with a spelled-out promise (Exod. 20:12, pointed out as such in Eph. 6:2) and works at every size: parent and child, citizen and government, and on down the generations through the long-life promise.**

**Certainty: Clearly Taught  ***— though the certainty is not even across the whole law, and it is worth saying where it is rock-solid and where it is more inferred. At its heart — honoring father and mother, the one commandment Scripture itself tags with a promise (Exod. 20:12; Eph. 6:2) — this law is as clearly taught as anything in this volume: a spelled-out promise, more than one writer, the same pattern showing up across the generations. The reach outward to governing and church authority (Rom. 13; 1 Pet. 2) is real and well-grounded in direction, but it stands a step further from the text: how flourishing follows from honoring a ruler or a church leader is more inferred than spelled out, and it leans on a discernment — which authority is actually “rightly ordered” — that Scripture everywhere takes for granted but nowhere catalogs. So hold the parental commandment-with-promise as Clearly Taught, and the wider civil and church-authority application as a reasonable inference: strong in direction, softer in the exact certainty. The law's limits (Acts 5:29; the prophets standing up to unjust kings) are part of how it actually works, not a threat to it — the leftover uncertainty is about how hard that weighing can be, and how far the promise reaches past the parental core, not about the law's basic direction.*

**A word from your Granddaddy.** Here is something I have come to see in years of talking this law over with people: honoring your father and mother is a lifelong command, but obeying them is not. When you are a child under your parents' roof, the two run together — Paul tells children to obey (Eph. 6:1). But once you reach the age of accountability, when you answer to God for your own choices, honoring your parents and obeying them are no longer the same thing. Honor never ends; you owe them honor as long as they live, and so do I. Obedience, though, becomes a matter of weighing — exactly the same discernment this law asks about any rightly-ordered authority. Sometimes honoring God will mean doing just what your parents judge best for you; sometimes it will mean choosing differently, while still treating them with all the honor the commandment requires. The one thing it never means is that you are off the hook for your own decisions. You stand before God for those — not your mother, not your father, and not me. Honor them always; obey God first.
