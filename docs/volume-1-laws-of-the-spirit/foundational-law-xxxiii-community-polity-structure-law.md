# Foundational Law XXXIII: The Community Polity Structure / Ecclesial Architecture Law

This law is about how a church is organized — what's often called its *polity*: who leads, who follows, how they relate as peers; the offices and what's required to hold them; how decisions get made; and the arrangements that keep a church going over many years. Every church runs under some such structure, and the structure shapes everything else the church does. The same congregation organized a different way would carry out its life differently. This isn't just paperwork you can change without effect; it's the frame inside which all the church's other work happens — its shared discernment (FL.XX Gathered-Body Discernment), its worship (FL.XXXII Communal Worship Heart-Alignment), its shared feeling and its care for the wounded (FL.XXIX Corporate Emotional Integration, FL.XXX Communal Soul-Care for the Wounded), its hearing of the Word (FL.XXXI Corporate Scriptural Reception), and the things it does together (FL.XVIII Bitter-Root Community, FL.XXIV Confession-in-Community, FL.XXV Restoration-of-the-Erring). The frame shapes all of them.

This law has to be handled carefully, because Scripture clearly teaches the principle — that a church needs some structure — but it doesn't lock in one particular form. Christians have organized churches in many ways: led by bishops (episcopal), by a council of elders (presbyterian), by the whole congregation (congregational), by Spirit-recognized leaders (charismatic), by a team of elders, as a household of faith, and in various blends of these. Which of those forms is most faithful is real theological work that each tradition does for itself; the catalog doesn't settle it. This is the same approach taken with FL.XXIII Sabbath Rest: accept the principle on its strong, many-author scriptural footing, and leave the specific form to each tradition. So here the catalog accepts that *some* structure must frame a church's life, while leaving the question of *which* structure to the traditions' own work.

## Scriptural Ground

Two biblical writers teach this law in depth, with more support across the rest of Scripture. Luke develops it through the book of Acts. The early church's first big organizing decision comes at Acts 6:1–7, the appointment of the seven: "Now in these days when the disciples were increasing in number, a complaint by the Hellenists arose against the Hebrews because their widows were being neglected in the daily distribution. And the twelve… said, '…pick out from among you seven men of good repute, full of the Spirit and of wisdom, whom we will appoint to this duty. But we will devote ourselves to prayer and to the ministry of the word.' And what they said pleased the whole gathering, and they chose Stephen… These they set before the apostles, and they prayed and laid their hands on them. And the word of God continued to increase, and the number of the disciples multiplied greatly in Jerusalem, and a great many of the priests became obedient to the faith." Here is how it happens: a real need forces the question (the church has grown, and some widows are being overlooked in the daily food distribution). The apostles work out a structure — seven men appointed to handle the serving so that the twelve can keep to prayer and the ministry of the Word. Notice the parts: the gathered church makes the decision under the apostles' leadership; the structure splits up the work (serving tables from the ministry of the Word and prayer); the whole church approves it; it's sealed with prayer and the laying-on of hands; and it bears fruit — the Word spreads, the disciples multiply, and even many priests come to faith.

Luke shows the pattern spreading at Acts 14:23: "And when they had appointed elders for them in every church, with prayer and fasting they committed them to the Lord in whom they believed." As Paul and Barnabas plant churches, they appoint elders in each one, sealing it with prayer and fasting. The phrase "in every church" matters — this isn't optional or church-by-church; structure is set up everywhere, because a church's life needs it. Luke shows the same later: Paul calls the Ephesian elders to Miletus (Acts 20:17) and tells them, "Pay careful attention to yourselves and to all the flock, in which the Holy Spirit has made you overseers, to care for the church of God, which he obtained with his own blood" (Acts 20:28). That the Holy Spirit makes them overseers grounds the structure in God's own appointing, not in mere human arrangement.

Luke shows the structure handling a crisis at Acts 15:1–35 — the Jerusalem council. Watch it work: a dispute breaks out at Antioch (vv. 1–2); the church deliberately sends the question up to Jerusalem (v. 2); the apostles, elders, and church there receive Paul and Barnabas (vv. 4, 6, 12); they hold an orderly discussion (Peter at vv. 6–11, Paul and Barnabas at v. 12, James at vv. 13–21); they reach a decision together (vv. 22–29, the apostles, elders, and whole church); they put it in a letter (vv. 22–29); and the letter is received with joy at the churches it concerned (vv. 30–35). This shows the structure reaching beyond a single congregation to arrangements that spanned whole regions in the early church.

Paul spells out the offices and what they require across 1 Tim. 3 and Titus 1. In 1 Tim. 3:1–13 he names the overseer (the Greek word *episkopos*, also translated "bishop"; vv. 1–7) and the deacon (Greek *diakonos*, "servant"; vv. 8–13), with the character each must have. In Titus 1:5–9 he names the elder (Greek *presbyteros*), whose qualifications largely match the overseer's — and verses 5 and 7 there treat "elder" and "overseer" as the same office. Those character lists matter: they show the structure isn't just about roles on an org chart but about the kind of person who fills them. The offices call for proven, godly character.

Paul names the deepest truths about church structure at Eph. 4:11–13: "And he gave the apostles, the prophets, the evangelists, the shepherds and teachers, to equip the saints for the work of ministry, for building up the body of Christ, until we all attain to the unity of the faith and of the knowledge of the Son of God, to mature manhood, to the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ." Three things stand out, and the whole law rests on them. First, the offices are Christ's gift — "he gave" them; they come from Him, not just from human organizing. Second, they exist for a purpose — "to equip the saints for the work of ministry, for building up the body of Christ"; the structure serves the body, it isn't there for its own sake. Third, they aim at a goal still ahead — "until we all attain … to the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ"; the structure is meant to carry the church toward full maturity in Christ, not just to keep an institution running.

Paul adds more at 1 Cor. 12:27–31, where the body of Christ has gifts and offices: "Now you are the body of Christ and individually members of it. And God has appointed in the church first apostles, second prophets, third teachers, then miracles, then gifts of healing, helping, administrating, and various kinds of tongues." His "first... second... third" shows an order of priority among the roles. Other verses fill in details: elders who lead well deserve fair support — "Let the elders who rule well be considered worthy of double honor, especially those who labor in preaching and teaching" (1 Tim. 5:17); the wider case for supporting those who minister (1 Cor. 9:1–14); and the purpose of church authority, "for building up and not for tearing down" (2 Cor. 13:10).

Hebrews adds a word about how leaders and members relate, at Heb. 13:7, 17: "Remember your leaders, those who spoke to you the word of God… imitate their faith" (v. 7); "Obey your leaders and submit to them, for they are keeping watch over your souls, as those who will have to give an account…" (v. 17). This names the give-and-take between leaders and led — each owes something to the other — and it names the accountability behind it: leaders will answer to God for the souls in their care, and that is why members are to follow and support them.

Peter adds his own word to elders at 1 Pet. 5:1–4: "So I exhort the elders among you, as a fellow elder…: shepherd the flock of God that is among you, exercising oversight, not under compulsion, but willingly… not for shameful gain, but eagerly; not domineering over those in your charge, but being examples to the flock. And when the chief Shepherd appears, you will receive the unfading crown of glory." This describes how an elder is to lead — willingly, not greedily, by example rather than by throwing his weight around — and it points to the reward Christ will give when He returns.

The Old Testament shows the same pattern from the start, in the structures Moses set up: Jethro's advice to appoint capable men over groups of thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens (Exod. 18:13–27); the seventy elders on whom God put His Spirit (Num. 11:16–17, 24–30); Moses's later retelling of it (Deut. 1:9–18); the appointing of judges and officials in every town (Deut. 16:18–20); and Jehoshaphat's setting up of judges and priestly arrangements (2 Chron. 19:4–11). From the very beginning of Israel's life as God's people, there was a God-given structure.

Put together, the witness runs across both Testaments. At least two writers teach the law directly and in depth — Luke through Acts and Paul through his letters — with Hebrews, Peter, and the books of Moses and the later Old Testament adding support. The thread runs unbroken from Moses's day to the early church.

## Mechanism

Here is how it works. A church's structure acts as the frame that shapes everything else the church does.

The most important thing Paul teaches is that the structure has a purpose. Eph. 4:12 names it: "to equip the saints for the work of ministry, for building up the body of Christ." The structure exists to build up the church, by equipping ordinary believers to serve. It's never an end in itself; it serves the church's growth toward maturity. That building-up happens through several kinds of work the structure makes possible: teaching that grounds the church in sound doctrine (1 Tim. 4:13; 2 Tim. 3:16–17; Titus 1:9); pastoral care that sustains the members (Acts 20:28; 1 Pet. 5:2; Heb. 13:17); discipline that deals with sin and restores the fallen (Matt. 18:15–20; 1 Cor. 5; 2 Cor. 2:5–11; Gal. 6:1); the equipping work of apostles and prophets that keeps forming the body (Eph. 4:11–16); the organizing work that arranges the church's life (Acts 6; 1 Cor. 12:28 lists "administrating" among the gifts); and the mission work that extends the church's reach (Acts 1:8). The structure exists to serve all these — and a structure that just keeps running out of habit, doing none of this real work, is exactly the failure the Mirror below describes.

The next thing Paul teaches is that the structure aims at a goal still ahead. Eph. 4:13 names it: "until we all attain to the unity of the faith and of the knowledge of the Son of God, to mature manhood, to the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ." The structure works within God's long plan; it's part of how the church keeps growing toward the full maturity Christ is preparing His body for. This is the same far horizon that FL.XXVIII Generational Nested Structure points to — the whole people of God moving toward what God has in store.

Christians have organized churches in genuinely different ways. Catholics and Orthodox have episcopal government (bishop, presbyter, and deacon; the line of bishops traced to the apostles; councils above). The Reformed have presbyterian government (elders in each church, with presbyteries and synods above). The Congregational and free churches have congregational government (each local church governs itself). The Anabaptists have gathered-church government (the congregation discerns together). The Wesleyans have connectional government (linked conferences, class meetings). The Pentecostals have Spirit-led government (gifts and offices as the Spirit leads). And many blend these — the Anglican mix of bishops and Reformed structure, the Baptist mix of pastors and congregational rule, the modern non-denominational versions — each with its own reasons.

The catalog does not take sides on the specific form. It accepts the principle — that *some* structure must frame a church's life — on its strong, many-author scriptural footing, and leaves which form is best to each tradition (the same approach as FL.XXIII Sabbath Rest: accept the scriptural principle, leave the day and the practices to the traditions).

And structure is not optional — some structure is always at work; a church can't do without one. Running with none brings real problems: drift, where there's no authority or accountability; fragmentation, where there's no way to decide; and disorder, where no one knows who is responsible for what. Which form it takes — bishops, elders, congregation, or another — is what the catalog leaves to each tradition's own theological work.

It's the frame inside which all the other community laws do their work — discernment, worship, shared feeling, care for the wounded, hearing the Word, confession, restoration. Just as FL.XXVIII Generational Nested Structure holds everything together across the generations, this law holds everything together within a single church.

## Why This Is a Foundational Law

This law meets all four tests for inclusion — with the special note that the principle is accepted while the specific form is left to the traditions. First, the scriptural backing is solid: at least two biblical writers teach it directly (Luke through Acts, Paul through his letters), with Hebrews, Peter, and the books of Moses and the later Old Testament adding support across both Testaments.

Second, every mainstream Christian tradition accepts the core claim — though they agree at the level of the principle, not the specific form. They all agree that some structure must frame a church's life; they disagree about which form is best, each holding its own view. The catalog accepts the shared principle while plainly leaving the question of form to the traditions.

Third, the law clearly runs in one direction: set up a structure → that structure shapes everything else the church does → and that shaping carries on through the church's life.

Fourth, it works as a principle, not just one custom. The principle — that some structure must frame a church's life — stands above any specific form (bishops, elders, congregation, and so on). The catalog grounds the principle in Scripture while leaving the specific forms to each tradition.

This law belongs with the community's structural frame — it's about the framework that holds a church together. And it runs from Christ down: the offices are Christ's gift (Eph. 4:11), the Spirit appoints the overseers (Acts 20:28), and the whole structure serves the growth of Christ's body toward maturity (Eph. 4:13). That it comes down from Christ is the main thing; the relationships among leaders and members flow out of His gift.

**Proposed Law: The Community Polity Structure / Ecclesial Architecture Law. Some structure — who leads, who follows, how they relate as peers; the offices and what they require; how decisions are made; the arrangements that keep a church going over time — always frames the gathered community's life and shapes everything else it does. That structure is Christ's gift (the offices come from Him, to build up His body), it exists for a purpose (to equip God's people for service and grow the body to maturity), and it aims at a goal still ahead (the church's growth toward the full stature of Christ). Which form the structure takes — bishops, elders, congregation, Spirit-led, gathered-church, or some blend — is left to each tradition's own theological work, and the catalog does not settle it.**

***Mirror (stated form):*** *The dark, idol-ward face of this law shows up in two opposite ways, both of which Paul warns against. The first is structure becoming the whole point — the framework treated as an end in itself, the building-up purpose forgotten, the machinery grinding on out of sheer institutional habit. This is the "having the appearance of godliness, but denying its power" Paul names at 2 Tim. 3:5 — the same hollow, form-without-power problem FL.XXVII Thick Practice Transmission describes for a church's shared mind, now at the level of church structure: the structure runs on while its real purpose has drained away. The second failure is the opposite — too little structure, or none: a church with no real authority or accountability, no way to make decisions, no clear offices or standards, where drift, fragmentation, and disorder pile up over time. Diotrephes is the New Testament's picture of structure gone wrong — "Diotrephes, who likes to put himself first, does not acknowledge our authority … he refuses to welcome the brothers, and also stops those who want to and puts them out of the church" (3 John 9–10) — a twisted structure producing twisted results everywhere it touches. The way back is either to restore the structure's true purpose (where it has become mere machinery) or to build the structure that's missing (where there's been too little) — and exactly how depends on the tradition's own form, which the catalog leaves to each tradition's theological work.*

***Certainty: Clearly Taught.*** *Two or more biblical writers across both Testaments back it, more than the catalog's minimum (Luke through Acts, Paul through his letters, with Hebrews, Peter, and the books of Moses and the later Old Testament adding support). Mainstream Christian traditions accept it at the level of the principle, while each holds its own view on the specific form — which the catalog leaves to them rather than settling. The direction it runs is clear. And it works as a principle, with the specific forms deliberately left to each tradition's own theological work.*

***A word from your Granddaddy.*** *After many years inside many kinds of organizations, here is what I've learned: a structure either lifts people up and frees them to grow, or it strangles the very life and exploration out of them. And you don't get to opt out — every church has some structure, whether it admits it or not. The only real question is the one underneath it: is the structure built to grow God's kingdom, or to feed the needs of the people running it? One thing I've learned to watch for is clear lines of spiritual authority — rightly aligned, authority is a force multiplier, not a cage (see [the Spiritual Authority exploration](exploration-07-spiritual-authority.md)). When your grandmother and I would move and go looking for a church home, I'd ask the pastor one question: whose authority are you under? More than once — especially in churches that answer to no body beyond themselves, for example, many non-denominational churches — I heard a warm "the Lord Jesus Christ." I liked the answer, but it isn't the test. The test is plainer: does anyone get to challenge you on your budget, and must you answer for it? Where no one does, you are probably under no real authority at all.*
