# Foundational Law XV: The Hardening Law

This fifteenth law names something Scripture treats as real and points to again and again across many writers: keep refusing to turn toward God, and your heart slowly grows less and less able to respond to truth, until the way is shut. The closing happens little by little and adds up, and it reaches a point past which you need help from outside, because your own ability to take truth in has been damaged. Scripture calls this *hardening*. It is the clearest "dark side" law about how responsive a heart stays, and it works at every size — one person, a whole community, and across the generations.

This is one of the more theologically loaded laws, because Christians have explained God's part and our part in the hardening differently. The Reformed tradition leans on God's part, reading the Romans 1 "God gave them up" line and the Exodus story of Pharaoh's hardening as God's own action that human refusal opens the door to. The Wesleyan-Arminian tradition leans on the human part, reading the same passages as the natural fallout of steady refusal — something God allows and confirms but doesn't directly cause. The Catholic tradition holds both sides together, calling it a hardening and the loss of grace once it has become a habit. The Orthodox tradition does the same, describing a will that settles into being set against God. The differences are real and worth naming, but the thing itself — that steady refusal hardens, that it builds up, that timing matters — every mainstream tradition agrees on. This chapter stays with how the law works and doesn't try to settle the debate over God's part versus ours.

## The Scriptural Ground

The hardening is named across both Testaments by at least four writers, and the part about timing — what sets hardening apart from other kinds of build-up on the line to God — is named most plainly in Hebrews.

Exod. 7:13, 22; 8:15, 19, 32; 9:7, 12, 34–35; 10:1, 20, 27; 11:10; 14:8 (Moses, ESV). The Exodus story of Pharaoh's hardening is the classic case, and the shape of the cycle is itself part of what the law is naming. Across the ten plagues the wording shifts — sometimes Pharaoh hardens his own heart (8:15, 8:32, 9:34), sometimes his heart "was hardened" with no one named (9:7, 9:35), sometimes the Lord hardens Pharaoh's heart (9:12, 10:1, 10:20, 10:27, 11:10, 14:8). Early on it is mostly Pharaoh hardening himself; later it is mostly the Lord doing the hardening; over the whole cycle it moves from self-hardening to being-hardened. The story isn't being sloppy about who is doing what — it is tracing the path: keep hardening yourself and you arrive at being-hardened, and the point where you would need outside help is exactly the point where the story stops saying Pharaoh did it and starts saying the Lord did.

Heb. 3:7–15 (the writer of Hebrews, ESV): "Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts as in the rebellion, on the day of testing in the wilderness, where your fathers put me to the test and saw my works for forty years… Take care, brothers, lest there be in any of you an evil, unbelieving heart, leading you to fall away from the living God. But exhort one another every day, as long as it is called 'today,' that none of you may be hardened by the deceitfulness of sin." Hebrews names three things no other passage names so directly: that timing matters ("today"), that it builds up ("every day, as long as it is called today"), and that the hardening fools you while it happens ("hardened by the deceitfulness of sin" — it works without your realizing it is working). And this is written to people inside the church: hardening isn't only an unbeliever's problem; it can grip a committed disciple, right at the point of the next refusal.

Rom. 1:21–28 (Paul, ESV): "Therefore God gave them up in the lusts of their hearts to impurity… For this reason God gave them up to dishonorable passions… And since they did not see fit to acknowledge God, God gave them up to a debased mind to do what ought not to be done." The three-times-repeated "God gave them up" is Paul's way of naming God's part, and the human part is right there in the same passage: "they did not honor him as God or give thanks to him… they did not see fit to acknowledge God." Both sit in one paragraph, and God's giving-them-up is named as the result of their refusing to acknowledge Him. Paul doesn't try to settle God's part versus ours; he names both inside the one process.

Mark 6:52 and 8:17 (Mark, ESV): "for they did not understand about the loaves, but their hearts were hardened." "And Jesus, aware of this, said to them, 'Why are you discussing the fact that you have no bread? Do you not yet perceive or understand? Are your hearts hardened?'" Mark's note about the disciples' beginning hardness — *inside the inner circle, after the feeding, after the second feeding* — widens the law's reach. Hardening isn't only for the stubborn outsider; it can touch even someone who has already received and is standing right there with the One whose voice she is hearing. The hardness Jesus spots in His own disciples is small and just starting — He sees it before they do. The law is at work at every stage of growth, not just at the doorway of first belief.

Eph. 4:18–19 (Paul, ESV): "They are darkened in their understanding, alienated from the life of God because of the ignorance that is in them, due to the hardness of their heart. They have become callous and have given themselves up to sensuousness, greedy to practice every kind of impurity." Paul names where steady hardening leads: a darkened understanding, being cut off from the life of God, a calloused response, a slide into impurity. He doesn't quite say the hardening *causes* the darkening; he names them as parts of the same condition, with the hardening as its central feature.

Prov. 29:1 (Solomon, ESV): "He who is often reproved, yet stiffens his neck, will suddenly be broken beyond healing." The wisdom writers name the same thing from another angle: warned again and again, the person stiffens his neck, and then is suddenly broken past mending. The point-of-no-return is named outright. The pattern is steady refusal, then a sharp break — and after the break the person can't recover by the means that would have worked before it.

## The Mechanism

Three things about how this law works.

First, it builds up; it isn't a one-time thing. A single moment of refusing truth isn't yet hardening in the sense this law means. The law needs *sustained* refusal — the same thing again and again over time, the weight piling up and steadily giving the next truth less and less to grip. Hebrews 3 names this build-up directly: "every day, as long as it is called 'today.'" That is why timing matters: the law works differently at the first refusal than at the tenth, and differently at the tenth than at the hundredth.

Second, it's far easier to go one way than the other. A heart's responsiveness is much easier to wear down than to build back up. The wearing-down comes from steady refusal; the building-back-up needs either help from outside (a prophetic word, the presence of God in the sacraments, a community that keeps speaking the truth, the Spirit's particular work) or some jolt that breaks the path the build-up has set. Prov. 29:1 calls this being "suddenly broken beyond healing" — the break is the sharp turn that's needed once the path has reached its final stretch — and Heb. 3:13 names the "today" that marks the window while it is still open. This one-way tilt is the very reason it gets a law of its own: unlike the build-up Sin Blockage describes, which clears when you confess, this is a one-way slide where getting back takes a fresh push from outside, not just more of the same.

Third, it works without the person noticing. Heb. 3:13 says the hardening happens "by the deceitfulness of sin" — you don't feel the hardening as hardening; you feel it as the new normal. The shrinking ability to respond just registers as your ordinary range, not as a loss. So the law is invisible to the person in the middle of it, and shows up only looking back, or to someone else — a prophet, a spouse, a Companion — who can see what the person on the slide can't. This is a real problem: someone in the early stages can't reliably spot it in herself, and a Companion working with her has to carry the watching she can't yet do for herself.

One thing more has to be said, because a law about God hardening hearts can leave a frightening picture of God if it stops too soon. Scripture does name God's sovereignty in it plainly — "he has mercy on whomever he wills, and he hardens whomever he wills" (Rom. 9:18). But it is just as emphatic that this is no pleasure to Him: "Have I any pleasure in the death of the wicked, declares the Lord God, and not rather that he should turn from his way and live?" (Ezek. 18:23; cf. 33:11, "I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked, but that the wicked turn from his way and live"). The God who hardens is the same God who pleads, right up to the edge, for the hardening person to turn. And He holds out the one cure the hardened heart cannot work for itself: "I will give you a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you. And I will remove the heart of stone from your flesh and give you a heart of flesh" (Ezek. 36:26). That is the deepest form of the outside help this law says is needed — not a technique the hardened can apply, but a new heart only God can give. So the dark pole has a face, and it is not a face that delights in the closing; it is the face of the One who keeps saying, "Turn, and live."

## Why This Is a Foundational Law

The backing from many writers is strong. They span at least five biblical authors — Moses, the writer of Hebrews, Paul, Mark, and Solomon — across both Testaments and across story, warning, letter, gospel, and wisdom writing. Which way it runs is uncontested across mainstream Christian traditions, even where they explain God's part and our part differently. The Reformed tradition leans harder on God's part; the Wesleyan-Arminian tradition leans harder on ours; the Catholic and Orthodox traditions hold the two in their own balance; all of them affirm that the hardening is real and works the way Scripture says. The differences are about how to describe the mechanism, not about whether the thing happens.

It is also well-documented in real life. Formation Companions see firsthand that someone who has been refusing the truth on a particular point for years relates to that point very differently than someone meeting it for the first time. Pastors know the slide of steady refusal is real, and that turning it around takes targeted work that ordinary preaching doesn't accomplish. Therapists know the same thing under different words — defensive patterns hardening into place. Different fields, different vocabulary, same thing — which is good confirmation it is real.

This law reaches wider than the build-up-on-the-line form of resistance that V1.Exp6 Obedience Channel names. Hardening covers a shrinking response to every way God speaks — the prophet's invitation, the prick of conscience, the grace at work even in those who haven't yet had any specific revelation, and the beginning hardness in a disciple *right inside* the formation work. The two laws are related (steady disobedience feeds hardening; hardening dulls your response to whatever God shows next) but they cover different ground.

**Proposed Law (Operational Law of Wide Consent): Steadily refusing to turn toward God slowly dulls the heart's response to truth, until the way finally closes; the closing happens little by little, adds up, and reaches a point past which you need help from outside, because your own ability to take truth in has been damaged (Heb. 3:7–15; Prov. 29:1). It is far easier to go one way than back — the dulling comes from steady refusal, while recovery takes outside help or a jolt that breaks the slide — and it works without the person in the middle of it noticing ("the deceitfulness of sin," Heb. 3:13). Scripture names both a human side (the person hardening himself: Exod. 8:15, 8:32, 9:34; Eph. 4:18) and a God side (the Lord hardening, or giving over: Exod. 9:12, 10:1, 10:20; Rom. 1:24, 26, 28); the traditions explain how those two fit together differently, and this law stays with the part they all hold in common. It works at every size: in one person (Pharaoh, the disciples in Mark, the believer Hebrews is writing to), in a whole community (the wilderness generation; the "they" of Romans 1), and arguably across the generations (the prophets' grief over Israel's centuries of refusal). It pairs with V1.Exp6 Obedience Channel as the wider law in the same family: Obedience Channel names the build-up from disobeying a specific word; Hardening names the broader slide across steady refusal of every kind.**

***Mirror:*** *This law has no separate Mirror of its own; it is itself the dark, idol-ward face of the building-back-up of responsiveness that V1.Exp6 Obedience Channel and FL.VII Drawing-Near Reciprocity name from the God-ward side. The bright face is the steady responsiveness that grows the heart's ability to receive — what Drawing-Near's "God draws near" describes, and what Obedience Channel's open way describes. The two faces together finish the picture: steady turning toward God builds responsiveness; steady refusal wears it down; one thing seen from the two directions the heart can turn.*

***Certainty: Clearly Taught.*** *The backing from many writers is strong (five biblical authors across both Testaments, several kinds of writing). Acceptance is uncontested across mainstream Christian traditions on the thing itself; they differ over how God's part and our part fit together, which changes the description but not the claim. And it is well-documented in real life — in pastoral, therapeutic, and contemplative writing alike. The one open question is where the line falls between ordinary resistance and full "hardening" — the slide is real and well-attested, but Scripture doesn't pin down the exact point, and it is fair to think it varies from person to person and depends on which truth is being refused.*

***A word from your Granddaddy.*** *You’ve just read how a heart can slowly close until it cannot hear — let me tell you I know it from the inside, because I lived the whole length of it. In my mid-teens I walked away from the church I grew up in and the faith I’d been taught, and I stayed away more than twenty years. I went down the dark hallways one after another — atheism, then agnosticism, then humanism — and when none of them held, I gave up and was really a religion of one; I’d have called myself a Tittle-ist, a man-made faith with a membership of me. The plain truth is the cognitive assent was never really there; all I ever had to do to keep going was turn away and keep walking, so I did. And across those years, without ever feeling it happen, I hardened my own heart — until I truly could not hear the deeper words the Lord was speaking. It had become my normal. Now hear the most important thing I can tell you: I did not find my own way back, because by then I couldn’t. It took Him breaking in from the outside. On June 14, 1982, He spoke to me directly, by name, and called me to Himself — and the thing I still cannot get over is that I knew exactly who it was, after all those decades of denying and ignoring Him. And I knew, somehow, that I had a choice right then, and that I might never be handed another. Praise God, I turned. I asked Him to be my Lord, and my life has not been the same for one day since.*
