# The Formation Companion

*A Synthesis Role, a Three-Taxonomy Development Map, and a Draft Job Description*

*John G. Tittle — Intentional Journey of the Heart — Formation Document v5_5_6. Draft — April 2026*

# Abstract
This paper proposes the Formation Companion as a synthesis role that stands at the intersection of four spiritual-care traditions centered on reflective listening. Three are established Christian traditions — Spiritual Direction, Transformation Prayer Ministry, and Stephen Ministry; the fourth, group facilitation as developed in the Band of Brothers and Fellowship of the Heart contexts, is Christian-framed but draws openly on secular group-work and depth-psychology tools, disciplined within a Christian framework (see the discerning-use section). All are held together by the framework of the Intentional Journey of the Heart project. This paper shows that these four traditions, developed largely independently over multiple years, converge on a substantial common core of competencies organized across the three learning taxonomies of Bloom, Krathwohl, and Simpson. A three-level development progression is proposed: Companion I (Protocol Competent), Companion II (Mode Practitioner), and Companion III (Integrated Practitioner). Companion  III includes capstone competency manifested by the capacity to discern in a given interactive moment the application of one or more of the four operational modes, and to move between modes without losing grounding within the need or needs presented. The paper also addresses the discerning use of non-Christian-grounded methodologies, such as Shadow Work, and the role of Bloom's taxonomies. These three competency levels require character prerequisites. These will be developed later in a separate paper based on consultations with the Council.
Keywords: spiritual formation, formation companion, spiritual direction, Transformation Prayer Ministry, Stephen Ministry, Fellowship of the Heart, facilitator development, Affective Taxonomy


# Introduction: The Who of Formation
This paper is derived from and supports the Formation Documents. These documents propose that the progressive internalization of hearing and obeying God is the essence of spiritual formation or becoming more like Christ. The first paper is about the parts that are formed (TA), how the heart forms (HFT), how the soul and spirit mature (SST), and how all three fit together at the individual and in interaction with a small group (MSFIG). This paper addresses the person who helps another on that formation journey.
This proposal was entered through a specific door. For some years, I have been part of a Band of Brothers, a path or tradition that trains men to facilitate the formation of other men in small groups. The training role was called the Weapons Master. Drawn from the military, the imagery was about equipping men with appropriate spiritual and mental weapons to fight their internal and external battles. A paper on that role was first published in February 2005 and was used by a church’s men’s ministry that wanted to establish and support men’s small groups. That job description emerged from training several men on the requirements of that role in mentoring such groups.  Since writing that paper, two events have provided impetus for this paper. First, much of the recent training is explicitly mixed gender, so the Weapons Master paper's masculine framing does not carry over. Second, the 2005 Weapons Master paper understated the role of the small group facilitator's ongoing internalization of hearing and obeying God's word. That was named as a prerequisite but not explicitly developed. Both of these gaps need to be filled.
When the Weapons Master role was aligned with three established Christian spiritual care traditions, Spiritual Direction, Transformation Prayer Ministry, and the Stephen Ministry tradition, something unexpected became visible. These three traditions (within small group dynamics) addressed development independently of each other over many years. However, they converge on a substantial common core. The differences among them, when examined carefully, turn out to be different operational modes, i.e., what the practitioner or facilitator does in a given moment, rather than apparent differences in what the practitioner fundamentally does or seeks to achieve. That common core is proposed here as the Formation Companion. The traditions are four ways the same person can be operating, depending on what the moment and need call for. The Companion at full maturity facilitates the core process with sufficient competence in each of the four modes to discern what a given moment requires.
This framing transcends my original version of a weapons master, which facilitates group interaction in support of Band of Brothers. The result is a proposed synthesis role that places the Formation Companion inside a much older and broader tradition than Band of Brothers alone, draws on the best of four independent streams of formation practice — three of established Christian tradition and one a Christian-framed group facilitation that disciplines secular tools — and uses the three-part taxonomy framework that helps make facilitation competencies measurable and developable. The taxonomies reveal the structure beneath separate traditions that appear different on the surface.
# What a Formation Companion Is — and Is Not
## The Definition
A Formation Companion is a fellow pilgrim on their own journey, willing to help another pilgrim. The Companion is not an expert or authority over the other person's formation and discipleship. They are not a paid professional therapeutic counselor, psychologist, or psychiatrist. They are a believer further along in their relationship with God, with faith and competence in specific tools that can open another’s heart to more of God. Maturity in a Companion’s walk can usefully accompany another person on part of their journey.
The word companion is load-bearing. It resonates with a partnering developed through Spiritual Direction and Stephen Ministry traditions, where a companion is defined by presence-with rather than expertise-over. Companion aligns with the pilgrim vocabulary from the Band of Brothers tradition. Companion embraces the core insight that formation is a journey two people can walk together, one further along on a particular stretch, both still on the road. This biblical walk is illustrated in Paul, a companion to Timothy. Richard Foster tells of a time when he sought spiritual direction. He traveled some distance to see a respected spiritual director. Before they started, the director asked Richard to hear the director’s confession. At the end, he asked Richard if he still wanted him (a sinner) to be his spiritual director. That humility demonstrates the attitude of a Formation Companion.
## This Is Not Therapy
This deserves clarity because this is an important boundary the Companion must hold. The Formation Companion is not a licensed professional counselor or therapist. The relationship is not clinical, meaning results-oriented therapy. Instead, the relationship and interaction are process-oriented. Effects of the companioning may be therapeutic. Healing and real change do happen, and often. However, a therapeutic effect is not the same as a therapy-defined professional relationship governed by state licensure, clinical training, ethical codes, insurance structures, and legal standards of care. Companioning is a fellow pilgrim helping another pilgrim externalize inner thoughts and process within the bounds of the Companion's training and maturity. More to the point, the core of spiritual direction is helping another to hear from God for themselves, not directing or having authority over their lives, no matter how much they might ask for it. See Nemeck and Coombs for details.
This boundary matters for three reasons, each of which needs to be understood and held by every Companion at every level:
- Protection of the pilgrim. A pilgrim whose situation requires licensed professional intervention, such as clinical depression, trauma beyond what a non-licensed helper can competently hold, acute crisis, psychosis, or any condition that meaningfully impairs functioning, requires a licensed clinician, not a Companion. A Companion who tries to intervene with some sort of clinical intervention is not helping. They are delaying more substantive help the pilgrim actually needs. Companion competency includes the capacity to recognize when a referral to a licensed professional is required and the willingness to make the referral without interpreting it as a personal failure. For example, Stephen Ministry provides exact criteria for making such a referral to professional help.
- Protection of the Companion. Companions are not insured as clinicians, are not credentialed as clinicians, and are not operating within the legal framework that protects clinical providers and their patients. Operating outside one's training is legally dangerous to the Companion and practically dangerous to the pilgrim.
- Protection of the role. The Formation Companion role is valuable precisely because it is not therapy. Therapy has its proper place; the Companion assumes a different place. Blurring the two collapses both. The Companion offers reflective listening that enables the receiver to externalize and review thoughts, concerns, worries, and fears, as well as to examine options for faith and hope (i.e., Christian formation). A Companion can and should refer to a therapist who certainly offers what the Companion cannot.
In practical terms, a Companion introduces themselves as a trained fellow pilgrim, not as a counselor. They do not diagnose. They do not bill. They do not take clinical notes. They listen. They work within the boundaries of their specific training and refer out when the situation crosses those boundaries. They are transparent with the pilgrim about what they are and what they are not.

# The Four Contributing Traditions
The four traditions below are offered as contributing streams that inform the Formation Companion role, not as boundaries of what a Companion must or may be. Each has its own history, literature, credentialing infrastructure, and body of practice. The Companion draws on what each offers while remaining grounded in the IJH framework that governs how these contributions integrate.
## Spiritual Direction
Spiritual Direction is the oldest of the four with a lineage reaching back to Ignatius of Loyola and the Jesuit order, the Benedictine order, and the Desert Fathers. Its core posture is attending. The director and the pilgrim recipient listen together for what God is already doing in the pilgrim's life. The director's role does not prescribe or teach but notices, reflects, and invites the pilgrim's deeper attending. Spiritual Director training is typically long, often two to three years in a recognized formation program, often culminating in certification through Spiritual Directors International or a denominational equivalent. The strength of Spiritual Direction is being the most developed tradition focused on contemplative listening and the Companion's own ongoing formation. This tradition synthesizes the default posture of attending before acting, the practice of contemplative silence, and the model of director-having-a-director, i.e., everyone directs, everyone is directed, a form of mutual submission.
## Transformation Prayer Ministry
Transformation Prayer Ministry (TPM), formerly known as Theophostic, is the most protocol-specific of the four. A trained facilitator helps a person identify emotional pain tied to a specific memory, locate the lie and possible vow embedded in that memory, and pray for Jesus to reveal himself and his truth in that memory. That provides a spiritual replacement image, often manifesting as neurologic healing of thoughts and memories. Highly structured, measurable, and reproducible. This is its strength because the process is teachable, making the process consistent across facilitators. However, the process is at risk when applied mechanistically without practiced spiritual discernment. SST's Stage 3.3 describes from the inside what TPM accomplishes as a method. Training is multiplemweeks plus supervised practice, with certification available. What this tradition contributes to the synthesis: a tight protocol for memory-level healing, a clear theology of truth and the real presence of Jesus Christ as the agent of transformation, and a reproducible framework that can be taught to new facilitators without extensive clinical background.
## The Stephen Ministry Tradition
Stephen Ministry is the most widely deployed lay Christian caregiving model in North America, with curricula refined across thousands of congregations since 1975. Fifty hours of initial training, strict confidentiality, referral-based assignment to a care receiver, and a walk-alongside posture for people in grief, crisis, transition, or hardship. The interaction de-emphasizes transformation by the care giver as that is the role Holy Spirit via prayer. The process focusses on reflective listening to establish a faithful Christian presence for someone going through difficulty. Its entry-level training infrastructure is the most institutionally mature of the four, and its supervision, referral, and confidentiality depth carries up into the Companion II and III tiers — the tradition is mature across the whole progression, not only at entry. What this tradition contributes to the synthesis: the most developed curriculum for training lay people into a defined Christian care role, a robust supervision structure, and a well-tested set of boundaries around confidentiality and referral.
## Group Facilitation (Band of Brothers and Fellowship of the Heart)
The group facilitation tradition that has developed in the Band of Brothers and Fellowship of the Heart contexts is the most group-oriented and tool-diverse of the four. It draws on secular group facilitation theory, the inner healing streams, and Eldredge's framing of formation as mission and calling. This group framework model is strongest at matching process tools or key questions to a specific need expressed by someone in the group. The framework is weakest in established credentialing by Christian sources, though secular certification pathways, such as Shadow Work facilitator certification, provide a credentialed depth. This tradition contributes to synthesis through its capacity to hold the group as a container, a safe place for the confidential, heartfelt exposure of a range of emotions, sins, faults, and joys. The tradition offers a diverse inventory of processes or tools for working into and through these knot-filled aspects and finding release from accompanying harmful wounds and patterns. It also offers an explicit apprentice-to-practitioner progression developed further. The Christian re-grounding of secular men's-work that this stream represents is not unique to the Band of Brothers context: The Crucible Project, an interdenominational ministry founded in 2002, performs the same extraction, adapting the experiential men's-work tradition (in the lineage of the ManKind Project's New Warrior Training Adventure) onto an explicitly Christ-centered basis.
## A Note on the Navigators Tradition
The Navigators tradition, founded by Dawson Trotman in 1933, is less a mode of practitioner role than a method of disciple-making, and so it does not appear as a fifth mode specialization. But two contributions are sufficiently important to be named explicitly. First, its emphasis on scripture memory as a discipline, i.e., the Topical Memory System covers sixty verses across twelve topics, and the discipline is designed to internalize specific scripture so thoroughly that it becomes internalized and load-bearing in daily life. Second is the principle of reproducibility/transferability. Every method, tool, and curriculum is sufficiently simple that the person being discipled can move on to disciple another person. This is the same principle that governs Level I of the Formation Companion, tools that, once seen, can be repeated by someone else after limited training. The synthesis of the four modes in this paper assumes the Navigators' reproducibility principle at Level I and integrates its scripture-memory discipline into one’s cognitive core. More than one contribution among others, this conviction — the Word internalized until it becomes load-bearing in the Companion's own life — is the formative ground the whole synthesis rests on; it is developed as the role's developmental spine under "Hearing and Obeying" below.
# The Core Competencies — What the Four Traditions Share
When the four traditions are placed side by side, most of what the practitioner develops for each is the same for all four. Each tradition adds mode-specific competencies on top of the core, but the core itself is what every Formation Companion must develop regardless of which mode they embrace. The core uses three learning taxonomies: cognitive (Bloom), affective (Krathwohl), and psychomotor (Simpson). This is consistent with the three-taxonomy framework proposed in TA and developed in HFT and SST.
## Cognitive Core — What Every Companion Must Know
- Scripture application in depth. Not technical mastery, but deep and current familiarity. The Navigators' scripture-memory discipline is a strong means of entry; other means include long-term engagement with reflective Bible study practices, such as the PROAPT method described in HFT and MSFIG.
- How formation actually happens. Change is slow, transformative trust in God is a nonlinear process, frequently involving regression before advance, and is activated by the Holy Spirit rather than by the facilitator's technique. A Companion who expects quick linear progress will either overstate their own role or become discouraged when the pilgrim's journey does not cooperate with the Companion's schedule.
- Self-knowledge. Their own wounds, patterns, shadows, and gold. A Companion who does not know their own inner landscape of patterns, their shadows from wounds and gold, will enact and project them unconsciously on the pilgrim, usually without realizing it.
- A clear boundary between their role and licensed professional counseling. When to refer, to whom, and how to make the referral without shaming the pilgrim.
- The specific framework of whichever mode(s) they practice. A Companion practicing spiritual direction needs the Ignatian framework. A Companion practicing TPM-style work needs TPM theology and discernment. A Companion doing group facilitation needs to manage process within a container theory and have experienced a range of processes that they can draw on as tools in their inventory, each recommended for helping others see themselves, others, and God with fewer or no hindrances.
- The IJH framework itself. What TA proposes about the inner person, what HFT and SST propose about heart and soul/spirit development, and how the Affective Taxonomy stage of the Companion affects what they can and cannot do. That the four modes hold together yet can be applied differentially offers a lifelong process of developing competency in each.
## Affective Core — What Every Companion Must Be
- A Christ-centered life of their own, not borrowed from the curriculum. The Companion cannot lead the pilgrim into territory the Companion has not walked.
- Affective Level 3 minimum in trust of scripture, measured as described in HFT. A Companion at Level 2 for whom scripture is interesting but not yet personally binding cannot facilitate Level 3 work in another person because they cannot model what they have not done. This is the developmental floor of the role. It is not optional and cannot be finessed. HFT provides the operational definition.
- Humility as the agent of change. The Companion is not the agent; the Holy Spirit is. The Companion creates conditions; the Spirit does the work. Stephen Ministry names this bedrock plainly: God is the Curegiver; the Companion is only the caregiver. A Companion who forgets this becomes either overinvested in specific outcomes or subtly manipulative in pursuit of them.
- Capacity to sit with pain, silence, ambiguity, and slow process without rescuing. Rescuing is how well-meaning Companions interrupt the Spirit's work. The discipline of staying present without fixing is a foundational affective competency developed through practice and simply taught cognitively.
- Ongoing attending to their own shadows. The Companion's unexamined interior does not disappear. It can be enacted on or triggered by the pilgrim. Two distinct relationships do this work, and a Companion needs both rather than either: being companioned or directed — the peer-level, mutual-submission relationship in which the Companion is themselves a pilgrim — and supervision, the asymmetric oversight of a more-mature practitioner who can see what the Companion cannot. Together with a disciplined peer group, these are the mechanism by which the Companion keeps their own material in their own awareness rather than outside of it.
- Themselves in an ongoing formation relationship. Every Companion has a Companion. Every director has a director. This is non-negotiable. A Companion who themselves is not being companioned has stopped growing, and a Companion who has stopped growing is heading for a ditch.
## Psychomotor Core — What Every Companion Must Do
- Active listening at depth — tracking content, emotion, and Spirit-movement simultaneously. This is the most fundamental skill, the hardest, and the one that cannot be faked. Reflecting back on what was heard engenders clarity.
- Asking open questions rather than questions that require the right answers. A well-formed, open question, a non-judgmental one, opens the pilgrim's interior, and an answer often closes it, even when the answer is correct. The simplest, “Can you tell me more about that?” Most untrained Companions offload questions that require direct answers. Mature Companions ask more open questions (not requiring yes or no answers) and answer their pilgrims' questions with reflection, affirmation, or sometimes not at all.
- Comfort with silence and skillful use of silence. Silence after a pilgrim's disclosure is not empty space to be filled; it is often where the Spirit is doing the work. Learning not to fill with answers or even talking is a learned skill. Eyes can be the window to the soul.
- Real-time discernment, knowing when to intervene, when to let the moment continue, when to redirect, when to invite deeper exploration. This is the integrative competency that Level 3 of the role discusses most directly.
- Prayerful attention throughout the session. The Companion is praying inside, even while speaking; listening, even while asking. These bifurcated yet integrated connections to the Holy Spirit and to the other person are a discipline developed through practice.
- Reflection, notes, and supervision between sessions. The work does not end when the pilgrim leaves. The Companion reviews what happened, what the Spirit seemed to be doing, what the Companion themselves was doing that helped or hindered, and brings the results of that reflection into their own supervision.
- Confidentiality, held with care and seriousness, though not on the same legal footing as a licensed clinician's. A lay Companion's reporting duties and legal protections differ by jurisdiction, so each Companion should know the local requirements and obtain legal guidance rather than assume a clinician's privilege. The limits of confidentiality — including the exceptions the law may require, such as a credible risk of harm to self or others — are named to the pilgrim at the first meeting, before disclosure, as a plain confidentiality covenant, not discovered afterward. And because the Companion brings their work into supervision, that material is anonymized and the supervision group is itself bound, so that "non-negotiable confidentiality" and "bring the case to supervision" are reconciled rather than in tension.
These are the common core binding together all four traditions. A Companion who has developed all of the above is most of the way to being functional in any of the four modes. What each tradition adds on top of the core is mode-specific and less extensive than the core itself.
# The Three Levels of Companion Development
Companion development is proposed as a three-level progression. The levels are cumulative: Companion II includes Companion I, and Companion III includes both. Each level represents substantial growth in all three taxonomy domains, not just one. The levels are deliberately named so that no level sounds like or remains as a permanent station. A Companion at Level I is not lesser; he or she is at the beginning of a long journey that a lifetime may or may not complete.
## Companion I — Protocol Competent
The Level I Companion can engage simple, repeatable tools and practices with fidelity. The tools at this level have been selected because they are sufficiently robust to produce real transformation and simple enough to be reproduced by anyone who has seen them well-led and received competent training. This is the Navigators' memory-incentivized reproducibility principle applied to formation tools. A new Fellowship of the Heart can be sustained for a long time by a group of Level I Companions alone, if they use the tools well and continue to grow. Some Companions will remain at Level I for their entire life of service, and that is honorable.
The Affective Taxonomy floor for Level I is Stage 3 — the scripture-is-personally-binding threshold from HFT. A Companion below Stage 3 is not ready to companion at Level I, even if they have learned the protocols. They can still be in the room, still be growing, still be part of a FotH group, but they should not be the ones leading the protocol.
## Companion II — Mode Practitioner
The Level II Companion has developed at least one mode specialization at working depth, i.e., Spiritual Direction, TPM-style work, Stephen-style care, or deep group process facilitation beyond the Level I protocols. This requires formal training in that mode (typically through a credentialing pathway, see below), sustained supervised practice, and integration of the mode's specific tools into the Companion's own discernment. A Level II Companion is also able to lead from behind, to help a pilgrim own their own next steps rather than prescribing the path.
The Affective Taxonomy floor for Level II is Stage 4, the values-integrated threshold, where the Companion's own life has been substantially reorganized around scripture and the Companion can model what that reorganization looks like from the inside, not just describe it from the outside. Level II work pulls on whatever unfinished business the Companion holds, and a Stage 3 Companion will not have the integration to hold the weight.
## Companion III — Integrated Practitioner
The Level III Companion has two or more mode specializations at working depth and has developed the capstone competency center for the interest of this paper: the real-time discernment of which mode a given moment requires, and the capacity to move between modes without losing grounding. The Level III Companion can also train other Companions. This can include certifying Level I, mentoring Level II toward Level III, and helping the Council (or other oversight body) assess whether a particular Companion is ready for a given level.
The Affective Taxonomy floor for Level III is Stage 4 or higher, and on the journey to Stage 5 over time. Stage 5 is the characterization stage, where the Companion's whole life has become coherent around their formation, and the modeling effect on others has become largely unconscious because it is simply who the Companion is. Few Companions reach Stage 5. For some, that is as it should be.
## The 3 × 3 Development Matrix
The three taxonomies crossed with the three levels produce a 3 × 3 matrix that makes the developmental progression specific. Each cell names what a Companion at that level must have developed along that taxonomy dimension. The matrix is read horizontally (what develops across levels within one domain) and vertically (what develops at each level across all three domains). The affective row functions as a developmental prerequisite or gate — the maturity floor that must be in place for the cognitive and psychomotor competencies to be exercised safely — rather than a third, co-equal skill set.

|  | Companion I (Protocol Competent) | Companion II (Mode Practitioner) | Companion III (Integrated Practitioner) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cognitive (what they know) | Scripture basics and ongoing memory work; IJH framework at overview level; tool theory for Level I protocols; adult learning fundamentals; container-forming theory; referral criteria for licensed care. | Deeper scripture engagement; full IJH framework, including TA/HFT/SST/MSFIG; tool theory for Level II mode work; contraindications for their mode; legal and ethical edges of their specific mode. | Integrated theological vision across modes; teaching-of-teachers competence; full legal and ethical boundary knowledge; capacity to evaluate and integrate new tools against the framework using Powlison/Trentham discernment. |
| Affective (what they value and are) | Affective Stage 3 minimum; scripture personally binding; honest self-awareness of their own journey; willingness to sit with others' pain without rescuing; love of people rather than role. | Affective Stage 4: value system reorganized around scripture; can hold own shadows without enacting them; reverent patience with the Spirit's pace; integrated ownership of the mode's work. | Affective Stage 4+ moving toward Stage 5; whole-life coherence; models what they teach by being it; forms others characterologically by presence more than by program. |
| Psychomotor (what they can do) | Run Level I protocols with fidelity (Social Contract, Clearings, Tell Your Story, Heart Bible Study, Hot Seat, Mission Clarity, etc.); form a safe container; active listening at working depth. | Lead mode-specific deep process work (Guts facilitation, Voice Dialogue, Inner Healing, or equivalent in other modes); lead from behind; match tool to moment within their mode. | Exercise proactive but Spirit-led discernment within ethical limits — knowing when to act and, as often, when to stay out of the way so that the Spirit, not the Companion, does the work (in TPM-style work especially, the operative skill is restraint: letting Jesus reveal the truth rather than the Companion supplying it); mode-switch in real time as the moment calls for; train and certify other Companions; develop or adapt tools against the framework. |

Of the Companion III psychomotor competencies above, all but one are certified against observable evidence (supervised case presentation, reviewed sessions, a track record of sound referrals and mode-shifts); real-time mode-switching itself is the exception — recognized over time through reflective case review rather than scored to a checklist, as described under The Level III Mode-Switching Capstone below.

# Mode Specializations
Each of the four modes adds specific competencies on top of the common core. What follows is a brief description of each mode's specialized content. This paper does not attempt to be a training curriculum for any of the four, as each has its own literature and training infrastructure, and no single paper could replace them. The intent here is to show what each mode contributes to the Formation Companion role as a specialization.
## Spiritual Direction Mode
The Spiritual Direction mode specialization is defined primarily by its posture of attending and its rhythm of long-term, infrequent, unhurried meetings (typically monthly, sometimes quarterly). The added competencies are contemplative listening (listening for what God is doing under what the directee is saying), the use of silence as an active practice rather than a pause, the specific questions of the Ignatian tradition (what consolation, what desolation, what is God inviting), and a light touch that invites without prescribing. The training pathway is typically a two- or three-year program through a Spiritual Directors International-recognized institution. The Companion operating in SD mode typically meets with a small number of directees over a long-time horizon, often years.
## TPM-Style Mode
The TPM-style specialization is the most protocol-specific and the most narrowly targeted. The added competencies are the identification of emotional pain tied to specific memories, the discernment of the lie embedded in each memory, the framing of prayer for Jesus to reveal truth into the memory, and the handling of resistance and multi-session work on recurring patterns. The training pathway is TPM's own facilitator certification, or equivalent training in related memory-healing modalities (Theophostic, Restoring the Foundations, Sozo, Leanne Payne). The Companion operating in TPM-style mode typically works one-on-one on specific targeted sessions rather than ongoing long-term companioning. Because this mode is so protocol-driven, its characteristic failure is to let the protocol become the authority — the facilitator driving toward an outcome. The governing move is the reverse: the facilitator only opens the door, and it is Jesus, not the protocol, who reveals the truth and heals. Here too the aim is the through-line of the whole role — to help the pilgrim hear from God for themselves, not to be told what to see.
## Stephen-Style Mode
The Stephen-style specialization is the mode of walking alongside a pilgrim through an extended season of crisis, loss, transition, or hardship. The added competencies are the discipline of Christian presence without an agenda, the handling of grief and acute distress, the management of long-term low-intensity relationships that can span years, and the institutional practices around assignment, confidentiality, and supervision that Stephen Ministry has developed. Training is through Stephen Ministry's own fifty-hour curriculum or equivalent. The Companion operating in Stephen-style mode typically works one-on-one with a care receiver through a defined season, not indefinitely.
## Group Mode (Band of Brothers / Fellowship of the Heart)
The group mode specialization is the most tool-diverse and, in part, the most dynamically complex, due in part to multiple participants. The added competencies are as follows:
- container theory and processes, i.e., how to form a group in which the Spirit's work can unfold safely;
- the full Level I tool set listed in the tool inventory below;
- the Level II deep-process tools (Guts facilitation, Voice Dialogue, Inner Healing, Paradox Holding, and others), reading group dynamics in real time; and
- the capacity to hold the group's formation at the same time as each individual's.
Training is through apprenticeship in an existing group, plus formal training in specific deep-process methodologies. The Companion operating in group mode typically leads a group of six to twelve pilgrims over months to years, with the group itself being the primary unit of formation. The characteristic failure of the group mode is the facilitator who drives the group toward a predetermined result, or who makes the tools the point. The governing move keeps every tool in service of the pilgrims' own hearing: the facilitator opens space for each person to hear from God and for the group to discern together, never letting the process stand in for the Spirit at work in it.
# The Level III Mode-Switching Capstone
The capstone competency of Level III is the real-time discernment of which mode a given moment calls for, and the capacity to move between modes without losing grounding. This is the competency that makes the synthesis framing of this paper matter in practice. A Companion who has only one mode is not a Formation Companion, as this paper defines the role; they are a specialist in that mode. A Formation Companion at full maturity reads what the moment is and responds with the mode that serves the moment. One mode, though, is not really among the things being switched: the contemplative posture of attending — listening for what God is doing beneath what is said — is the always-on ground of the whole role, carried in the common core every Companion must be, and it is never switched off. Spiritual direction names both that posture and the long-horizon, directive-light relationship built on it; the other three modes are the discrete kinds of work a Companion takes up, and sets down, within it. "Mode-switching," then, is the rise and fall of those tool-acts on a steady contemplative ground, not the trading of one whole stance for another ([V2.Exp0B](../volume-2-knowing-to-doing/exploration-0b-contemplative-substrate.md) treats this attending-as-substrate at length).
A few illustrations make this concrete. A pilgrim arrives for what was scheduled as a spiritual direction session and discloses a childhood memory that is clearly active, unresolved, and asking for attention. Here the immature move — and the dangerous one — is to plunge into TPM-style memory work on the spot. A mature Companion does the opposite: they slow the session down, stay present, and help the pilgrim feel held rather than opened further. A memory surfacing with that much charge is precisely the moment the "This Is Not Therapy" boundary exists to govern. The Companion discerns, without hurrying, between a memory the pilgrim is steady enough to bring to prayer — which can be named now and contracted for explicitly, with the pilgrim's consent and the proper setup, often in a dedicated later session — and a trauma surfacing beyond what a non-licensed Companion can competently hold, which calls for steadying the pilgrim and referring to a licensed clinician. Memory-level work is never entered reactively, mid-session, on the strength of a single disclosure; entering it carries its own screening and setup that no mode-switch can compress away. A pilgrim in a group session who is in acute grief over a loss the group did not know about. A mature Companion can hold the group's work in suspension and shift into Stephen-style presence with that pilgrim, bringing the group back in at the right moment. If a group is plateauing in their formation work, a mature Companion can recognize that what is needed is not a new tool but a season of simpler, spiritual-direction-style attending to what God has been doing that the group has not noticed.
One direction of mode-switching is governed by a rule that admits no exceptions: a Companion never switches toward trauma on the fly. When unresolved trauma surfaces, or when a pilgrim begins to dysregulate, that is a contraindication for going deeper — not an invitation. The safe in-the-moment moves are always toward presence and steadying, and, when the moment calls for it, toward referral. The one mode-switch a Companion may make without hesitation is the move out to licensed care; the move further into memory or deep-process work always waits for screening, consent, and setup, and a lay Companion must never improvise it. More generally, when it is unclear which mode a moment calls for, the Companion defaults to the gentler, less invasive mode; and before shifting into any deeper work, the Companion names the shift to the pilgrim and asks their consent — a brief re-contracting that keeps the pilgrim, not the Companion's read of the moment, in charge of how far the session goes.

Mode-switching requires three things that cannot be acquired separately. First, it requires genuine competence in more than one mode, which is why it is a Level III competency and not available earlier. Second, it requires enough integration in the Companion's own formation that they can hold the different modes as complementary rather than competing. Third, it requires a nudging from the Holy Spirit. A Companion whose own spiritual direction and TPM training have never been integrated may tend to pick whichever one they are more comfortable with, rather than whichever one the moment calls for. Integration is the Stage 4 to Stage 5 movement of the Companion's own Affective Taxonomy journey and experience. It cannot be reached through training alone.

How, then, is Level III assessed? The partition matters. The demonstrable competencies are certified — protocol fidelity in each mode the Companion practices, mode-specific skill, sound referral judgment, and the habit of naming each mode-switch afterward in supervision; these can be observed, evidenced, and scored. The integrative capstone itself — reading the moment and moving between modes without losing grounding — is not scored to a checklist. It is recognized over time, attested by the Council or a senior Companion through reflective case review, the way the older traditions have always recognized maturity rather than tested for it. And because a genuine nudging of the Spirit can be confused with the Companion's own preference, the discipline that tells them apart is built into that review: a true prompting can be named, submitted to a more mature Companion, and weighed against fruit and Scripture, while a drift toward whichever mode the Companion simply finds most comfortable tends to show up, across a series of cases, as a pattern. What cannot be certified is held accountable instead.
Referral can be considered as a mode. The most important mode-switch a Companion can make is not between the four Christian care modes but between companioning and referral to licensed professional care. A Companion recognizes when the moment calls for a move outside the Companion's scope entirely and has the clarity to make that referral without hesitation or self-judgment. Referral is not a failure of companioning; it is a competency of companioning. The pilgrim whose situation requires a clinician is best served by a Companion who knows it and says so.
# On the Discerning Use of Non-Christian-Grounded Methodologies
Several of the tools and methods that appear in Companion practice have origins outside the Christian tradition. For example, Shadow Work is from the Jungian and mythopoetic lineages, elements of Voice Dialogue in Hal and Sidra Stone's Psychology of Selves, the somatic practices underlying Guts facilitation, and Kahneman's System 1 / System 2 framework. The question of how these methodologies should be evaluated and used by a Christian Formation Companion warrants explicit treatment rather than a case-by-case approach. It is worth saying at the outset what this discipline does and does not govern: it governs the imported tools that the group-facilitation mode draws on, not the Christian-originated modes themselves. Spiritual Direction, TPM, and Stephen-style care rest on Christian theology and a Christian account of who heals — in TPM especially, Jesus, not the technique, is the agent — and so are not "non-Christian methodologies" to be filtered.
TA proposed two discernment protocols for exactly this purpose: Powlison's three-step epistemological priorities and Trentham's four-step reading of the social sciences. Both are worth rehearsing here in the context of Companion practice. Powlison's protocol: (1) ground the topic in scriptural truth first, typically through systematic theology; (2) analyze the secular model critically, exposing and assessing its underlying assumptions; (3) extract valuable truths from the model while leaving the assumptions that conflict with scripture. Trentham's protocol is similar in shape: (1) establish the doctrinal base; (2) read the secular research with an open rather than automatically critical posture; (3) apply reflective discernment; (4) apply what is genuinely useful.
This can be applied to Shadow Work, for example. Shadow Work offers a genuinely useful method for naming and honoring the reality of the inner division that scripture calls the old self, the false self, and the flesh. Its practice of bringing disowned material into conscious relationship maps well onto what Vol 2 calls identifying the wound and the false self, and onto SST's Stage 2 of the soul. These methodological contributions are usable. What Shadow Work does not provide, and what the Christian Companion must bring from elsewhere, is the naming of sin as sin, the recognition that some shadow material is fallen nature requiring repentance rather than integration, the identification of the Holy Spirit rather than the integrating self as the agent of transformation, and the refusal of the Jungian metaphysics that treats the collective unconscious as if it were theologically neutral. The Companion who uses Shadow Work tools well does so within a Christian framework they bring to the work, rather than within the Jungian framework that originally produced them.
As a general rule, the Companion is free to use methods whose origins are not Christian, provided the Companion brings a Christian framework to govern how the methods are used, names explicitly what the methods do and do not supply theologically, and is willing to leave behind elements of the original framework that conflict with scripture. The Companion is not free to use methods as if the framework does not matter, or to pretend that neutral-framework methodologies are theologically neutral. Method is never neutral; method always assumes an answer to what the person is, how change happens, and who the agent is. The Christian Companion holds those answers and chooses methods accordingly.

Two cautions keep this rule honest. The first is that for some methods the frame is not merely a backdrop but the mechanism — the thing that makes the method work. Take Voice Dialogue: part of its power comes from treating each inner "self" as an autonomous voice to be honored and given its say. A Companion can adapt the practice of letting an unspoken part of the self be heard; but the moment the Companion names one of those parts as sin to be repented of rather than a voice to be honored, the move has changed in kind. That may be exactly the right pastoral move — yet it is no longer Voice Dialogue used within a Christian framework; it is a new practice that has to prove itself on its own terms and carries its own risks (done clumsily mid-disclosure, it can shut down the very opening it created and leave the pilgrim ashamed). Honesty requires naming it as such, rather than claiming the borrowed method's credibility for something its originators would not recognize.

The second caution is that the rule has an edge it will not cross. Not every method can be redeemed by reframing. Where a practice is constituted by contacting or submitting to spiritual agencies other than the Triune God — mediumship, necromancy, the invocation of guides or deities, or any technique whose efficacy depends on a power Scripture forbids — there is nothing to extract: the method is refused, not reframed (Deut 18:10-12; 1 Cor 10:20-21). Refused too is any method that can be used only by setting aside the sufficiency of Christ or the authority of Scripture. "Use any method, provided you bring a Christian framework" is therefore not unlimited; sometimes the framework returns the verdict no.

# Tool Inventory — First Pass
The tool inventory below is a first pass adaptation of the Weapons Master paper's tool list for the mixed-gender Fellowship of the Heart context. It is presented as a draft for review and workshop testing rather than a finished curriculum. Some tools are kept as-is because their names and content are already gender-neutral; some have naming notes where the current name carries masculine framing even though the underlying work is universal. Some have framing notes where the tool is usable across genders but should be led with explicit attention to how it will land differently. This section assumes ongoing workshopping with practitioners rather than settlement in a single pass. Two cautions govern the renaming itself. First, a renamed or softened tool must still do the work the original did: changing "Guts Commando" to a gentler name, or reframing "shadow," is legitimate only if the substance and intensity that made the tool effective are preserved — a sanitized name over a de-fanged process helps no one, so the workshop test for each renamed tool is whether it still produces the change the original produced. Second, the tier placement of the depth tools is itself open: Shadow Mission, for example, draws on depth-psychology material, and whether it belongs at the reproducible Level I tier or among the Level II deep-process tools is a question for practitioners to settle rather than a settled call.
## Level I Tools
These are the protocols a Companion I can lead with fidelity after having seen them well-led and received the training. They are simple enough to be reproducible and rich enough to produce real transformation. This is the Navigators' reproducibility principle in practice. Detailed protocols will be provided later.
- Social Contract — Keep as-is. Gender-neutral in name and content.
- Container Forming — Keep as-is. Gender-neutral.
- Clearings — Keep as-is. Gender-neutral.
- Tell Your Story — Keep as-is. Gender-neutral. Note: the specific invitations within the process may need to be framed to honor that stories from women's and men's lives often turn on different formative experiences; the tool itself works the same way.
- Who Are You — Keep as-is. The question of identity is universal.
- Heart Bible Study — Keep as-is. The PROAPT method described in HFT is gender-neutral in design.
- Shadow Mission — Keep, with note. The term "shadow" is borrowed from Jungian psychology; the tool itself helps a pilgrim identify the inverse of their true mission — the life they are tempted to live instead of the one God is calling them to. Framing note: Shadow missions are as real for women as for men; the content of shadow missions tends to differ in gendered ways, which the Companion should be aware of without scripting.
- Mission Clarity and Stretch — Keep as-is. Universal; applied identically.
- Hot Seat — Keep the name. The term has a masculine-coded history in the men's work movement, but it is descriptive (the pilgrim is in the hot seat of the group's focused attention) and is widely used in group facilitation contexts for mixed genders. Note: the Companion leading must explicitly hold safety for the person in the seat in a gender-aware way.
- What's at Risk — Keep as-is.
- Want-Resist — Keep as-is. The internal tension the tool works with is universal.
- Group Mission — Keep as-is.
- Heart Prayer Process — Keep as-is.
- Council of Elders — Review. The phrase council of elders carries a masculine connotation in some Christian traditions; in others, it is gender-neutral. Options: keep as-is with framing, rename to Wisdom Council, or provide both names and let the specific FotH choose.
- 7 × 7 — Keep as-is (assuming it is the specific process named in the Weapons Master paper).
- Kingdom of God — Keep as-is.
- Mini Hot Seat — Same notes as Hot Seat.
## Level II Processes
These are the deep-process tools that require formal training, skills practice, and supervised mentorship before a Companion II can lead them. They are not list items for Companion I to attempt after reading a description.

Because these tools work at depth, they carry their own hazards — abreaction, dissociation, decompensation, and, in memory work, the risk of confabulated or false memory. Three disciplines therefore govern every tool on this list, ahead of any of the gender-and-framing notes that follow. First, stabilization comes before depth: a pilgrim who is acutely dysregulated, in crisis, or simply not steady enough to stay present is not a candidate for deep-process work in that moment, and forcing it does harm. Second, every Companion II carries a mid-process protocol for dysregulation — if a pilgrim begins to flood or dissociate, the move is to stop, ground, and return them to the present, never to push through, and to refer when the depth exceeds what a non-licensed Companion can hold. Third, the screening criteria and contraindications for each tool should be worked out with, and vetted by, a trauma-informed licensed clinician before the tool is used in a FotH, not improvised by the facilitator. These are not optional refinements of the tools below; they are the conditions under which a lay Companion may use any of them at all.
- Guts Facilitation — Keep the name with framing. Guts, as a term, has masculine-coded history in the men's work movement, but is descriptive of somatic emotional processing and has gender-neutral versions in practice. The Companion leading Guts work with mixed-gender groups should have received explicit training in how the work lands differently for women and men and how to hold the container accordingly.
- Shadow Work Version — Keep as-is. Bilgere's work has mixed-gender applications that are well developed.
- Dmitri's Version / Guts Commando — Rename needed. Commando is masculine-coded and martial in a way that does not carry into FotH. Propose: Dmitri's Intensive Guts Process, or Dmitri's Variant, or similar.
- Voice Dialogue — Keep as-is. Stone and Stone's Psychology of Selves is gender-neutral in design.
- Paradox Holding — Keep as-is.
- Big Mind Process — Keep as-is, with note that the Big Mind framework has non-Christian metaphysical roots (Zen Buddhist) and should be used with the same discerning framework that applies to Shadow Work above. Worked through that filter: its practice of giving voice to disowned aspects of the self can surface real material, but its underlying non-dual metaphysic — in which the distinctions between self, world, and the absolute finally dissolve — cannot be imported, because it contradicts the Christian account of a personal God distinct from his creatures and of a self that stands before him. Use the method's capacity to surface; refuse its metaphysic.
- Inner Healing — Keep as-is. The inner healing tradition (Leanne Payne, Rita Bennett — already in Vol 5) is mixed-gender in origin and application.
- Restoring the Foundations (RTF) — Keep as-is. Chester and Betsy Kylstra's ministry is mixed-gender in design.
- Emotionally Free — Keep as-is.
# Hearing and Obeying — The Developmental Spine
One move from the original Weapons Master paper requires explicit correction in this paper: the original treated scriptural engagement and hearing-and-obeying God as prerequisites (checkbox items the Companion either has or does not have). Scriptural engagement and hearing-and-obeying, however, are not prerequisites; they are the developmental spine of the Companion role, and they continue to develop across all three levels of Companion maturity.
Operationally, this means the Companion's own Affective Taxonomy stage, as defined in HFT, is not a one-time checkpoint but a continuously developing variable that governs what the Companion can and cannot do. A Companion at Stage 3 can Companion I. A Companion at Stage 4 can become a Companion II. A Companion moving toward Stage 5 is approaching Companion III maturity. The stage is not an honorific; it is a working measure of the Companion's capacity to model what they lead. But it is a discerned measure, not a self-certified one. As MSM cautions, the affective stages are not cleanly self-distinguishable from the inside — an immature Stage 2 and a mature Stage 5 can look much alike to the person living them, and Scripture warns that the heart is not transparent to itself (Jer 17:9). So above the Level I floor, a Companion's stage is a maturity judgment held in community — named by a mentor or the Council and tested against the fruit of a life — rather than something the Companion scores on themselves. Two further cautions keep the stage in its place. It is a necessary floor, not a sufficient one: meeting the Stage 3 or Stage 4 threshold does not by itself confer the mode-specific skill a level requires, and demonstrated skill does not by itself confer the stage — both the affective floor and the psychomotor competence must be present. And affective maturity is not the same thing as the clinical competence required to hold trauma safely; a Stage 4 Companion can still be the wrong person to sit with a severely traumatized pilgrim, which is why the referral discipline, not the stage, governs that boundary.
HFT also describes the PROAPT method as the primary practice for moving the Companion through their own stage progression. A Companion who is not themselves engaged in PROAPT (or an equivalent disciplined scriptural practice) has stopped developing, and their capacity to companion others will begin to erode even if their skills-level competencies remain. The Companion's own PROAPT practice is not a tool they teach; it is the ground they walk. The Council's ongoing testing work (Vol 4) treats the Companion's own Affective Taxonomy stage as an explicit independent variable for exactly this reason.
Practical corollary: every Companion has their own PROAPT practice, their own being-companioned relationship (spiritual direction), supervision of their active work, and a peer accountability group. While carrying active assignments, that supervision should follow the Stephen-Ministry pattern: a regular cadence, a designated supervisor more mature in the Companion's mode, and a confidential peer-presentation group in which cases are brought, anonymized, for review. The most senior Companions most need the mutual, being-companioned relationship precisely because they have the least oversight above them. These together form the minimum scaffolding that keeps the Companion developing. A Companion missing any one of them is vulnerable to drift — not because their training has failed, but because formation development does not sustain without ongoing practice.
# Recognized Credentialing Pathways
The Formation Companion role benefits from but is not defined by formal credentialing. A Companion I operating in group mode within a FotH may be trained entirely through the FotH's own internal apprenticeship, without external credentialing, and still serve well. A Companion II or III typically has at least one external credential in their primary mode, because the training depth required to practice that mode at working depth exceeds what internal training can usually provide.
The recognized credentialing pathways, by mode:
### Spiritual Direction
- Two- and three-year programs accredited by Spiritual Directors International (SDI) or equivalent institutional recognition. Examples: Shalem Institute, Spiritual Formation Academy, Oasis Ministries, Christos Center, denominational programs (Jesuit, Benedictine, Catholic seminary-based; various Protestant equivalents).
### Transformation Prayer Ministry and Related Memory-Healing Modalities
- TPM's own facilitator certification through the Aslan Group; Theophostic training (historical predecessor); Sozo training (Bethel); Restoring the Foundations facilitator certification through the Kylstra ministries.
### Stephen-Style Lay Care
- Stephen Ministry's formal Leader's Training Course (one week residential) and ongoing local training for Stephen Ministers (fifty hours).
### Group Mode Deep-Process Work
- Shadow Work facilitator certification through Shadow Work Seminars (recognized as a non-Christian-grounded methodology to be used within a Christian framework per the discerning-use section above); inner healing training through schools such as Pastoral Care Ministries (Leanne Payne), Elijah House, or equivalent; group facilitation training through secular programs (e.g., Institute of Cultural Affairs) for the container-forming and group-dynamics competencies.
No single pathway is required. What is required is that a Companion II or III has received training at working depth in at least one of these pathways for their primary mode and is in ongoing supervision with a practitioner more mature than themselves.
# The Four Connects as the Companion's Own Formation
The Companion is themselves a pilgrim. The Four Connects architecture described in Vol 2 applies to the Companion's own life as much as to any pilgrim they serve. The Companion who is not connecting with self cannot help another connect with self. The Companion who is not connecting with God in an ongoing and honest way cannot help another connect with God. The Companion who is isolated from a real community of peers cannot model the Connect-with-Others axis they are trying to facilitate in others. The Companion who has no mission beyond companioning has stopped becoming.
This is why the Companion's own formation is not an extra — it is the ground of the role. A Companion whose practice has become disconnected from their ongoing Four Connects journey is practicing from diminishing capital rather than growing capacity. That practice will eventually show, either in burnout, in subtle projection onto the pilgrim, or in the Companion plateauing at whatever level of formation they had reached when they started. The Companion's own ongoing Intentional Journey is the non-negotiable condition of the role's sustainability.

# Conclusion
The Formation Companion is a synthesis role, now defined as a category in this paper, though the reality of its names has existed as long as believers have walked with one another. Four spiritual-care traditions — three of them established Christian traditions (Spiritual Direction, Transformation Prayer Ministry, Stephen Ministry) and the fourth a Christian-framed group facilitation, developed in the Band of Brothers and Fellowship of the Heart contexts, that disciplines secular group-work and depth-psychology tools within a Christian framework — have developed mostly independently of one another for decades or centuries. When laid side by side, they converge on a substantial common core and theme. That core centers on the essence of the Formation Companion role. The mode specializations are what make the role operational in a given moment. The capstone competency is the real-time discernment of which mode serves the moment.
This paper provides a starting framework, i.e., a vocabulary, structure, and a first pass at an architecture that can withstand testing, argument, and refinement, and settle into a structure that the Fellowship of the Heart can actually use. The Formation Companion role has always existed, and this paper is a first attempt to name what has always been there so that training for it can be done intentionally rather than disparately.
The author holds this paper at Speculative confidence as a first draft with forthcoming substantial revisions. There are likely tools, nuances, and modes missed. The synthesis framing is new and has not yet been tested. Further, the three-taxonomy 3 × 3 matrix is a structural proposal that needs to be workshopped with practitioners before becoming a training curriculum. The tool inventory adaptations need the tacit judgment that only years of practice can supply. The character prerequisites section requires additional work beyond this paper, which likely means the next version will be significantly different. In sum, the wider Laws of the Spirit that motivates this paper has always been an open exploration. This paper is explicitly an invitation to the next round of that exploration.
