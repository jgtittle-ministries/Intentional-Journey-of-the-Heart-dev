# Appendix: TFT Tested Against the History of Philosophy

*Read this after [TFT Challenged](appendix-tft-challenged.md). That appendix tested Transcendental Field Theory's method. This one tests its lineage: where the claim stands in the long history of thought about the true, the good, and the beautiful. It reports what that history says, and then answers it. The answers are given as a Christian theist would give them, because that is what this volume is.*

[Source document (pdf) ↗](source-pdfs/tft-tested-against-history-source.pdf){: .md-button .md-button--primary .pdf-popup data-pdf-label="TFT — Tested Against History (source)" }

## Why a second test

The first test asked whether TFT's mathematics does honest work. A review panel tried to break it, and the [Analogy Boundary Principle](appendix-tft-challenged.md#the-analogy-boundary-principle) came out of what survived.

This second test asks a different question. Philosophers have argued about truth, goodness, and beauty for some 2,400 years. Does TFT stand inside that conversation, or outside it? Which of the old objections does it already meet, and which does it still owe an answer?

The source document above is an AI-generated assessment, written against an early draft of TFT. That draft still described truth, goodness, and beauty as real, quantifiable *fields*. Volume 3 has since withdrawn that claim. So the assessment was arguing against a stronger theory than the one this volume now holds.

That turns out to be useful. Its verdict lands almost exactly where the review panel's did, reached from the history rather than from the method: **thinkable as analogy, unwarranted as physics.** Two independent tests agreeing is worth more than either alone.

One caution about the source. Its citations are website names, not references, and several are unreliable. Every quotation in this appendix has been checked against the primary text or a standard reference work; the sources are listed at the end. Quote from this appendix, not from the source document.

## What the history says

### The classical tradition: real, and one

The ancient and medieval tradition is on TFT's side about one thing and against it about another.

It is on TFT's side that truth, goodness, and beauty are **real**. For Plato, Beauty itself is "everlasting, not growing and decaying," and beautiful things are beautiful by sharing in it (*Symposium* 210e–211b). The Good is the source of the being and knowability of everything else, as the sun is the source of sight; it "far exceeds essence in dignity and power" (*Republic* 508b–509b). Plotinus held that "all the loveliness of this world comes by communion in Ideal-Form," and that ugliness is what form has not mastered (*Enneads* I.6.2). Aristotle brought beauty down to earth, without making it any less real: "beauty depends on magnitude and order" (*Poetics* 7, 1450b). And Thomas Aquinas taught that "every being, as being, is good" (*Summa Theologiae* I q.5 a.3), and that every being is likewise true (ST I q.16 a.3).

It is against TFT that the three are **separate things**. For Aquinas, "goodness and being are really the same, and differ only in idea" (ST I q.5 a.1). The beautiful and the good are "identical fundamentally". They differ only in how we meet them: the good answers to desire, while "beauty relates to the cognitive faculty; for beautiful things are those which please when seen" (ST I q.5 a.4 ad 1). Nothing real is added to being by calling it true or good: "as good adds to being the notion of desirable, so the true adds relation to the intellect" (ST I q.16 a.3), a relation "which is merely conceptual" (*De Veritate* q.21 a.1). Truth, goodness, and beauty are one reality, distinguished by the ways we receive it.

That is the tradition's sharpest question for TFT, and it gets a section of its own below.

### The modern objections

Modern philosophy kept truth largely objective and grew doubtful about the other two.

- **David Hume** observed that writers on morals pass, without noticing, from what *is* to what *ought* to be. The shift is "imperceptible", he wrote, but "of the last consequence", and it needs explaining (*Treatise* 3.1.1.27).
- **G. E. Moore** held that "'good' is a simple notion, just as 'yellow' is a simple notion", and so cannot be defined in natural terms (*Principia Ethica*, 1903, §§7, 14). He called the attempt the *naturalistic fallacy* (§10).
- **J. L. Mackie** argued that objective values, if they existed, would be "of a very strange sort", unlike anything else in the universe. Knowing them would need "some special faculty of moral perception or intuition" (*Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong*, 1977, p. 38). He concluded that there are none.
- **Immanuel Kant** held that a judgment of beauty rests on a ground that "can be no other than subjective", yet claims everyone's agreement (*Critique of Judgment* §§1, 6–8). Beauty, on this view, is not a property of things in the way that mass is.

Against a theory of *goodness fields* and *beauty fields*, these objections are decisive. Against the theory Volume 3 now holds, most of them have already been met, or can be answered. The scorecard below sorts them.

### Earlier attempts to put numbers on value

TFT is not the first attempt to measure the good and the beautiful, and the earlier ones are instructive.

- **Jeremy Bentham's** calculus of pleasure and pain, later called the felicific calculus (*An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, printed 1780, published 1789, ch. IV), scored an act's pleasures and pains by intensity, duration, certainty, nearness, fruitfulness, purity, and extent, and summed them. It foundered on the question of what the numbers measure.
- **George Birkhoff's** *Aesthetic Measure* (1933) proposed that beauty is order divided by complexity, *M = O/C*. It captured something real about simplicity and richness, and missed meaning, context, and novelty.
- **Kenneth Arrow** proved that no rule can combine individual rankings into a group ranking while meeting a few reasonable fairness conditions at once (*Social Choice and Individual Values*, 1951; Arrow called it the General Possibility Theorem). Value may not add up into a single currency.

On the other side of the ledger, the physicist **Paul Dirac** wrote that "it is more important to have beauty in one's equations than to have them fit experiment" (*Scientific American*, May 1963). Werner Heisenberg, in a 1970 lecture, recalled an old Latin motto: *pulchritudo splendor veritatis*, beauty is the splendour of truth. Their experience is a witness, though not a proof, that truth and beauty are bound together in the world God made.

## Volume 3's answers

### To Mackie: the strangeness points to God

Mackie is right that objective goodness would be strange in a universe of matter alone. A world of particles has no place for a quality that carries its own "ought". If the world were only that, he would be right to give goodness up.

But the Christian does not say goodness floats free in such a world. Goodness is grounded in a Person. God is good (Mark 10:18; Ps. 100:5), and created things are good by bearing the stamp of His goodness (Gen. 1:31). The philosopher Robert Adams develops this at length: God is the Good itself, and finite things are good by resembling Him (*Finite and Infinite Goods*, 1999).

On that account, the strangeness Mackie found is not a reason to deny objective value. It is a clue to where value comes from. Mackie himself saw the force of this. In *The Miracle of Theism* (1982, pp. 115–16) he granted that if there were objective, intrinsically prescriptive values, "they make the existence of a god more probable than it would have been without them". He rejected the values in order to keep the conclusion away. This volume accepts the values, and with them the conclusion.

Mackie's second worry was the "special faculty" needed to know such values. Scripture names it: the law written on the heart and the conscience bearing witness (Rom. 2:14–15), and, beyond conscience, the Spirit who guides into truth (John 16:13). Philosophers call the account that follows from this *Reformed epistemology*. Calvin held that every human mind carries a sense of the divine, the *sensus divinitatis* (*Institutes* I.3.1). Alvin Plantinga argues from it that belief in God can be *properly basic*, held without argument as memory and perception are, when it comes from faculties working as God designed them (*Warranted Christian Belief*, 2000). Mackie's "special faculty" is, on this view, not an embarrassment to be explained away. It is part of how we were made. That this faculty is real, and also damaged, is exactly what Volumes 1 and 2 claim and test.

### To Hume and Moore: the *is* was never value-free

Hume's gap is real when the *is* is a bare fact about matter. Nothing about the arrangement of atoms, by itself, yields an obligation. Moore was right too that "good" cannot be defined as pleasure, or survival, or any other natural quantity.

The Christian claim is different. The world is not a bare *is*. It is a creation, made by a good God for a purpose, and called good by Him. Its facts already carry His intention. "You shall be holy, for I am holy" (Lev. 19:2; 1 Pet. 1:16) moves from who God *is* to what we *ought* to be. That move is not a fallacy, because the *is* in question is a Person whose character is the standard.

So Volume 3 does not derive goodness from its models. Its models describe patterns in how people respond to a goodness that is already there. That is the line the Analogy Boundary Principle draws, and it is the line Moore's argument requires.

### To Kant: beauty is real, and the beholder must be made able to see it

Kant was right that beauty involves a response. Aquinas said as much: beautiful things are those that *please when seen*. But for Aquinas the pleasure is the mind's apprehension of real form, real proportion, real radiance. The response is to something that is there.

The Reformed tradition puts it in its own words. Jonathan Edwards taught that "true virtue most essentially consists in benevolence to being in general", and found the highest beauty in "being's consent to being": the harmony of a creature's heart with God (*The Nature of True Virtue*, 1765, chs. I and III). Beauty, on this view, is the visible glory of goodness. "Worship the Lord in the beauty of holiness" (Ps. 96:9).

Kant's real point survives, though, and the volume should keep it. Not every beholder sees. The noetic effects of sin dim the eye (Rom. 1:21; 1 Cor. 2:14), as TFT Challenged already insisted. Beauty is objective, and the capacity to perceive it has to be restored. That restoration is part of what formation is.

### To Law: good and evil are not mirror images

The sharpest recent form of the modern objection comes from Stephen Law. Every argument for a good God, he claims, works about as well for an evil one: a first cause, a fine-tuner, a lawgiver could be either. Since nobody believes in an evil God, belief in a good God should look just as weak ("The Evil-God Challenge," *Religious Studies* 46, 2010).

The challenge fails because good and evil are not mirror images. Jack Symes, who set out to defend Law, tested about fifteen arguments and found two that fit both hypotheses and thirteen that favour a good God (*Defeating the Evil-God Challenge*, 2024). The decisive asymmetry is the classical one already met above. Being and goodness are the same reality, so a being complete in every way is complete in goodness too. Evil is not a second substance. It is a privation, a lack or corruption in something good, and it can exist only as a parasite on what God made.

Experience points the same way. Merete Jakobsen studied the accounts of evil in the Alister Hardy archive of religious experience: about 170 among more than 4,000 letters. Writers named its source as their own heart, as Satan or the Devil, or as a power opposed to God. The protection they reached for hardly varied: the sign of the cross or the Lord's Prayer, and in most accounts the attack stopped (*Negative Spiritual Experiences: Encounters with Evil*, 1999). The evidence fits a real enemy who is lesser and answerable to the name of Christ. It does not fit two equal powers.

This is also the shape Volume 3 needs when it finally models the adversary. See *What stays open*, below.

## Aquinas's objection, and the reply

### The objection

If truth, goodness, and beauty are one reality, distinguished only in how we receive it, then a theory with three separate fields has the wrong shape. It treats as three things what is one thing seen three ways. Aquinas would not object to the mathematics as such. He would object to the count.

### What TFT actually contains

TFT's own equations answer this better than the early draft suggests. Three features matter.

1. **One action, not three.** The equations put all three under a single variational principle: one total action whose extremum is "the best reality configuration". The three appear as parts of one whole, not as independent systems.
2. **A Trinity analogy at the root.** The framework calls its unifying structure "a unified 'Trinitarian field theory' in an abstract sense."
3. **A symmetry that turns the three into one another.** In the gauge form, the three transcendental directions can be rotated into one another "while preserving total 'magnitude'". In that form, which component you call truth, goodness, or beauty depends partly on the frame you choose. What stays the same under every rotation is the whole.

The third feature is closer to Aquinas than its authors may have known. A quantity that is one under every change of frame, and is divided into three only relative to a frame, is a fair mathematical picture of *one in reality, distinct in idea*.

### Where the reply holds, and where it fails

The reply holds if TFT is read through its symmetry: the three fields are three coordinates of one state, not three things.

It fails if TFT is read as its equations are first written: a sum of three sectors, each with its own field, its own mass, and its own dynamics, coupled together afterward. That reading is the one Aquinas's objection hits, and it is the one the early draft invited.

So the reply comes with a condition, and Volume 3 adopts it:

> **The three transcendental fields in TFT are three aspects of one reality, not three realities. Where the equations write them as separate sectors, that is bookkeeping, not ontology.**

This is the Analogy Boundary Principle applied to the count of fields, as TFT Challenged applied it to their substance.

### A caution about the Trinity analogy

The symmetry that rescues TFT from Aquinas creates a danger on the other side.

Truth, goodness, and beauty are distinct only in idea. The Persons of the Trinity are really distinct: the Father is not the Son, and the Son is not the Spirit. If the three transcendentals are mapped straight onto the three Persons, the rotation symmetry would say the Persons are one being seen from three frames. That is the ancient error of modalism.

The tradition's own practice avoids this. It *appropriates* attributes to the Persons rather than identifying them. Aquinas, following Hilary, appropriates beauty to the Son, who is the Image of the Father: "species or beauty has a likeness to the property of the Son" (ST I q.39 a.8). Appropriation says that an attribute shared by all three is fittingly shown in one. It does not say the attribute *is* that Person.

TFT's Trinity language should be read the same way: an appropriation, not an identity.

### Where this goes next

This reply changes nothing in TFT's mathematics and promotes no analogy, so it does not need the Council's decision. It will be told to the Council for counsel in November 2026. If, on reflection, the reply does not hold, and three distinct fields cannot be read as aspects of one, then the architecture has to change. That would come to the Council as an ask.

## What stays open

### Can truth, goodness, and beauty pull against each other?

The source assessment asks whether the three can conflict: a true word that wounds, a beautiful lie. Traditional theism says they cannot conflict *in God*, who is all three without division. The Christian does not have to pretend they never conflict *in the world*.

The world is fallen, and there is an enemy. Lies can be made beautiful (2 Cor. 11:14). Truth can be wielded without love (1 Cor. 13:2). These are not failures of the transcendentals. They are the work of an adversary who separates what God joined.

TFT has no term for that. Its couplings all pull toward coherence. The law tests of October 2026 found the same gap from another side: Volume 3 has no adversary, and one candidate model treats the adversary as a parasitic disturbance on the system. Spiritual warfare is before the Council as its core discussion in October 2026. What the Council counsels there will shape how this question is answered here. It is listed in [Open Trails](open-trails-what-vol-3-cannot-yet.md).

### What earlier attempts to measure value teach

Bentham, Birkhoff, and Arrow failed in instructive ways. Bentham could not say what his numbers measured. Birkhoff measured what was easy to count and missed what mattered. Arrow showed that some values will not combine into one total without loss.

[Exploration 1](exploration-01-case-for-quantification.md) makes the case for quantification without yet facing these precedents. It should. The lesson is not that measurement is wrong. It is that a measure must say what it measures, must not mistake the countable for the important, and must not force plural goods into a single currency. Volume 4's instruments should be read against the same three tests. This is also listed in Open Trails.
