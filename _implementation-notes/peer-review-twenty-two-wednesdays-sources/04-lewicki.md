# Source-Fidelity Review — Roy J. Lewicki

**Paper:** "Twenty-Two Wednesdays as a Fellowship of the Heart," draft v2.3 (2026-08-18)
**Reviewing for:** fidelity to Lewicki & Bunker (1996) and Lewicki, Tomlinson & Gillespie (2006)
**Scope:** my own work only. I offer no judgment on the theology, the curriculum, or the pilot's prospects.

---

## 1. What the paper gets right about my work

Let me start where the author has done well, because in my experience of being cited by people outside
organizational behavior, this paper is better than most.

**The three stages are named correctly, in the right order, with the right basic architecture.** [41]:
"calculus-based (I behave because the structure holds me), knowledge-based (I trust because I know you
now), and identification-based (your values have become mine)." The sequence is ours, the names are ours,
and the third gloss — "your values have become mine" — is close to how Bunker and I actually described
identification-based trust. Whatever quarrels follow, the author has read the model rather than a summary
of a summary.

**The division of credit is attempted explicitly, in the sentence itself.** [41]: "the trust literature
supplies the stages, and this project reads the answers as a staged vocabulary: language diagnostic of a
stage." I want to say plainly that this is the sentence I would have asked for. It is uncommon for a
practitioner-author to volunteer the seam between what the cited literature established and what the author
is adding. Most papers I am cited in do not draw that line at all. My findings below concern whether the
line is held everywhere else, not whether the author drew it.

**The proposed mapping is flagged as proposed.** [58]: "would break the proposed mapping between trust
stage and internalization level, which this project has begun registering as a formal research trail." The
word "proposed" is doing honest work there, and the author has committed to a condition under which his own
mapping fails. That is more than my model has ever been subjected to in a field setting.

**The bibliographic apparatus is accurate.** Footnote 10 and bibliography entries [76] and [77] give the
1996 chapter with the correct editors (Kramer and Tyler), publisher, and pagination (114–139), and the 2006
review with the correct journal, volume, issue, and pages. I have been miscited on both often enough to
notice when I have not been.

**The first-fight claim is not charged to me.** [42] attributes it to "the group-development literature"
and footnotes Tuckman and Wheelan. Correctly so. I have findings about that sentence, but not about its
attribution to me, and the author should not be charged for a fault he avoided.

**The permission ethic is better grounded in my literature than the author seems to know.** [21] "Nothing
is ever asked" and [34] "a room that does not feel surveilled behaves like itself" arrive independently at
something the trust literature has argued for decades: monitoring and formal control substitute for trust
rather than supplement it, and a heavily monitored relationship will produce calculative behavior because
calculative behavior is what monitoring rewards. If you install instruments people feel, you will read
calculus-based trust all year and conclude the curriculum failed. The author's restraint protects his own
measurement. He should say so.

**Finally, and I mean this as a real contribution rather than a courtesy:** the design already contains the
single best identification-based-trust indicator I have seen in a field protocol, and does not recognize it
as one. See Finding 2's remedy, below.

## 2. Findings

### Finding 1 — "Fifty years of that literature describes trust developing through three levels" overstates both the age and the empirical standing of the model. **HIGH**

[41]: "Fifty years of that literature describes trust developing through three levels."

Three problems, compounding.

*Arithmetic.* Bunker and I published in 1996 — thirty years ago as this paper goes out, not fifty. The
typology we adapted was published in 1992. "Fifty years" appears to have migrated from [16], where the
author correctly says the disclosure research "has said for fifty years" (Altman and Taylor, 1973 — which
is about right). The phrase has been reused where it does not fit.

*Consensus.* "That literature describes trust developing through three levels" tells a reader that the
trust field, as a field, holds a three-stage view. It does not, and the paper's own footnote 10 disproves
the sentence it supports. Mayer, Davis and Schoorman (1995) is not a stage model at all — it is a model of
trustworthiness perceptions across ability, benevolence and integrity. Rousseau et al. (1998) is a
cross-disciplinary definitional synthesis, not a developmental sequence. Three of the four works in that
footnote are cited under a sentence claiming a three-stage consensus that two of them do not share.

*Empirical status.* This is the part that matters most, and it is the part I would be most embarrassed to
have carried on my name. The 2006 review the paper cites — Lewicki, Tomlinson and Gillespie — exists
substantially because the developmental models, mine included, had accumulated far more theoretical
elaboration than direct empirical test. We surveyed the developmental approaches and reported that the
evidence for staged transition specifically was thin: the stages are a well-specified conceptual framework
whose sequential claims had not been demonstrated longitudinally. The paper cites that review as "updated
in," which reads to any reader as *confirmed and refined in*. It was not a confirmation. A protocol paper
resting a new instrument on my stages should say that it is resting on a theoretical model, not on fifty
years of findings.

*Fix.* Rewrite to something like: the model is a widely used theoretical framework, roughly three decades
old, whose stage transitions remain under-tested longitudinally — a limitation this pilot's three self-reads
are, in a small way, positioned to speak to. That is both accurate and considerably more interesting for the
author, because it turns a borrowed warrant into a modest original contribution.

### Finding 2 — The house words held / known / owned are not faithful glosses; all three drift onto a single dimension of felt safety, and the instrument loses its ability to discriminate the stages. **HIGH**

[41]: "a room holds together because the container holds it (held), because its people know each other
(known), or because they have become each other's people (owned). 'It's safe and well-run' is held
language; 'they know me there' is known; 'these are my people, I could say anything' is owned."

Take them one at a time.

**held.** Calculus-based trust in our model is not a container holding people safely. It is a calculation.
Its ground is deterrence — the trustor's assessment that the costs to the other party of violating trust
(punishment, reputational damage, loss of the relationship's benefits) exceed the gains — supplemented by
the rewards of preserving the relation. It is wary trust, low-vulnerability trust, monitoring-heavy trust,
the trust of two negotiators who have not yet given each other a reason. "The structure holds me" is a
defensible half-reading of the constraint side. But the exemplar phrase gives the game away: "It's safe and
well-run" is not a statement about cost calculation. It is a statement about *felt safety and competent
administration*, which is Edmondson's construct — the very one the paper correctly names in [17]. A person
saying "it's safe and well-run" may be reporting either a functioning container or a wary calculation, and
this instrument cannot tell the two apart.

There is an internal collision here the author should see. [17] declares "The container is the first
curriculum" and treats a room that holds without enforcement as the first quarter's win [49]. [51] and [58]
then treat "held" language as the immature stage the room should migrate away from. The paper's own
foundational achievement is coded as its lowest trust rung, because the house word was chosen from the
container metaphor rather than from the model.

**known.** This one is a directional inversion, and it is precise. Knowledge-based trust in our model rests
on **predictability**: I have enough history with you to anticipate how you will behave, and that
anticipation, not intimacy, is what lets me rely on you. The trustor knows the trustee well enough to
forecast them. The paper's exemplar is "they know me there" — passive, the member being known *by* the
group. That is disclosure received, which is Altman and Taylor's territory, not predictability held.
A member can feel profoundly known and be unable to predict a single other person in the room; a member can
predict everyone in the room precisely and disclose nothing. The house word captures the wrong one.

**owned.** Identification-based trust is the stage at which one party can **act as agent for** the other:
understanding the other's wants and preferences well enough to substitute for them, to represent their
interests in their absence, to speak for them. The paper's exemplar — "these are my people, I could say
anything" — is again a psychological-safety statement, a claim about permission to speak, not about
capacity to represent.

Put the three together and the pattern is the finding. "Safe and well-run" → "they know me there" → "I could
say anything here" is a single ascending scale of felt safety and belonging. It is a perfectly reasonable
scale. It is not my three stages, which are three *different mechanisms* (cost calculation, behavioral
prediction, internalized identification) rather than three intensities of one feeling. The instrument as
worded will measure warmth thrice and will report migration whenever a room gets warmer — which the
curriculum is separately designed to produce. That is a construct-validity problem, and my name on it makes
it look like a measurement problem it is not.

*Fix, and this is the constructive half.* If the author wants an identification-based-trust read, he should
stop listening for warm vocabulary and start watching for **agency substitution**, which his own design
already schedules. [19]: a teenager runs the second running and "the container survives in a younger voice."
[36]: "the sought stray (when someone goes quiet, who notices, and who goes after them)." A member who runs
the process on the leader's behalf, and a member who goes after a stray without being sent, are both acting
as agent for another — the canonical behavioral marker of identification-based trust, and far harder to fake
than a phrase. The paper calls the first of these "the leadership absence test" and reads it against
internalization. It is also, and more defensibly, the trust reading he is looking for. Likewise, a
knowledge-based read is available behaviorally: can members correctly anticipate one another — who will
volunteer, who needs to be asked twice, who is about to say the hard thing. That is predictability, and it
is observable.

### Finding 3 — "The identification stage is internalization by another name, visible at group scale" is an unhedged equation of two constructs from different traditions, and a silent level-of-analysis shift, both stated in my voice. **HIGH**

[41]: "The identification stage is internalization by another name, visible at group scale."

This is a fourteen-word sentence carrying three claims, none of them mine, all of them sitting directly
under footnote 10.

*Claim one:* that identification-based trust and Krathwohl's affective internalization are the same
construct. They are not. Identification-based trust is a property of a **relationship between parties** — it
has a referent, a specific other whose preferences I have internalized well enough to act for them.
Krathwohl's internalization is a property of a **person's relation to a value**, with no interpersonal
referent required. There is a genuine resonance — both involve something external becoming constitutive of
the self — and it is a resonance worth writing about. But "is … by another name" asserts identity, not
resonance, and the author elsewhere shows he knows the difference: [58] calls it "the proposed mapping."
The hedge exists in the paper. It is simply absent from the sentence that most needs it.

*Claim two:* "visible at group scale." Our model is a model of **interpersonal, largely dyadic trust**. We
did not establish, and did not claim, that a group possesses a trust stage. The move from "A trusts B at
stage two" to "this room is at stage two" is a referent shift, and in organizational research it is a
contested one that requires either an explicit composition model (how do dyadic states aggregate?) or a
distinct group-referent construct. The paper makes the shift in a subordinate clause.

*Claim three, implied:* that stage and level advance together, such that vocabulary migration would evidence
internalization. That is the mapping [58] properly labels as proposed.

*Fix.* Reword to something like: identification-based trust and affective internalization are distinct
constructs from different traditions that this project proposes to treat as convergent, and the model's
dyadic origin is being extended to a group referent without warrant from its authors. Then footnote 10 can
stand where it is without carrying the extension.

### Finding 4 — A single room-level stage reading masks within-room heterogeneity, and the pilot's families are the acute case. **MEDIUM**

[30]: "circling one word for the quarter's trust read." [28]: "map them … to the three signs and to the
trust vocabulary."

Trust in our model is dyadic, directional and asymmetric. In a room of, say, eight families, member A may
sit at identification-based trust with her own mother, knowledge-based with two other teenagers, and
calculus-based with everyone else. One word per quarter compresses all of that to a point estimate, and the
compression is not neutral: it will be driven by whoever speaks.

The pilot has a specific version of this problem that a work-relationships model never had to face. **The
room contains pre-existing identification-based dyads on day one.** Parent and child are, in trust terms,
frequently the paradigm case of identification-based trust — long history, internalized preferences,
routine agency substitution. So the design starts with a set of dyads at stage three embedded in a group of
strangers at stage one, and it starts with those exact dyads doing the disclosing in front of everyone
([16], "in every practice the parents start"). A teen describing "this group" warmly in week seven may be
describing a family relationship that predates the curriculum. Nothing in the self-read separates trust in
*the room* from trust in *my people who happen to be in the room*.

*Fix.* State the dyadic origin and the aggregation problem as a limitation; and consider asking the third
question in a form that forces the room referent — describing the group to an outsider *who does not know
your family* — so the answer cannot be satisfied by naming kin.

### Finding 5 — The scope conditions of the original model (work relationships, professional, voluntary, exitable, adult, roughly symmetric) are never stated, and one of them is inverted by the design. **MEDIUM**

[41] introduces the model as "the trust literature" and "that literature." Nowhere does the paper say what
kind of relationships the stages were developed for.

I note that the author does this correctly for Edmondson — [17]: "The workplace literature calls the
underlying construct psychological safety." One sentence, and the reader knows exactly what is being
extended from where. My model gets no such sentence, though it needs one more, not less. Bunker and I wrote
about work relationships: professional, largely voluntary, exitable, between adults of roughly comparable
standing, often negotiated. The pilot applies the model to parents and adolescent children — a relationship
that is involuntary, non-exitable, permanently power-asymmetric, and lifelong. That is a substantial
extension. It may well be a productive one. It is undeclared.

One consequence deserves its own line, because it is squarely in my territory. The design has the
higher-power party disclose first, every time, by rule. In a power-asymmetric relationship, having the
powerful party model disclosure and then wait is one of the most reliable ways to elicit **compliant
reciprocation** — the lower-power party matches because the cost of not matching is visible and immediate.
Matched disclosure of that kind is a calculus-based behavior wearing a knowledge-based face. I am not saying
that is what is happening in this room; the author's field experience with one family may well outweigh my
prior. I am saying the design contains a mechanism that can manufacture the appearance of stage advance, and
a paper claiming vocabulary as a stage diagnostic ought to name it.

### Finding 6 — The division of credit for "staged vocabulary" holds in the prose but not in the apparatus. **MEDIUM**

[41]: "the trust literature supplies the stages, and this project reads the answers as a staged vocabulary:
language diagnostic of a stage, **since the words a member reaches for when describing the group carry the
marks of the trust actually held in it**, and a question that asks for description rather than a rating lets
those marks surface unprompted."

I traced this through every paragraph as asked. The verdict is mixed, and mostly in the author's favor:
[51] ("watch the vocabulary … migrate from safety language toward belonging language") is plainly the
author's own voice; [58] labels the mapping "proposed"; [28] and [30] refer to "the trust vocabulary" as a
house term without leaning on anyone. The division survives in the running text.

Where it fails is here, in two places within one sentence. First, the clause I have bolded is introduced by
"since," which grammatically presents it as an *established reason* rather than a *proposed inference*.
"Since X" asserts X. Second, and more seriously, footnote 10 — the note that names me and Bunker, and
Tomlinson and Gillespie, and Mayer, and Rousseau — attaches in this vicinity and is the only citation the
reader has. A reader who reaches the end of that sentence and glances down finds four trust-literature
citations under a paragraph containing both the stages *and* the linguistic inference. The prose divides the
credit; the note apparatus recombines it. I have no linguistic markers. Neither Bunker nor I ever proposed
that spontaneous vocabulary is diagnostic of trust stage. It is not in the 1996 chapter and it is not in the
2006 review, and if a discourse-analytic literature on trust language exists, I am not it.

*Fix.* Cheap and complete: add a sentence to footnote 10 along the lines of *the cited works establish the
stages; the inference from spontaneous vocabulary to stage is this author's proposal and is untested.* Turn
"since" into "on the proposal that." Two edits and the finding disappears.

### Finding 7 — The design treats stage progression as one-way, sequential and retained; our model claims something more conditional, and includes decline. **MEDIUM**

[54] "If the ladder never climbs." [58] "whether the room's unprompted language moves from held toward
owned." [51] "intrinsic valuing displacing external structure."

Three corrections, all of which make the design better rather than worse.

*Most relationships do not traverse all three stages, and this is not pathology.* Bunker and I were explicit
that a great many relationships settle permanently at calculus-based trust, and that this is often
appropriate to what the relationship is for. Identification-based trust is comparatively rare, requires
substantial shared time and experience, and is not the natural terminus of every relationship that goes
well. A room that ends the year sounding like knowledge-based trust has not failed at anything. The paper
already knows how to say this — [12], on Krathwohl level four, is a model of the right posture: "designed
for but never claimed … its absence at year's end is not read as failure." The trust ladder deserves the
same sentence and does not have it.

*The stages are cumulative, not replacive.* Advancing does not dissolve the calculus base; the deterrence
floor and the structural guarantees remain in place underneath, and higher-stage trust rests on them. So
"migration" and "displacing" are the wrong verbs. Practically, this creates a live misreading risk for the
companion team: a room whose spring language stops mentioning safety may have internalized the container —
or may have started taking it for granted, or may have had it quietly fail while nobody wanted to say so.
Declining "held" language is ambiguous evidence, and the coding scheme as described cannot disambiguate it.

*Trust declines, and my model says how.* A meaningful portion of the 1996 chapter concerns violation and
repair — what a breach does at each stage, and why a breach at a higher stage is more damaging, not less.
The pilot's disconfirmation set [53]–[58] is admirably thorough and contains no regression condition. One
broken confidence in a room this size is entirely capable of moving a group backward, and a design that has
only "if the ladder never climbs" will read a regression as noise or as a bad quarter.

*Fix.* Add a disconfirmation condition for regression, and one sentence stating that stages are cumulative
and that arrival at identification-based trust is neither expected nor required within a year.

### Finding 8 — The "first fight" claim is not mine, and as stated it is not quite anyone's; its sequence and its mechanism come from two different literatures welded with an unearned "because." **MEDIUM**

[42]: "open disagreement tends to arrive before mature trust, not after it, **because** people risk conflict
only in a room they believe will hold it; the first honestly-processed disagreement is usually a milestone,
not a malfunction."

The paper does not charge this to me, and I said so in section 1. But since I was asked whose it is: the
*sequence* claim (conflict arrives early, before cohesion) is Tuckman's storming, correctly footnoted at 11.
The *mechanism* claim (people voice disagreement only where they believe it is safe to) is Edmondson's, from
the psychological-safety and voice literature that the paper cites well at [17]. Those two are not the same
proposition, and in one respect they run against each other: in Tuckman's sequence, storming occurs in a
group that has *not yet* established norms or cohesion — conflict as a symptom of unsettledness — whereas
Edmondson's logic makes voiced conflict a symptom of established safety. The paper's "because" fuses them
into a single causal story that neither author advanced. The word "usually" then makes it a frequency claim,
and I know of no base rate for it in any of these literatures, let alone in family-integrated church groups.

From my own side of the fence, a caution the author will find useful: in the negotiation and trust
literature, first conflict is where trust is *tested*, and it is at least as capable of destroying trust as
of demonstrating it. A first disagreement at a calculus-based stage frequently produces exit, silence, or
covert retaliation, none of which look like a milestone. The marker as written cannot distinguish "the room
held it" from "the room appeared to hold it and the dissenter never spoke again."

*Fix.* Split the citations (Tuckman for the sequence, Edmondson for the mechanism), downgrade "usually" to
"can be," and pair the marker with a follow-up read: did the disagreeing member speak again in the following
three sessions, and did anyone go after them. The design already has the machinery — [36]'s sought stray is
precisely the follow-up.

### Finding 9 — The trust read has no coding procedure, no second coder, and three observations, while the paper's other instruments are carefully calibrated. **MEDIUM**

[30]: "circling one word for the quarter's trust read." [41]: "the scribe's verbatim notes from three
self-reads will show whether it did."

I note the contrast within the paper itself. The three vital signs get aged rungs, an explicit
seen / not yet / no occasion coding, a three-consecutive-weeks promotion rule, and — [38] — "worked examples
of what each reading looks like at each rung sit on two teaching pages at the front of the pack." That is a
real codebook. The trust vocabulary gets three example phrases in a single sentence of running prose and one
circled word per quarter.

Consequences: three occasions cannot demonstrate migration through three stages under any coding scheme;
the coder is the same person who leads the room and wants the migration to be there; and there is no
agreement check available even though the design has a second person present, since [28] gives the
co-companion the verbatim scribe role and [38] gives the team a joint call. Independent coding is nearly
free here — have the scribe and one other companion code the same verbatim transcript blind before the call,
then compare on the call, and report the disagreements. Disagreement between the two coders is, by the
paper's own governing rule [32], data rather than noise.

### Finding 10 — Collecting the vocabulary aloud, leader first, undercuts the paper's own claim that the salience is unprompted. **MEDIUM**

[40]: "the room answers them together, leader first as always." [28]: "The leader answers first, all three,
each with a story attached, and then the room speaks." [41]: "unprompted salience, what a room spontaneously
names as worth keeping, is a truer read of what it values than any answer to a supplied category."

I take the author's point that a room is not opaque to its members, and for *observable events* — did the
circle hold, was anyone noticed — the argument in [41] is sound and rather good. It does not extend to trust
states, and the collection method works against the instrument in two ways.

First, priming. Whatever words the leader uses in his answer are in the room before anyone else speaks, and
they arrive attached to a story, which makes them more memorable, not less. A leader hoping to hear
belonging language and speaking first every quarter is running a vocabulary supply operation, however
unintentionally. The design took considerable care [40] to avoid handing the room a category to rate itself
against, and then hands it a lexicon instead.

Second, selection. The members whose trust in the room is lowest are precisely the members least likely to
say so aloud in it. Spoken group data is voice data, and voice is a function of the safety being measured.
The resulting vocabulary sample will skew toward the high-trust end by construction, and will skew further
in that direction over the year as the room warms — producing exactly the migration the design predicts,
from the sampling process alone.

*Fix.* Have members write their answer before anyone speaks — the paper already sends the questions home
"across the break on purpose so the members who think best in quiet arrive ready" [40], so it is a small
step to have them arrive with a written line. Or, at minimum, let the leader answer last for the third
question. And record who did not speak; silence is part of the sample.

### Finding 11 — "It is seen in trust, as the group risks deeper and more costly self-revelation" defines trust as disclosure depth, which is a different literature. **LOW**

[13]: "It is seen in trust, as the group risks deeper and more costly self-revelation with one another."

This is Altman and Taylor's social penetration — correctly cited elsewhere, at [16] and footnote 5 — and it
is not a definition of trust in our sense. Disclosure depth is one behavior that trust can enable; it is not
the construct. The paper is quietly running two theories under one word, "trust," and the reader carries the
fusion into [41]. Since the exemplar phrases at [41] also lean toward disclosure ("I could say anything"),
the fusion has consequences downstream. A one-clause distinction at [13] would prevent it.

## 4. What I would want cited differently

**1. Credit the typology's origin.** The three-type structure — deterrence-based, knowledge-based,
identification-based — comes from Shapiro, Sheppard and Cheraskin, "Business on a Handshake," *Negotiation
Journal* (1992). Bunker and I adapted it, developed the transitions, and renamed deterrence-based as
calculus-based to capture the reward side alongside the deterrence side. A footnote naming them costs one
line and gets the lineage right. (The author should verify pagination; I am confident of the source, not of
the page range from memory.)

**2. Cite the 2006 review for what it found.** Footnote 10 currently reads "updated in Roy J. Lewicki,
Edward C. Tomlinson, and Nicole Gillespie …," which implies the later piece confirms and refines the earlier
one. Its actual contribution relevant to this paper is a stock-taking that found the developmental models
under-tested and called for longitudinal work. Cite it for that, and Finding 1's problem largely resolves
itself.

**3. For the counterfeit-harmony point at [42], add the trust-and-distrust work.** [42] and footnote 12
rest the "sweetest-looking room can be the lowest-trust room" claim on Peck's *pseudocommunity*, which is
pastorally apt but not empirical. The organizational warrant is Lewicki, McAllister and Bies, "Trust and
Distrust: New Relationships and Realities," *Academy of Management Review* 23, no. 3 (1998), which argues
that trust and distrust are separable dimensions rather than poles of one scale. That is exactly the
author's point in rigorous form: a room can be simultaneously low-distrust and low-trust — bland, agreeable,
and empty — and a single-scale instrument will score it as healthy. It also happens to be the sharpest
argument available *against* the author's own three-phrase single-dimension coding, which is why I recommend
it.

**4. Move De Jong, Dirks and Gillespie to the job it can actually do.** It sits in footnote 9 as "general
trust-and-performance evidence at team scale." It is the paper's best available warrant for the
level-of-analysis move in Finding 3 — trust studied with a team referent — and should be cited there.
Two cautions if he does: it treats team trust as a state, not as a developmental stage sequence, and it is
a meta-analysis of trust's *consequences*, not of its *development*. So it licenses the referent, not the
ladder.

**5. Do not let footnote 10 stand under the vocabulary inference without a disclaimer.** As in Finding 6.
This is the single highest-value citation edit in the paper.

**6. Fix "fifty years."** Either "three decades" for the staged model, or reserve the fifty-year span for
trust research broadly and cite something appropriate to that span. As written it borrows Altman and
Taylor's number from [16].

**7. Separate the trust footnote from the disclosure claim at [13].** Social penetration is already
correctly cited at footnote 5; [13]'s "trust, as the group risks deeper and more costly self-revelation"
belongs to that note, not to mine.

## 5. My single biggest concern

If I could say one thing to the editor it is this: the paper's most original instrument — the staged
vocabulary, which the author himself calls "quietly the best instrument in the pilot" [41] — is built on
three house words that all measure the same thing. "It's safe and well-run," "they know me there," and
"these are my people, I could say anything" form a single ascending scale of felt safety and belonging;
they do not distinguish cost calculation from behavioral predictability from internalized identification,
which are three different mechanisms and the entire content of the model being borrowed. The practical
consequence is that the instrument will register migration whenever the room grows warmer — which is what
the curriculum is separately designed to produce — and the pilot will have confirmed its central prediction
by construction, with my name in the footnote making it look like established measurement. The remedy is not
to abandon the idea, which I think is genuinely interesting and which my own literature has never tested
longitudinally in a natural group; it is to read the stages off **behavior the design already schedules** —
who can predict whom (knowledge-based), and who acts as agent for another without being sent, in the
teen-led second runnings and the sought stray (identification-based) — and to let the vocabulary be
corroborating evidence rather than the instrument. Do that, add one sentence to footnote 10 saying the
linguistic inference is the author's and not the literature's, and stop describing a thirty-year-old and
under-tested theoretical model as fifty years of findings, and the paper's use of my work becomes something
I would be glad to be cited in.
