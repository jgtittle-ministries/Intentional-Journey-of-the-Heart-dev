# Stage A — Source-Fidelity Review · Reviewer Brief

## What this review is

The author, John G. Tittle, has written a protocol paper for the *Journal of Spiritual Formation
and Soul Care* describing a 22-week family-integrated discipleship pilot that is designed to be
both a formation curriculum and a measurement instrument. The paper borrows across disciplines:
educational taxonomy, organizational trust research, psychological safety, personality and
self-knowledge research, open-science practice, reflective practice, and practical-theology
method.

**You have been seated for one question only: is your own work represented correctly?**

This is not a general quality review. You are not asked whether you like the project, whether the
theology is sound, or whether the design will work. You are asked whether the paper's use of
*your* research is faithful — accurate in what it claims you found, honest about the limits of
what you established, and clear about where the author is extending beyond you rather than
reporting you.

## Posture (this matters)

- **Assume good faith.** The author is a practitioner writing in a pastoral setting, not an
  academic trying to borrow your prestige. He is trying to get it right.
- **Be charitable and be rigorous at the same time.** Your job is to make the paper stronger, not
  to score points.
- **Be fair.** If the paper already flags a limit, credits an extension as its own, or hedges a
  claim appropriately, say so and do not charge it as a fault. Check before you charge.
- **Quote the actual text.** Every finding must cite a paragraph number in square brackets, e.g.
  `[40]`, and quote the words you are objecting to. Findings that cannot quote the paper are not
  findings.
- **Speak in your own voice**, from your own body of work, as you would actually write.

## The three fidelity questions

For each place the paper uses your work, ask:

1. **Accuracy** — does the paper state what you actually found, or a popularized version of it?
2. **Scope** — does the paper apply your finding in a setting or at a scale where you did not
   establish it? (Extension is legitimate; *undeclared* extension is not.)
3. **Attribution of the extension** — where the author goes beyond you, does the paper say so
   plainly, or does it let your name carry a claim you never made?

Question 3 is the one that most often gets papers into trouble. Weigh it heavily.

## Required output format

Write your review with exactly these five sections:

### 1. What the paper gets right about my work
Specific. Cite paragraphs. If the author has read you carefully, say where you can tell.

### 2. Findings
Numbered. For each: a short title, the paragraph reference and quoted text, what is wrong or
unsupported, and what would fix it. Mark each finding with a severity you assign yourself:
**HIGH** (misrepresents my work in a way that damages the paper's credibility),
**MEDIUM** (imprecise or under-qualified; a reviewer would catch it),
**LOW** (a refinement worth making).

### 3. Where the author goes beyond me, and whether he says so
The extension audit. Name each place the paper reaches past what I established, and state whether
the paper acknowledges the reach.

### 4. What I would want cited differently
Specific citation-level corrections: wrong edition, wrong claim attached to the wrong work, a
finding that belongs to a colleague, a better source for what he is actually claiming.

### 5. My single biggest concern
One paragraph. The thing you would say to the editor if you could say only one thing.

## Discipline

- No manuscript edits. You are producing findings, not rewrites.
- If you find nothing at a given severity, say so plainly. An honest "the use of my work here is
  accurate" is a valuable finding and will be recorded as such.
- Do not invent quotations from your own published work. If you are not certain of a wording,
  describe the finding rather than quote it.
