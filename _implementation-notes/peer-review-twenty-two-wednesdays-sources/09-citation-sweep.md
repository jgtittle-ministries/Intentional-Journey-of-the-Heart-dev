## 1. Marilyn Strathern, "'Improving Ratings': Audit in the British University System" (1997)

**(a) Bibliographic details — CORRECT.** Verified against the article itself: *European Review*
vol. 5, no. 3 (1997): 305–321, published by John Wiley & Sons; adapted from the Founders' Memorial
Lecture at Girton College, Cambridge, 11 March 1997. Bibliography entry [87] and fn 13 both give
volume, issue, year, and page range correctly.

**(b) Use — INCORRECT in fn 13. Severity: HIGH.**

fn 13 reads:

> "Marilyn Strathern, "'Improving Ratings': Audit in the British University System," *European
> Review* 5, no. 3 (1997): 305–321, **popularly summarized as Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes
> a target, it ceases to be a good measure.**"

This inverts the provenance twice over. What Strathern actually writes, on p. 308 of the article,
verbatim:

> "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. The more a 2.1 examination
> performance becomes an expectation, the poorer it becomes as a discriminator of individual
> performances. Hoskin describes this as 'Goodhart's law', after the latter's observation on
> instruments for monetary control which lead to other devices for monetary flexibility having to be
> invented."

Three things follow:

1. **The famous sentence is Strathern's own.** It is not a "popular summary" of her article; it is a
   line *in* her article, written by her, and it is the sentence the world now quotes. Describing it
   as a summary of her paper implies someone else coined it and that she is being paraphrased.
2. **Goodhart's law is a distinct claim in different words by a different person.** Charles
   Goodhart's 1975 formulation, from work on UK monetary policy, is: "Any observed statistical
   regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes." That is a
   claim about statistical regularities under policy pressure. It is *not* the "measure becomes a
   target" sentence, and Goodhart never wrote that sentence.
3. **Strathern is scrupulous about the label and the paper is not.** She does not claim to be
   restating Goodhart; she notes that *Hoskin* attaches the name "Goodhart's law" to the phenomenon
   she has just described in her own words. The paper's footnote collapses Strathern, Hoskin, and
   Goodhart into one undifferentiated attribution.

**Why HIGH.** This is a paper about the corruption of measures that become targets. Getting the
provenance of that literature's single most-quoted sentence backwards, in a footnote, in the paper's
own subject area, is exactly the kind of thing an editor or an economically literate reviewer
notices, and it is trivially checkable. The bibliographic entry is fine; the gloss is the problem.

**Fix.** Something like: *Marilyn Strathern, "'Improving Ratings'…," 305–21, at 308 ("When a measure
becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"), a formulation now widely — and inaccurately —
attributed to Charles Goodhart, whose own 1975 statement was that "any observed statistical
regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes."* If the
author wants only one sentence, cite Strathern for the wording and drop the Goodhart name, or cite
Goodhart separately.

**Credit where due — the body text is clean.** [45] says only: "the measurement literature has its
own name for the corruption of measures that become targets, and churches are not exempt from it."
That is accurate, appropriately hedged, and names nobody. The damage is confined to fn 13 and does
not need a body-text rewrite.

**LOW.** fn 13 is an omnibus note bundling five unrelated sources (Strathern, Vazire, Bonhoeffer,
Hawkins & Parkinson, Schwarz) behind a single paragraph about targets corrupting mirrors. A reader
cannot tell which source supports which claim, and three of the five support no claim visible in
[45] at all. Consider splitting it.

## 2. Irwin Altman and Dalmas A. Taylor, *Social Penetration* (1973)

**(a) Bibliographic details — CORRECT.** *Social Penetration: The Development of Interpersonal
Relationships*, New York: Holt, Rinehart and Winston, 1973. Verified. Bibliography [66] and fn 5
agree and are both right.

**(b) Use — substantially accurate, with one real scope problem.**

[16]: "Vulnerability is reciprocal; **the disclosure research has said for fifty years that people
deepen by matched steps, each opening the next level for the other.**"

This is a fair statement of social penetration theory: relationships develop by reciprocal
self-disclosure expanding along breadth (range of topics) and depth (intimacy), with reciprocity a
core mechanism. "Fifty years" from 1973 is right (53). The paper also hedges well by attributing the
claim to "the disclosure research" rather than to Altman and Taylor personally.

A point in the paper's favour that it does not claim: Altman and Taylor's model is explicitly a
*social-exchange* (reward–cost) account — people weigh the cost of vulnerability against the reward
of intimacy. The pilot's central construct is literally named "**costly** telling." The theory
supports that construct more directly than the paper says.

**MEDIUM — Finding 4: the 1973 model was superseded by its own author, and the pilot's
disconfirmation conditions depend on the superseded version.**

Altman, Vinsel and Brown (1981, *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 14: 107–160)
substantially revised social penetration away from a linear, monotonically deepening model toward a
**dialectical** one, in which openness and closedness cycle and relational development involves
ongoing negotiation of boundaries rather than steady penetration inward. Subsequent work has found
empirical support for the cyclical model.

That matters here, because the pilot predicts monotonic movement and treats its absence as
disconfirming:

- [54]: "A cohort still showing *not yet* on first forms in February, or no growing form all year,
  falsifies either the phasing or the curriculum."
- [58]: "Stagnant vocabulary alongside advancing sign-readings would break the proposed mapping."
- [37]: promotion of a rung only "after three consecutive weeks of *seen*."

Under the dialectical revision, a cohort that deepens, closes up for a stretch, and re-opens is
behaving normally, not failing. As written, the design has no way to distinguish a dialectical
withdrawal from a genuine failure to climb.

**Fix.** One sentence acknowledging the revision, and one line in [54]/[58] distinguishing *cyclical
retreat* from *flat trajectory* as disconfirming evidence. Adding Altman, Vinsel & Brown 1981 to the
bibliography would also protect the author from a reviewer who knows the literature's second act.

**LOW — scope.** Altman and Taylor's evidence base is dyads, much of it from isolation studies and
undergraduate samples, not families, not multi-generational groups, and not adolescents disclosing
in front of a parent. The paper applies it to precisely that setting. It is largely protected here,
because [16] is candid that field evidence and not the literature drove the design ("A year and a
half of prototyping with one faithful family taught me the opposite, provided one condition holds:
the parents tell first"). That is an honestly declared extension and should not be charged as a
fault. A half-sentence noting that the reciprocity finding was established in dyads would close it
entirely.

## 3. Bruce W. Tuckman (1965) and Susan A. Wheelan (2005)

**(a) Bibliographic details — CORRECT, both.**
- Tuckman, "Developmental Sequence in Small Groups," *Psychological Bulletin* 63, no. 6 (1965):
  384–399. Verified.
- Wheelan, *Group Processes: A Developmental Perspective*, 2nd ed., Boston: Allyn and Bacon, 2005.
  Verified (ISBN 0205412017; 1st ed. 1994).

**(b) Use — the sequence claim is supported; the mechanism and the evidential framing are not.
Severity: MEDIUM (Finding 3).**

[42]: "Two findings from the group-development literature are built into the team's expectations
rather than discovered by surprise. First, **open disagreement tends to arrive before mature trust,
not after it, because people risk conflict only in a room they believe will hold it**; the first
honestly-processed disagreement is usually a milestone, not a malfunction."

*The sequence is right, and Wheelan supports it better than Tuckman does.* Wheelan's Integrated
Model of Group Development runs: Stage 1 Dependency and Inclusion → **Stage 2 Counterdependency and
Fight** → **Stage 3 Trust and Structure** → Stage 4 Work and Productivity. Stage 3 is literally
named "Trust and Structure" and it follows the fight stage. The author has picked exactly the right
source for this claim and could lean on it harder by naming the stages.

Two problems:

1. **The causal clause is the author's, not the literature's.** Neither Tuckman nor Wheelan says
   conflict arrives *because* members believe the room will hold it. In both models the Stage-2
   fight is **counterdependency** — resistance to the leader and to group influence, and struggle
   over goals, roles, and authority — and successfully working *through* it is what generates trust.
   The paper has the arrow pointing the other way. This is not a large error, and the practical
   guidance ("a milestone, not a malfunction") survives it intact; but it is presented as a finding
   of the literature and it isn't one.
2. **Tuckman 1965 is not a "finding" in the sense implied.** It is a narrative review of roughly
   fifty studies, most of them therapy and T-group studies, which Tuckman himself framed as a
   hypothesis requiring empirical test; he coined the four labels only in the article's summary, and
   later added "adjourning" (Tuckman & Jensen, 1977). Subsequent historical reviews describe the
   model's empirical validation as thin and note that groups do not reliably move through the stages
   linearly. Wheelan's IMGD is the better-evidenced of the two and the paper should let it carry the
   weight.

**Fix.** Recast as: *the group-development literature places conflict before consolidated trust —
Wheelan's Stage 2 (Counterdependency and Fight) precedes Stage 3 (Trust and Structure) — though the
models explain that conflict as counterdependency rather than as trust-enabled risk-taking; on our
reading, in a room where safety is explicitly built first, the first honestly-processed disagreement
should be read as a milestone.* That keeps the practical claim, credits the literature for the
sequence only, and marks the mechanism as the author's inference.

**LOW.** "Fifty years"/"two findings" framing aside, the paper never cites Tuckman's stage names.
Naming them would let a reader check the claim; as written the citation is a bare gesture at fn 11.

## 4. M. Scott Peck, *The Different Drum* (1987)

**(a) Bibliographic details — CORRECT.** *The Different Drum: Community Making and Peace*, New York:
Simon and Schuster, 1987. Verified. Bibliography [81] and fn 12 agree.

**(b) Use — accurate, and well hedged.**

[42]: "the sweetest-looking room can be the lowest-trust room in the building, its harmony
maintained by hiding differences, **the state one classic account names pseudocommunity**."

This is a faithful description. Peck's pseudocommunity is defined by conflict avoidance: members are
extremely pleasant with one another and avoid all disagreement. The paper's gloss matches the
source. The hedge "one classic account names" is exactly the right register and should not be
charged against the author.

**LOW — two refinements.**

1. *Peck's pseudocommunity is a normal first stage, not only a counterfeit.* In Peck's four-stage
   model (pseudocommunity → chaos → emptiness → true community) every group passes through
   pseudocommunity; it is where community-making starts, not a pathology some groups fall into. The
   paper's "the counterfeit" reads as the latter. A team trained to expect pseudocommunity as
   *stage one* will treat it with patience; a team trained to see it as a counterfeit may go hunting
   for it. Given [49]'s excellent instinct ("A young room steady at its young rungs is a healthy
   room"), the stage reading is the one the paper actually wants.
2. *Peck's next stage is chaos, which is the paper's first finding.* [42]'s two findings are, in
   Peck's own model, consecutive stages: pseudocommunity is escaped *through* chaos — open
   disagreement. The paper cites two sources for two findings and misses that one source supplies
   both, in order. That is a strengthening cross-reference sitting unused.

**LOW — evidential status.** [42] introduces both items as "Two findings from the group-development
literature." Peck's model is an experiential, workshop-derived account from his community-building
practice, not empirical group-development research. The "classic account" hedge partly covers this,
but the framing sentence does not. Recommend distinguishing the two sources' evidential weight in
the framing sentence rather than in the hedge.

## 5. Helen Cameron, Deborah Bhatti, Catherine Duce, James Sweeney, Clare Watkins,
## *Talking About God in Practice* (SCM, 2010)

**(a) Bibliographic details — CORRECT, including author order.** Verified: Helen Cameron, Deborah
Bhatti, Catherine Duce, James Sweeney, and Clare Watkins, *Talking About God in Practice:
Theological Action Research and Practical Theology*, London: SCM Press, 2010 (ISBN 9780334043638;
SCM is an imprint of Hymns Ancient & Modern). Bibliography [70] and fn 15 both correct.

**(b) Gloss — ACCURATE.** fn 15: the book "treats a community's own working theology as data to be
surfaced rather than noise to be filtered out." That is a fair, compact statement of the ARCS
project's central contribution: the **four voices of theology** — normative, formal, espoused, and
operant — and a method for accessing the *implicit* theologies embedded within practices. The
author has read this correctly. Good citation.

**(c) MEDIUM — Finding 6: the method's own remedy for the paper's acknowledged conflict goes
unmentioned.**

Theological action research as Cameron et al. set it out was developed through the ARCS project as a
**partnership**: an outside research team working with a practising community, so that the
conversation between the four voices is brokered by insider–outsider pairs rather than conducted by
one person who is simultaneously designer, leader, and observer.

The pilot has the designer as sole observer, and the paper knows it — [59] is admirably direct: "the
observer who designed the instruments is also the shepherd who loves the room, a conflict I have
tried to bind with covenant and disconfirmation rather than pretend to escape." That candour earns
real credit and I do not charge the design itself.

What is missing is one step: Cameron et al. are cited as the *warrant* for the method, and their
method contains the standard structural answer to exactly the conflict [59] names. A reviewer who
knows the book will ask why. One sentence — that a Formation Companion team plus a signed
measurement covenant is the pilot's substitute for the ARCS insider–outsider structure, and that a
future cycle could add an external partner — closes it.

**LOW.** The paper's own emphasis at [9] on first-person voice, and its refusal to hide the
researcher behind the passive, is very much in the spirit of this literature. Worth one clause
saying so, since it converts a methodological liability into a cited-and-warranted choice.

**(b) Use — ACCURATE.** fn 15: "The tradition begins with Kurt Lewin." That is the standard and

begins with" is the right level of claim. Nothing to fix.

## 7. Trust literature: Mayer/Davis/Schoorman (1995); Rousseau/Sitkin/Burt/Camerer (1998);
## De Jong/Dirks/Gillespie (2016); Lewicki/Tomlinson/Gillespie (2006)

**(a) Bibliographic details — ALL FOUR CORRECT.** Verified individually:

| Source | Verified |
|---|---|
| Mayer, Davis & Schoorman | *Academy of Management Review* 20, no. 3 (1995): 709–734 ✓ |
| Rousseau, Sitkin, Burt & Camerer | *Academy of Management Review* 23, no. 3 (1998): 393–404 ✓ |
| De Jong, Dirks & Gillespie | *Journal of Applied Psychology* 101, no. 8 (2016): 1134–1150 ✓ |
| Lewicki, Tomlinson & Gillespie | *Journal of Management* 32, no. 6 (2006): 991–1022 ✓ |

Author orders, article titles, and the "de Jong" / "De Jong" capitalization (lowercase in the
footnote's running order, capitalized at the head of the inverted bibliography entry) are all handled
correctly. This is careful work.

**(b) Uses.**

**Mayer, Davis & Schoorman — ACCURATE, no finding.** fn 10: "On trust as willingness to be
vulnerable." That is precisely their definition (p. 712): the willingness of a party to be
vulnerable to the actions of another, based on the expectation that the other will perform a
particular action important to the trustor, irrespective of the ability to monitor or control. The
paper cites the right paper for the right claim in the right words.

**Rousseau et al. — ACCURATE for the vulnerability claim.** Their cross-discipline definition —
trust as a psychological state comprising the intention to accept vulnerability based on positive
expectations of another's intentions or behaviour — supports the same clause.

*LOW:* Rousseau et al. propose their **own** four-fold typology (deterrence-based, calculus-based,
relational, institution-based). Bundling them in fn 10 immediately after the Lewicki & Bunker
three-stage model implies the two converge on one staged account, which they do not. One clause
separating "the definitional consensus" from "the stage models" would fix it.

**De Jong, Dirks & Gillespie — ACCURATE.** fn 9: "On the general trust-and-performance evidence at
team scale." Correct and appropriately generic: 112 independent studies, 7,763 teams, intrateam
trust positively related to team performance at ρ ≈ .30, robust after controlling for trust in
leader and past performance, and across cognitive and affective trust dimensions.

*LOW:* the outcome variable is *task performance in work teams*. There is no performance outcome in
a formation cohort. So long as the footnote stays at "general evidence at team scale," this is fine;
if the body ever leans on it to argue that trust-building *produces* formation outcomes, that is an
undeclared cross-domain transfer. I could not locate the exact anchor sentence for fn 9 (the anchor
falls somewhere in the observation-architecture material, most plausibly [36]) — flagging as
unverified, and recommending the author check that the sentence it hangs on claims no more than the
meta-analysis establishes.

**MEDIUM — Finding 2: [41]'s "fifty years," the uncredited origin of the three-stage typology, and
the "updated in" gloss on LTG 2006.**

[41]: "**Fifty years of that literature describes trust developing through three levels:
calculus-based** (I behave because the structure holds me), **knowledge-based** (I trust because I
know you now), **and identification-based** (your values have become mine)."

Three separate problems in one sentence:

1. **The date is off by roughly twenty years.** The three-stage typology is not fifty years old. It
   enters the literature with **Shapiro, Sheppard & Cheraskin, "Business on a Handshake,"
   *Negotiation Journal* 8 (1992): 365–377**, as deterrence-based / knowledge-based /
   identification-based trust. Lewicki & Bunker (1996) adapted it, reconceptualizing
   deterrence-based as *calculus-based* — which is the exact vocabulary the paper uses. So the model
   is ~34 years old in 2026, not 50. Trust research *broadly* is older (Deutsch 1958, Rotter 1967),
   but "that literature," with "that" pointing at the three-level model, is the narrower claim.
2. **The originating source is uncited.** The paper uses Lewicki & Bunker's renaming without
   crediting Shapiro, Sheppard & Cheraskin for the underlying typology. Adding one line to the
   bibliography fixes this and costs nothing.
3. **LTG 2006 is glossed as an endorsement when its burden is a caveat.** fn 10 says the stage model
   is "updated in" Lewicki, Tomlinson & Gillespie 2006. Literally true — it is Lewicki's own later
   review. But that review's actual argument is that most trust research has taken a static
   "snapshot" view and that *limited attention has been given to conceptualizing and measuring trust
   development over time*; it catalogues transformational/stage models as one of four approaches
   rather than confirming them. A reviewer who opens the citation will find the paper's own best
   caveat sitting inside a footnote used as a warrant.

**Fix, and it strengthens the paper.** Recast as: *Since the early 1990s a strand of this literature
has described trust developing through three levels — deterrence- or calculus-based, knowledge-based,
and identification-based (Shapiro, Sheppard & Cheraskin 1992; Lewicki & Bunker 1996) — though
Lewicki, Tomlinson & Gillespie (2006) note that trust development over time remains
under-conceptualized and under-measured, which is part of why this pilot registers its trust-stage
mapping as a research trail rather than a settled instrument.* That is more accurate **and** it
converts a vulnerability into a declared contribution.

**Credit where due — the extension is declared, and declared well.** [41]'s proposal that a room's
*unprompted vocabulary* indexes its trust stage is a genuinely novel measurement claim; nothing in
the trust literature validates linguistic markers for calculus/knowledge/identification trust. The
paper does not pretend otherwise. It says "**this project** reads the answers as a staged
vocabulary," keeps the mapping "in house words on **its side of the paper**," and lists the mapping's
failure as a disconfirmation condition at [58] ("would break **the proposed** mapping…, which this
project has begun registering as a formal research trail"). Under the three fidelity questions, that
is a model extension: acknowledged, named as the author's, and falsifiable. It should be recorded as
a strength, not a finding.

## 8. James K. A. Smith, *You Are What You Love* and *Desiring the Kingdom*

**(a) Bibliographic details — CORRECT, both.**
- *Desiring the Kingdom: Worship, Worldview, and Cultural Formation*, Grand Rapids: Baker Academic,
  2009 (vol. 1 of the Cultural Liturgies trilogy). Verified.
- *You Are What You Love: The Spiritual Power of Habit*, Grand Rapids: Brazos, 2016. Verified.

Bibliography [85]–[86] and fn 4 agree.

**(b) Use — ACCURATE.** [12], third principle: "form desire through practice, not information through
content: the unit of the curriculum is not the lesson but the repeatable practice, **because human
beings are formed less by what they are taught to think than by what they are repeatedly trained to
love.**"

This is a fair and well-put rendering of Smith's core anthropology: humans are desiring and
liturgical animals rather than primarily thinking things, and thick, repeated practices — malls,
stadiums, and universities as much as sanctuaries — form what we love before they inform what we
believe. *Desiring the Kingdom* contains a chapter titled "From Thinking Things to Liturgical
Animals." Citing the popular volume in the footnote with the academic volume as "see also" is the
right ordering for a practitioner audience.

**LOW.** "Not information through content" is a sharper either/or than Smith holds. He is arguing
against an *intellectualist reduction* of the person, not against teaching; he has been at pains
elsewhere to say he is not recommending anti-intellectualism, and his own books are works of content.
The paper's own design does not actually enact the dichotomy either — [29] has six minutes of
teaching on Romans 10:17 before the practice. Softening "not information through content" to
"formation before information" or "practice as the unit, with teaching in service of it" would match
both Smith and the paper's own Wednesday.

## 9. Dietrich Bonhoeffer, *Life Together* (1954, tr. Doberstein)

**(a) Bibliographic details — one anachronism. Severity: LOW.**

Translator (John W. Doberstein), title, year (1954), and city (New York) are all correct. German
original: *Gemeinsames Leben* (1939).

The publisher is not. The 1954 first English edition was published by **Harper & Brothers**.
**Harper & Row did not exist until 30 April 1962**, when Harper & Brothers merged with Row, Peterson
& Company. "New York: Harper & Row, 1954" is therefore impossible as printed, though it is an
extremely common error — later Harper & Row printings carry the 1954 copyright, and many
bibliographies reproduce the mismatch.

**Fix, pick one:** (i) *New York: Harper & Brothers, 1954* if citing the first edition; (ii) *New
York: Harper & Row, 1976* (or whichever printing the author owns) if citing a later printing; or
(iii) — preferable for a JSFSC readership — the critical edition: *Life Together and Prayerbook of
the Bible*, Dietrich Bonhoeffer Works vol. 5, trans. Daniel W. Bloesch and James H. Burtness
(Minneapolis: Fortress, 1996). The Doberstein translation remains perfectly legitimate to cite; DBW
5 is simply the scholarly standard now.

**(b) Use — ACCURATE.** fn 13: "On confession within community as the ground of honest fellowship."
*Life Together*'s five chapters are Community; The Day with Others; The Day Alone; Ministry; and
**Confession and Communion** — the book culminates in exactly this claim, that a gospel community is
made of sinners who have confessed. The gloss matches the source, and it is well matched to the
pilot's first-quarter "confession and restoration" night at [23].

**LOW.** Bonhoeffer appears only in a footnote and the bibliography; he is never named in the body.
For a paper whose first-quarter arc is built on confession, and whose central risk is the
sweet-looking room that hides its differences, *Life Together*'s warning against the pious fellowship
in which no one may be a sinner is a stronger warrant than the footnote lets it be. This is an
opportunity note, not a fault.

## 10. Greg L. Hawkins and Cally Parkinson, *Move* (2011)

**(a) Bibliographic details — CORRECT.** *Move: What 1,000 Churches Reveal about Spiritual Growth*,
Grand Rapids: Zondervan, 2011 (published with the Willow Creek Association). Verified. Bibliography
[74] and fn 13 agree.

**(b) What it actually found — for the record, since it is frequently misdescribed.** *Move* reports
the second wave of the **REVEAL Spiritual Life Survey**, begun at Willow Creek Community Church in
2004 and extended over six years to over a thousand churches and roughly a quarter-million
respondents. Its principal results:

- A four-segment **spiritual continuum**: Exploring Christ, Growing in Christ, Close to Christ,
  Christ-Centered.
- **Church activity and involvement do not predict spiritual growth.** This is the headline and the
  one most often garbled.
- **Personal spiritual practices — above all reflection on Scripture — are the strongest catalyst**
  of movement along the continuum.
- The church's role is **not** uniform: it matters most in the early segments and recedes as people
  move along the continuum.

Three common misdescriptions to avoid: that *Move* found "church programs don't work" (it found
activity does not predict growth, and that the church is decisive early); that it is longitudinal (it
is cross-sectional self-report survey data); and attributing to *Move* findings that belong to the
earlier *Reveal: Where Are You?* (2007).

**(c) MEDIUM — Finding 5 (shared with Schwarz): the "instrument landscape" framing.**

fn 13: "For the instrument landscape the companion article surveys, see e.g. Greg L. Hawkins and
Cally Parkinson, *Move*…; Christian A. Schwarz, *Natural Church Development*…"

Neither book is an instrument for measuring **individual spiritual maturity** in the sense the
companion article's affective-taxonomy argument requires. REVEAL/*Move* is a **congregational
segmentation survey**: it sorts attenders into four attitudinal segments from self-report and reports
catalysts of movement. NCD is an **organizational church-health** instrument: thirty questions each
across eight quality characteristics, aimed at diagnosing a congregation's "minimum factor," not an
individual's internalization. Presenting them as the maturity-instrument landscape is a category
slip.

Two qualifications in the paper's favour. First, the footnote says "see e.g.," which is a light
claim, and it explicitly defers the surveying to the companion article — so the burden properly sits
there, not here. Second, both books *are* self-report instruments in wide church use, which is
exactly the paper's point about where the field's tools sit. The fix is small: say what they are.
E.g. *"…for the congregational-survey landscape the companion article surveys — instruments that
measure congregational segmentation (Hawkins and Parkinson) and organizational church health
(Schwarz) rather than individual internalization…"*. That turns a vulnerability into supporting
evidence for the paper's own thesis.

**LOW.** Since the footnote is the only place *Move* appears, and it makes no substantive claim about
what *Move* found, there is no misdescription in this paper to correct. The risk lives in the
companion article. If that article characterizes *Move*'s findings, the four bullets above are the
statements to check it against.

## 12. Robert Wuthnow, *Sharing the Journey* (1994)

**(a) Bibliographic details — CORRECT.** *Sharing the Journey: Support Groups and America's New Quest
for Community*, New York: Free Press, 1994. Verified. (A later paperback bears the variant subtitle
*Support Groups and the Quest for a New Community*; the paper cites the 1994 hardcover subtitle,
which is correct for that edition.)

**(b) Use — ACCURATE, with a caution.** fn 9: "On the tendency of church small groups to drift toward
comfortable mutual support absent load."

This is a fair paraphrase. Wuthnow's three-year study — a team of fifteen scholars, over a thousand
Americans — found roughly 40 percent of the population in small groups of some kind, and his central
critique is of a **"domesticated" spirituality**: shallow, accommodating to secular therapeutic
culture, offering comfort and self-esteem while making few demands; he challenges small groups to
serve a wider community and to recover a deeper vision of the sacred. The paper's gloss captures
this.

**LOW — two cautions.** (i) Wuthnow's fieldwork dates from roughly 1990–91; the small-group landscape
he describes is thirty-five years old, and the paper cites it in the present tense as a live
"tendency." A dating clause would help. (ii) His critique is of the American *support-group movement*
broadly — recovery groups, Bible studies, self-help — and is a sociological account of the
movement's effect on the sacred, not specifically a finding that church groups soften *when load is
absent*. The "absent load" clause is the author's framing laid over Wuthnow's finding. It is a
reasonable reading; it is not Wuthnow's sentence.

**LOW.** Like Bonhoeffer, Wuthnow appears only in a footnote and never in the body, though his
finding is arguably the sharpest available warrant for the pilot's entire "response to load" vital
sign and for [42]'s pseudocommunity worry. Under-used rather than misused.

**MEDIUM — Finding 7: Romans 10:17 at [29].**

"The teaching turns to Romans 10:17 and the hearing chain, six minutes, short on purpose. The whole
room then walks the hearing practice through Mark 1:14–20 together."

Two points, one small and one not.

*Small:* the "chain" proper is Rom 10:14–15 (sending → preaching → hearing → believing → calling);
v. 17 is Paul's summary of it. Citing v. 17 alone for "the hearing chain" is slightly under-cited;
10:14–17 is the unit.

*Not small:* in context, Romans 10 is about **the proclamation of the gospel to Israel and the
nations**, and the "hearing" that produces faith is hearing the preached word of Christ. It is an
evangelistic-proclamation text. The pilot uses it as the teaching text for a six-step **personal
Scripture-hearing practice** whose final step is telling someone what was heard. That is a different
sense of "hearing," and a JSFSC reviewer with any exegetical instinct will say so. The practice does
not need the verse to be about personal discernment — the sixth "tell" step actually recovers the
proclamation sense rather nicely, since the practice ends with someone speaking what was heard to
another person. But the paper should either make that connection explicitly or pick a different
anchor text.

**LOW — 1 Cor 2:16 at [12].** Of the three texts offered for "a competence Scripture treats as
belonging to the gathered body itself," 1 Cor 14:26–29 and Acts 15:28 are strong and do the work.
1 Cor 2:16 ("but we have the mind of Christ") is plural, which is the paper's point, but in context
2:14–16 is contrasting the spiritual person with the natural person rather than describing a corporate
discernment procedure. It is the one a reviewer would poke. Consider leading with 14:26–29.

**LOW — Jer 17:9 at [6].** The paper's use is precise (opacity only) and I do not charge it. But note
that Jeremiah's own answer to "who can know it?" comes in v. 10: *God* searches the heart. The verse
establishes the opacity premise; it does not by itself warrant the community as the remedy. Since
[6] correctly attributes the community move to the companion article rather than to Jeremiah, the
paper is safe — but a reader pressing on the proof text will land here, and a clause acknowledging
v. 10 would disarm it.

**LOW — Eph 6:4 at [16].** The verse addresses *fathers* specifically; the paper's claim is about
"parents." Deut 6:6–7 carries the broader claim without qualification, so the pairing works. Trivial.
