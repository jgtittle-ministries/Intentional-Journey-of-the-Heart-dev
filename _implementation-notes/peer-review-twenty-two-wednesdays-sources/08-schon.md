# Source-Fidelity Review — Donald A. Schön

**Paper:** "Twenty-Two Wednesdays as a Fellowship of the Heart," draft v2.3 (2026-08-18)
**Reviewer's remit:** whether this paper represents *my* work faithfully — accuracy, scope, and honest attribution of extension.

A preliminary word. I am cited exactly once in this manuscript, in footnote 14, in support of one small
move — the teach-back — and I appear once in the bibliography. That is a modest footprint, and I want to
say at the outset that a paper which uses a source lightly and does not pad its warrant list is already
doing something right. My findings below are mostly about a body of work the paper does *not* invoke and,
I think, badly needs: the distinction Chris Argyris and I drew between espoused theory and theory-in-use.
That is not a complaint about misuse. It is an argument that the author has cited the wrong half of me.

---

## 1. What the paper gets right about my work

**The refusal of the passive voice [9].** "When the researcher is inside the room, the researcher is part
of the instrument, and a paper that hid its author behind the passive voice would be concealing the very
thing a reader needs in order to weigh it." I spent a good deal of my life arguing against what I called
Technical Rationality — the model in which professional practice is the application of research-based
theory by a practitioner who stands outside the situation. This paragraph is a practitioner declining that
model out loud, and declining it for the right reason: not because rigor is unavailable to him, but because
the standard rigor would require him to hide the most consequential fact about the study. The next sentence
— "That is not a hedge against rigor but a different kind of rigor" — is, near enough, the argument of my
1983 preface. The author is not citing me for it. He could.

**The situation that talks back [5].** "We solved this the way most of this curriculum got built: the
problem became a practice. … I have stopped being surprised when the constraint turns out to be the
curriculum." This is the best single paragraph in the paper from where I sit, and it is a clean instance of
what I described as the practitioner's reflective conversation with a situation: the four-o'clock classroom
resists, the practitioner reframes the resistance as material, and the reframing produces something —
families who build a room together settling into it differently — that no prior theory predicted. Note
especially the words "I did not plan that." That is the *back talk* of the situation. It is the raw
material of reflection, and the author knows how to notice it.

**The public reporting of two failed designs [40].** "The mechanism was redesigned twice in the field, and
the failures are instructive, so I will report them." Then the two failures, named, with the diagnosis
("revealed criteria are still criteria"). This is reflection-on-action performed publicly, and it is a far
better instance of my argument than the teach-back the paper actually cites me for. If the author wants a
Schönian exhibit, this is it.

**Coaching by exemplar rather than by rule [38].** "worked examples of what each reading looks like at each
rung sit on two teaching pages at the front of the pack, so a companion who has never read a word of the
underlying research can still mark honestly." Learning to *see* — to recognize a case as an instance of
something — is not transmitted by definitions. It is transmitted by exemplars, demonstration, and
correction on live attempts. The author has arrived at that independently and built it into the instrument.

**The rotation architecture as a practicum [19].** "Entrusting leadership now, with a full team present and
a family helping, is the apprenticeship for leading later, when no adult will be down the hall." A setting
in which a learner does the real thing at reduced risk, with the practice available to be attempted before
it is understood, is the practicum in the strict sense. The design has the structure. My finding 4 below
concerns what the structure is missing, but the instinct is sound and I want it credited before I complain.

**The honest terminal claim [59].** "The honest claim available at the end of this pilot will not be 'this
works' but 'this ran, here is everything it recorded against the predictions it published, and here is the
revised design.'" Appropriately sized. I have nothing to add.

**And one negative credit, since the brief asks me to check before I charge.** I was told to look for my
name near the paper's action-research framing. It is not there. The action-research footnote (15) cites
Lewin and Cameron et al. and does not name me; my footnote is 14 and is confined to the teach-back. The
author has not borrowed my prestige for a tradition I do not belong to. Finding 2 below is about a risk of
adjacency, not an act of conflation, and I have graded it accordingly.

## 2. Findings

### Finding 1 — The teach-back is not reflection-on-action, and "consolidation" is not my claim
**Severity: HIGH**

**Text.** [20]: "The round also carries a third movement for the leader alone, the teach-back: 'if I were
to teach tonight's process to someone, here is what I would tell them,' **a consolidation move the learning
literature would recognize as a reflective practice** and the room experiences as humility." Footnote 14:
"Schön's argument is that skilled practitioners know more than they can say, their competence residing in a
tacit knowing-in-action **that reflection afterward makes available for examination and teaching.** The
teach-back asks exactly this: the evening's leader states, to an imagined learner, the process just run by
feel."

**What is wrong.** Three things, of ascending seriousness.

*(a) "Consolidation" is somebody else's finding.* Consolidation is a claim about memory and retention. I
never studied it, never measured it, and would not know how to. The effect the author is describing —
that preparing to teach, or explaining a procedure aloud to an imagined learner, deepens the explainer's
own grasp — is real and well evidenced, but it belongs to the self-explanation and learning-by-teaching
literatures, not to me. As the sentence stands, one clause makes a psychological claim about memory and the
footnote hangs my name under it. That is the wrong warrant on the right observation.

*(b) The gloss makes my argument too optimistic.* "Reflection afterward makes [tacit knowing] available for
examination and teaching" reads as though reflection were a readout — as though the tacit becomes explicit
intact, and what the practitioner then says is what he in fact knows. My position is nearly the opposite,
and it is the part of the argument I most cared about. A practitioner's description of his own
knowing-in-action is a *construction*, made after the fact, in words, for an audience — and it is likely to
distort what it describes. Practitioners routinely give accounts of their procedure that do not match the
procedure an observer sees them perform. That is not deceit; it is the normal condition of skilled action.
So the teach-back does not deliver the leader's knowing. It delivers the leader's *account* of his knowing,
which is a different object and, for this paper's purposes, a much more interesting one — because the gap
between the account and the performance is exactly what a design like this ought to be watching.

*(c) The placement in my framework is wrong, and the two terms need separating in the text.* Let me state
the distinction plainly, since the paper does not.

- **Reflection-in-action** happens in the *action-present* — the stretch of time in which doing can still
  change the outcome. It is triggered by surprise: something in the situation does not go as the
  practitioner's knowing-in-action expected. He thinks about what he is doing *while* doing it,
  restructures his strategy, his understanding of the phenomenon, or his framing of the problem, and tests
  the restructuring by an on-the-spot experiment. The whole thing may take seconds. The leader who feels
  the room go tight and abandons his planned question is reflecting in action.

- **Reflection-on-action** is retrospective. After the fact, we think back on what we did in order to
  discover how our knowing-in-action may have contributed to the outcome. Its subject matter is a
  *particular episode that has already happened*, and its characteristic yield is a revised understanding
  of the case.

Now place the teach-back. It is retrospective, so it is not reflection-in-action. But it is not
reflection-on-action either, because its subject matter is not the episode — it is the *procedure*. "If I
were to teach tonight's process to someone, here is what I would tell them" invites the leader to state a
generalized method to a hypothetical learner. Nothing in the prompt requires him to attend to anything that
actually happened in that room on that Wednesday. Nothing puts a surprise in front of him. Absent surprise,
there is nothing for reflection to bite on, and the most likely output is a fluent recitation of the design
as designed. In my vocabulary the teach-back is a **description of knowing-in-action** — articulation. It
is one useful *input* to reflection-on-action and it is not itself the reflective move.

And here is the sting the author will appreciate, because it is the most useful thing I can give him: what
the teach-back elicits is, almost by construction, the leader's **espoused theory** of the evening. The
paper has built an instrument that produces espoused theory weekly, in public, in the leader's own voice,
and has not noticed what it has. That is a considerable asset if it is named correctly — see Finding 3.

**What would fix it.**
1. Cut my name from the "consolidation" clause and cite the self-explanation / learning-by-teaching
   literature there instead.
2. Cite me — accurately — for the round's *first two* questions, "what went well and what they would do
   differently." Those are addressed to the episode, they are retrospective, and they are genuine
   reflection-on-action. That is where I belong in this paper.
3. Name the teach-back as what it is: an articulation of espoused procedure, valuable as a check against
   observed performance. One sentence.
4. If the author wants the teach-back to *become* reflection-on-action, it needs one more clause, and the
   clause is cheap: "…and here is one thing tonight that did not go the way I expected, and what I did about
   it in the moment." That imports the surprise, and as a bonus it produces the paper's only trace of
   reflection-*in*-action, of which there is currently none anywhere in the design.

### Finding 2 — Two traditions sit adjacent and are not distinguished
**Severity: MEDIUM**

**Text.** [9]: "This is action research: the practitioner stands inside the practice being studied, the
inquiry aims at improving that practice rather than holding it still, and looking and doing alternate in
cycles." [44]: "This is the action research named at the outset, carried into family formation." Footnote
15 cites Lewin (1946) and Cameron et al. (2010). My footnote is 14, immediately preceding.

**What is wrong.** As I said above, the paper does not put my name on action research, and I credit that.
But two things create a real risk of the reader doing it for him. First, the sentence at [9] is a
*composite*: "looking and doing alternate in cycles" is Lewin's spiral, while "the practitioner stands
inside the practice being studied" and the "different kind of rigor" that follows are the epistemological
claim — my territory, not Lewin's. The two are cited to Lewin alone. Second, footnotes 14 and 15 are
adjacent, and I am the paper's only reflective-practice source; a reader who knows the field will read the
pair as one citation cluster.

The distinction is worth a sentence because the traditions warrant different things. Lewin's action
research is a *method for producing social change* — iterative planning, action, and fact-finding, with the
community as participant. My project was an *epistemology of practice*: an account of what competent
practitioners already know and how they learn, offered as a critique of Technical Rationality. One
authorizes a research design; the other authorizes a claim about the standing of practitioner knowledge.
Fusing them lets a paper draw the rigor of the first from the reputation of the second, and vice versa.
This paper has not done that. It has left the door open.

**What would fix it.** In [9], split the sentence and cite separately: Lewin (and Cameron et al. for the
theological form) for the cyclical inquiry design; me, explicitly, for the claim that a practitioner inside
the situation possesses a form of knowing that the outside stance cannot access, and that this constitutes
a different rigor rather than a deficit of it. That is an *upgrade* to the paper's warrant, not a
subtraction — the author is currently under-citing me where I actually apply.

### Finding 3 — No mechanism for detecting divergence between espoused theory and theory-in-use
**Severity: HIGH**

**Text.** [9]: "I designed this, I changed it after it failed in the field, I sit with the families to ask
permission, and I am the leader whose leadership the design intends to move further and further into the
background." [59]: "the observer who designed the instruments is also the shepherd who loves the room, a
conflict I have tried to bind with covenant and disconfirmation rather than pretend to escape." [44]: "The
design accepts what that costs, since an observer inside the room cannot claim the distance of one outside
it." [38], the weekly call's fixed liturgy: "look back (the signs first, then the feedback round's echo),
look at the flock (every follow-up closed or carried…), look ahead (next week's session walked in advance
and its roles assigned)."

**First, the credit, because the brief tells me to check before I charge and there is a great deal to
check.** The disconfirmation conditions at [53]–[58] are a genuine and unusual discipline. Publishing in
advance what would count as being wrong is a form of public testing of assumptions, and Argyris and I
argued that public testing is precisely what distinguishes a Model II theory-in-use from the defensive
Model I pattern that dominates ordinary practice. The author has done it voluntarily, at cost, in a setting
where nobody would have required it of him. [59] then names the founder conflict without flinching. The
"discrepancy as data" architecture at [44] is built so that sources can contradict each other. None of that
is decoration, and I do not want the finding below to read as though I had not seen it.

**What is missing.** Every one of the disconfirmation conditions at [54]–[58] is stated in the design's own
vocabulary and read by the design's own instruments: the aged rungs, the source discrepancies, break
survival, teen-led nights, the trust vocabulary. They can tell the author that the curriculum is failing on
its own terms and prompt him to adjust the curriculum. That is **single-loop learning** — error detected,
strategy corrected, governing variables untouched. Not one of the five conditions can tell him that the
*governing variables* are wrong: that "nothing is ever asked" [21] produces a room in which nothing ever
costs anything; that "the leader publicly stays a sheep" [20] is a form of leader-centrality wearing
humility's clothes; that the three vital signs are the wrong three. **Double-loop learning** — the
questioning of the governing variables themselves — has no route into this design.

And here the failure mode our work predicts arrives with unusual precision, so let me set it out.

The whole architecture is an account of what the program *believes it does*. That is an espoused theory,
and a very carefully constructed one. What the program actually does — the theory reconstructable from its
performance — is a separate object, and the finding Argyris and I kept running into is that people cannot
report their own theories-in-use. Not will not: cannot. The discrepancy is systematically invisible to the
person who holds it, and it is invisible in direct proportion to the sincerity of the espousal. Theory-in-use
failures are not a failure of intention. They happen to people of excellent intention. This matters here
because the author's stated safeguard at [59] is "covenant and disconfirmation" — and a covenant binds
*intention*, which is the one thing not at risk.

Nor is there an independent channel. The designer is the lead observer. The scribe's mapping happens "on
its own side of the paper" [28], [41]. The Thursday call [38] is run by the team he selected and formed —
"How such companions are selected and formed is a successor article's subject" [36] — so their
theories-in-use were shaped by the same source as his. There is no eye in the system that was not
calibrated by the designer.

Three candidate divergences the design cannot currently see, offered as illustrations rather than
accusations:

- **[21], "Nothing is ever asked."** Espoused: no demand; practices, leadership, even the faith commitment
  are offered and left standing. Consider the theory-in-use. The leader goes first every week [20]. Parents
  go first in every practice [16]. A teenager's second running is "led by a teenager when they are a willing
  volunteer" [19] — in a room of eight or ten families where five such nights are scheduled and the design
  publicly depends on them. A structurally undeclinable offer is a demand with better manners. I am not
  asserting that this is happening; I am observing that if it were, no instrument in the pilot would
  register it, because the only channel through which a participant could say "I felt I had to" is a page
  they hand back to the man who leads the room.

- **[20], the Leader Feedback Round.** "receiving it looks like strength because it has only ever been
  modeled as strength." Espoused: the leader models receiving correction. Alternative theory-in-use: a
  round designed by the leader, opened by the leader every week, with a fixed two-question shape and a
  positive item first, teaches the room the *acceptable form and content* of correction. Both produce the
  same observable — a room that gives feedback and a leader who receives it warmly — and the design cannot
  distinguish them. The test that would distinguish them is simple and is not in the paper: **has anything
  material in the design ever changed because of something said in the round?** Note that the paper's two
  documented design changes [40] came from "a colleague's honest reaction, and mine on reflection" — from
  outside the round. That is one data point, and it points the wrong way.

- **[46], the permission asymmetry.** "the team never asks permission to pay attention… if it is paper a
  participant touches, it is asked for; if it is a shepherd's eye held under a signed covenant, it is care."
  Espoused: a bright line between measurement and care. In use: the shepherd's eye is being recorded on a
  standardized debrief page, against pre-printed rungs, with counts and calibration exemplars, and promoted
  after three consecutive weeks of *seen* [37]–[38]. That is an instrument. The author's line is defensible
  and I take his pastoral reasoning seriously, but the design has no way to learn that it drew the line in
  the wrong place, because the only people who could tell it are on the instrument's side of the paper.

**What would fix it.** Three additions, all cheap, all in the paper's own idiom:

1. **A surprise line on the debrief page.** One line, added to the existing ten-minute sheet: *"Where did
   tonight differ from the plan, and what did I do in the moment?"* This gives the design its only trace of
   reflection-in-action, and surprises are the raw material of double-loop learning. Over twenty-two weeks
   the accumulated surprises are the most likely place a governing variable will show itself to be wrong.

2. **A left-hand-column exercise, once a quarter, for the lead companion.** On the right, what was actually
   said in one hard moment of the quarter; on the left, what he was thinking and not saying. Argyris and I
   used this precisely to surface theory-in-use, it takes about twenty minutes, and it does not require the
   author to have read a word of the organizational-learning literature to run.

3. **One outsider, two hours a quarter.** Someone who did not design the curriculum, does not lead in the
   room, and has no stake in its success. Give them three debrief sheets and one scribed self-read per
   quarter, and one question: *"Judging only by what this program does, what does it appear to believe?"*
   Then set the answer beside the six commitments at [16]–[21]. Where the two lists differ, that is the
   finding. This is the entire detection mechanism, and it would let the paper claim something it cannot
   currently claim: that its account of itself has been tested against its behavior.

I would put it this way to the author. He has written a paper whose central methodological virtue is that
disagreement between sources is treated as data rather than noise [32], [44]. He has built that discipline
for every relationship in the room except one — the relationship between what the program says about itself
and what the program does. That is the one where disagreement is most likely and least detectable.

### Finding 4 — The Leader Feedback Round is missing three conditions the practicum requires
**Severity: MEDIUM**

**Text.** [20]: "Every session closes with the Leader Feedback Round: the evening's leader, beginning with
me, tells the whole room what went well and what they would do differently, then invites the room to answer
the same two questions about the evening."

**Credit first.** The round satisfies the practicum's first condition well: freedom to learn by doing at
reduced risk. [19] — "with a full team present and a family helping" — is exactly the low-stakes virtual
world in which a learner can attempt a practice he does not yet understand. And [38]'s Thursday call, with
its "look back / look at the flock / look ahead" liturgy, is a respectable second reflective loop; most
ministries have nothing of the kind.

**What is missing.** Assessed against what a reflective practicum actually requires, three things:

*(a) There is no coaching leg.* A practicum is not a feedback survey; it is a **dialogue between coach and
student, conducted largely through reciprocal reflection-in-action**. The coach demonstrates, the student
imitates and gets it partly wrong, the coach shows the difference, the student tries again. What the round
provides is serial monologue — each person answers the same two questions, and the evening ends. Nobody
says to the sixteen-year-old, "here is what I saw you do in the ten seconds when the room went quiet, and
here is what I would have tried, and why." None of the coaching forms I described — demonstrating and
having the student say what he saw, joint experimentation, working the student's own framing back to him —
is present. The round tells the teen leader how the evening *landed*. It does not teach him to see what he
did.

*(b) There is no private channel.* The public round is the only feedback mechanism the paper names. In my
experience of practicums, the things a student most needs to say to a coach, and most needs to hear, do not
get said in the circle — and this design makes that harder than usual, because a teenager giving the adult
leader real criticism would be doing it in front of his own mother. That is a direct consequence of a
commitment the paper is right to hold ([16], the family is never divided), which means the paper should
name the cost rather than leave it implicit. Five minutes between the lead companion and the teen leader
after each second running, out of the circle, would cost nothing and is where the actual learning is likely
to happen.

*(c) The learning bind has been designed out rather than worked through.* A student in a practicum cannot
at first understand what he needs to learn; he can only learn it by beginning to do what he does not yet
understand. That bind reliably produces defensiveness, and a practicum's real work is not to avoid the
defensiveness but to get through it. The round's design — leader first, positive item first, correction
always modeled as strength [20] — removes the occasion for defensiveness. For a young room that is humane
and probably correct, and I would not change it in the first quarter. But by spring a round that has never
once produced a difficult moment is not producing learning; it is producing a *ritual of receptivity*. The
paper should say what the round is expected to look like when it is working hard, not only when it is
working smoothly, and it should add a disconfirmation condition for the round itself — see the test I
proposed in Finding 3: if nothing in the design ever changes because of what the round surfaced, the round
is ceremony.

*(d) A scope note.* My practicum work concerns *professionals* — architects, psychotherapists, planners,
managers — people with a body of practice, an acquired competence, and a professional community that
supplies standards against which a coach can correct. A parent and a teenager in a church fellowship are
not professionals in that sense, and the coaching structure presupposes a coach who has mastered the
practice being learned. The extension to lay formation is legitimate and interesting, but it does not
transfer automatically, and the paper should say it is making the extension.

### Finding 5 — Unprompted vocabulary is still espoused, and the design has been teaching it
**Severity: LOW**

**Text.** [41]: "the words a member reaches for when describing the group carry the marks of the trust
actually held in it… unprompted salience, what a room spontaneously names as worth keeping, is a truer read
of what it values than any answer to a supplied category." [51]: "the room's own words about itself will
migrate from safety language toward belonging language."

**Credit.** The author has already built the corrective in principle: [44] makes cross-source disagreement
the unit of finding, and [32] instructs the team to treat disagreement as data. So this is a refinement,
not a fault.

**The refinement.** Spontaneous vocabulary is still *talk*, and a room that has heard "safe, present,
clear, intentional" spoken aloud at the opening and closing of twenty-two consecutive sessions [17] has
been taught a vocabulary. Migration in the words is therefore consistent with two very different stories:
the room's trust matured, or the room learned the dialect its leaders reward. Nothing in the third question
distinguishes them. The paper's own architecture supplies the discriminator and simply does not schedule
it: pair each quarter's vocabulary read against a behavioral trace from the same quarter — break survival
[56], a stray actually sought [37], a disagreement processed without the leader [50] — and treat agreement
between word and deed as the finding, rather than the words alone. One sentence in [41] and one column on
the quarterly sheet.

## 4. What I would want cited differently

1. **Add *Educating the Reflective Practitioner* (San Francisco: Jossey-Bass, 1987).** The 1983 book makes
   the epistemological argument; the 1987 book is where the practicum, the coaching dialogue, and learning
   by doing under supervision are worked out. Nearly everything this paper leans on from me — the rotation
   apprenticeship [19], the calibration exemplars [38], the feedback round [20] — is 1987 material, and only
   1983 is in the bibliography [83]. If my name is to carry the practicum claims, that is the book.

2. **Add Argyris and Schön for the concept the paper most needs and does not have.** *Theory in Practice:
   Increasing Professional Effectiveness* (San Francisco: Jossey-Bass, 1974) for espoused theory versus
   theory-in-use; *Organizational Learning II: Theory, Method, and Practice* (Reading, MA: Addison-Wesley,
   1996) for single- and double-loop learning. This is not padding. Finding 3 is unfixable without the
   vocabulary, and with it the author gains a precise name for what his disconfirmation conditions do
   (single-loop testing) and for what they cannot do.

3. **"Practitioners know more than they can say" should credit Polanyi.** Footnote 14 gives me a
   formulation whose core is Michael Polanyi's — *Personal Knowledge* (1958) and *The Tacit Dimension*
   (1966), from which the "we know more than we can tell" line comes. I adopted and adapted it; I did not
   originate it. A parenthetical is enough, but I would want it there.

4. **Move "consolidation" to its proper literature.** Whatever the author decides to cite for
   learning-by-teaching and self-explanation, it is not me. As it stands, footnote 14 places a retention
   claim under a book that makes no retention claims.

5. **Correct the emphasis of footnote 14's gloss.** "Reflection afterward makes [tacit knowing] available
   for examination and teaching" should become something nearer: reflection *yields a description of*
   knowing-in-action — a construction, useful and checkable, and never a transcript. The difference is not
   pedantic in this paper: on the author's current wording the teach-back delivers the leader's competence;
   on the accurate wording it delivers the leader's espoused account of his competence, which is the more
   valuable datum and the one his own architecture is built to cross-check.

6. **Cite me at [9], where I actually apply**, for the claim that the practitioner inside the situation
   holds a form of knowing the outside stance cannot reach, and that this constitutes a different rigor
   rather than a shortfall of the usual one. That sentence is currently carried by the Lewin note, which
   does not warrant it.

## 5. My single biggest concern

If I could say one thing to the editor it is this: this paper has been built, with great care, out of its
designer's account of what his program does — and it contains no mechanism capable of telling him where
that account and the program's actual behavior come apart. The author has done more than most to guard
against this. He publishes disconfirmation conditions in advance, he names the founder conflict without
euphemism, he binds his team by covenant, and he treats disagreement between sources as the unit of

never show him that the terms are wrong; and every observational channel runs either through him or through
companions he selected and formed. That is single-loop learning with the double loop unbuilt, and the
reason it matters here is the finding Chris Argyris and I kept meeting in every profession we studied: a
practitioner's theory-in-use is not available to him by introspection, and the sincerity of his espoused
theory does nothing to close the gap — if anything it hides it. My concrete recommendation is small and
cheap: one line on the weekly debrief sheet asking where the evening departed from the plan and what the
leader did in the moment; and one genuine outsider, two hours a quarter, reading the sheets and answering
"judging only by what this program does, what does it appear to believe?" Set that answer beside the six
commitments in [16]–[21], and the differences are the paper's most interesting data. Without it, the
sentence at [59] — "a conflict I have tried to bind with covenant and disconfirmation rather than pretend
to escape" — is an honest confession that has not yet become a method.
