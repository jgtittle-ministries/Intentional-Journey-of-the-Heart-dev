# Source-Fidelity Review — David R. Krathwohl

**Paper:** "Twenty-Two Wednesdays as a Fellowship of the Heart," draft v2.3 (2026-08-18)
**Reviewing for:** fidelity to Krathwohl, Bloom, and Masia, *Taxonomy of Educational Objectives,
Handbook II: Affective Domain* (New York: David McKay, 1964)

A prefatory word, because it bears on everything below. I have spent a long career watching that
1964 volume be used, and the great majority of the misuse has been committed by people acting in
perfectly good faith. The book has a seductive quality: five tidy categories, a plausible ordering
principle, and no visible warning label. Bloom and I did put the warnings in the text, but they
are in the discursive chapters that nobody reads, while the five category names are on a chart that
everybody copies. So I want to say at the outset that this author has used the taxonomy more
carefully than most, that several of his moves are ones I wish more people had made, and that the
findings below are the findings of a man who is glad to see the book in use and would like to see it
in use correctly.

---

## 1. What the paper gets right about my work

**The names, the order, and the ordering principle are correct.** Paragraph [12] lists "Receiving,
Responding, Valuing, Organization" in the right sequence, and consistently calls the organizing
construct *internalization* rather than one of the loose substitutes ("commitment," "depth,"
"ownership") that usually appear. That is the actual name of the principle on which we ordered the
categories, and the author uses it as we used it: as a continuum along which control of behavior
shifts from outside the person to inside. The gloss at [12] — the level two to three transition as
the place "where intrinsic motivation begins to take over from external structure" — is a fair
paraphrase of what we meant by the move from Responding to Valuing. The vocabulary is imported
(Deci and Ryan's, not ours), but the substance is right.

**The domain restriction is correct usage, and I want to be emphatic about this.** Paragraph [18]:
"Everything runs through a single domain, hearing and obeying God." Affective objectives are always
objectives *about something*. There is no such thing as Valuing in the abstract; there is valuing
*this*. A great deal of the damage done with our taxonomy over sixty years has come from people
treating the five categories as a general-purpose maturity scale detached from any content. Naming
one value domain and holding the whole architecture inside it is exactly right, and [18]'s reasoning
for it — "adequate measurement is domain-indexed: a profile, not a score" — is a better statement of
the point than any sentence I wrote on the subject. (It has a cost at level four, which is Finding
3 below, but the commitment itself is sound.)

**The refusal to score persons is the position Handbook II took, and the author arrived at it
independently.** Paragraphs [21], [34], [44], and [45] — "We are grading the curriculum, never the
family"; "No participant is ever told a level or a stage, theirs or anyone's"; "mirrors, never
levers; no scores." That is our position. One of the central practical arguments of the 1964 volume
was that affective outcomes should serve the evaluation of *programs* and not the grading of
*students*, both because grading them corrupts the measurement and because probing a student's
interior life and then marking it is an invasion we were not willing to license. We also identified
the reluctance to grade affective outcomes as one reason affective objectives had quietly eroded out
of American schooling. The author has reinvented that distinction from pastoral premises and states
it more crisply than we did. He should know he has a sixty-year-old ally, and he should cite it (see
section 4).

**The fifth principle understands what the top of the taxonomy is.** [12]: "at the top, the
curriculum recedes: the settled life in which hearing and obeying God has become simply who a person
is cannot be produced by a syllabus, and a curriculum that tried would do harm." We said something
close, and less well: the upper categories describe outcomes that take years, involve the whole
person, and are rarely appropriate as the objectives of any single course. A designer who declines to
promise Characterization has read the book properly.

**Break-survival is the single best measurement idea in the paper, and it is properly derived.**
[56]: "Internalization has a behavioral signature: practices that continue when the structure stops
holding them. The year has three scheduled structure-stoppages." That is a genuinely good
operationalization of the continuum. The whole distinction between Responding and Valuing turns on
whether the behavior requires external prompting, and this design deliberately withdraws the prompt
three times and counts. I have read a great many attempts to instrument the affective domain, and
most of them ask a person to rate how much they value something. This one removes the scaffold and
watches. That is the right instinct, and it is the author's own — he should claim it as a
contribution rather than let it read as a deduction from us.

**The event-contingency rule is measurement-literate.** [37]: an event-contingent sign with no
occasion is marked "no occasion" and treated as "missing data, not failure." Distinguishing an
unobserved opportunity from a failed one is a discipline that affective instruments almost never
maintain, and its absence is one reason affective data are usually uninterpretable.

**Multi-source, with disagreement treated as signal.** [32], [44]. We argued that affective
assessment cannot rest on self-report and must combine methods — observation, unobtrusive measures,
inference from behavior. The author goes further and makes the disagreement itself the finding.
That is a legitimate development of our position, not a distortion of it.

## 2. Findings

### Finding 1 — A classification of *objectives* is used as a *scale of persons*, and the paper never says which it is doing. **HIGH**

[6] "existing instruments for measuring spiritual maturity cluster at the lower levels of
Krathwohl's affective taxonomy." [12] "The individuals are assumed to be a mix of one, two, or
three, and possibly four (Receiving, Responding, Valuing, Organization)." [12] "each individual's
progress from wherever they start will be tracked."

What we classified in 1964 were *educational objectives* — statements of the behavior a curriculum
intends to make possible — not persons, and not states of persons. The distinction is not
pedantry. An objective can be classified by inspecting its wording. A person cannot; a person can
only be *inferred* to have reached a category, from behavior, through an instrument, with error. The
paper slides from the first to the second without a sentence marking the move, and once a reader is
told that individuals "are a mix of one, two, or three," the categories have silently become a
personal attribute with a value.

Now let me answer the question I expect is being asked of me plainly, because I think this is where
reviewers will divide. **Is treating the levels as a developmental sequence a person moves up over a
year a category error?** No. It is an *extension*, and one within the spirit of what we intended —
we ordered objectives by internalization precisely so that curriculum builders could sequence them
for learners, which presupposes that learners move along the continuum. What is illegitimate is not
the extension but its silence. Three things must be said out loud and are not said anywhere in this
paper:

1. the taxonomy classifies objectives, and using it to describe persons is an inference, not a
   reading;
2. the ordering is *ordinal* — see Finding 2;
3. the hierarchy was derived logically from the internalization principle and has never been
   validated as a developmental stage model — see Finding 4.

**Fix.** One paragraph in the design-commitments section, before the five principles, stating what
the taxonomy is, what it is not, and that the paper is extending it. It costs eight sentences and it
inoculates the paper against every reviewer who knows the source.

### Finding 2 — "On average, one level or more" performs arithmetic on categories that will not bear it. **HIGH**

[12] "The desire is that each individual will move, on average, one level or more."

This is the one quantitative commitment in the paper, and it is the sentence I would flag first if I
had only one flag.

Our five categories are ordinal. They are not equal-interval, they were never claimed to be, and
they cannot be averaged. The distances are visibly unequal even on the face of the scheme: each
major category carries subcategories (Receiving has three, Responding three, Valuing three,
Organization two), and "one level" of movement can mean anything from a shift inside Receiving —
from bare awareness to willingness to attend, which is nearly nothing — to the passage from
Commitment into Conceptualization of a Value, which is the hardest transition in the taxonomy and
may take years. Calling both of those "one level" and then averaging across a cohort produces a
number with no referent.

There are two further problems in the same sentence. The grammar is incoherent — "each individual
will move, on average" cannot be parsed; either each individual moves a level, or the cohort mean
moves a level, and these are different claims with different evidentiary requirements. And the
target contradicts the paper's own fifth principle. Individuals are said to include some at level
four [12]; a person at Organization who moves "one level" has arrived at Characterization inside a
school year — the very outcome [12] says "cannot be produced by a syllabus." The target, taken
literally, promises what the paper elsewhere promises not to promise.

I will add the view of the 1964 volume, for whatever it is worth: we expected affective change of
this magnitude to take years rather than terms, and one of the reasons affective objectives
disappeared from school practice was that nobody's evaluation cycle was long enough to see them
move.

**Fix.** Strike the numeric target. Replace it with a described transition — the paper already has
the better instrument in hand, in the aged sign-forms of [37] and the break-survival counts of [56].
"We expect most participants to show the growing forms of at least one sign by spring, and the
cohort's break-survival to rise across the three re-entries" is a falsifiable prediction that does
not require a scale we do not have. If the author wants to keep a level-language target, express it
as a distribution ("the proportion of the cohort showing behavior at Valuing or above"), never as a
mean of level-changes.

### Finding 3 — "Organization" is redefined as a corporate competence. That is not the category. **HIGH**

[12] "Fourth, at the level of Organization, make the corporate turn: the group learns to hear God as
a body, one member receiving, another testing, another seeing the application."

Organization, in our scheme, is 4.1 Conceptualization of a Value and 4.2 Organization of a Value
System. Its defining work is *intrapersonal and cross-domain*: the person abstracts a value
sufficiently to relate it to other values they hold, discovers where those values conflict, and
builds an ordered, internally consistent system in which the conflicts are resolved. It is the level
at which a person works out what to do when two things they genuinely value pull against each other.
Nothing in it is corporate, and nothing in it can be performed by a group as a group.

What the paper describes at [12] and instruments throughout — a body distributing the work of
hearing, one receiving, another testing — is a real competence and, so far as I can tell from the
Scripture cited, a well-grounded one. It is simply not the thing our fourth category names. A
different construct has been placed under our label, and readers who know the source will read [12]
as a claim about the taxonomy that the taxonomy does not make.

This finding interlocks with the domain restriction I praised in section 1. The single-domain
commitment is right for levels one through three, but it *removes the material Organization operates
on*. If the only value in view is hearing and obeying God, there are no competing values to
conceptualize against and no system to order — so the category has nothing to do, and the paper fills
the vacancy with the corporate turn. So my answer to whether the single-domain restriction helps or
hurts is: it helps decisively at Receiving, Responding, and Valuing, and it structurally breaks
Organization, and the paper has not noticed the seam.

**Fix.** Two options, and I would take the first. (a) Keep the corporate turn, name it as the
author's own construct, and say plainly that it is placed *alongside* Organization rather than being
an instance of it. (b) Instrument Organization as we defined it — the moment a participant hits a
genuine conflict between hearing-and-obeying and another thing they value (a job, a friendship, a
family expectation) and has to order them. That is, incidentally, a rich pastoral occasion and the
design's costly-telling sign is already well placed to catch it.

### Finding 4 — The taxonomy is made to generate falsifiable predictions about phasing, without any statement that its hierarchy was never empirically validated. **HIGH**

[12] "the sessions are one walk through the levels of internalization, each presupposing the ones
before." [54] "A cohort still showing not yet on first forms in February, or no growing form all
year, falsifies either the phasing or the curriculum."

The paper nowhere asserts that our taxonomy was validated. I want to be fair about that; it does
not. But it does something with equivalent effect: it lets the taxonomy do work only a validated
stage model can do — supply the expected sequence, ground the phasing of a curriculum, and stand as
one of exactly two disjuncts a disconfirming year could falsify — and it never once tells the reader
what the taxonomy's evidentiary status actually is. Silence in that position reads as endorsement.

The status is this. The internalization ordering was arrived at analytically. We proposed it as a
useful and defensible way to arrange objectives, we were candid in the volume that it rested on
weaker ground than the cognitive handbook's ordering, and the cumulative-hierarchy claim — that each
category presupposes the ones below — was a working hypothesis we offered for testing, not a finding
we reported. It has never been established as a developmental stage model in the sense that
Piaget's or Kohlberg's sequences were argued for: no invariant-order evidence, no scaling studies of
the kind that would license "each presupposing the ones before" as a statement of fact. When Lorin
Anderson and I revised the cognitive handbook in 2001, we deliberately loosened the strict cumulative
hierarchy even there, where the evidence was better. No comparable revision of the affective volume
was ever undertaken, which means the 1964 categories have never been re-examined against six decades
of subsequent evidence. That is not a reason to discard them. It is a reason to say so.

Note how differently the paper treats the trust literature. At [41] and [58] it says "the trust
literature supplies the stages" and calls its own mapping "proposed," and at [58] it flags the
mapping as "a formal research trail." That is exactly the right register. Our taxonomy gets no such
register anywhere in the paper, and it is the more load-bearing of the two.

**Fix.** State the status in two sentences where the taxonomy is introduced. And add a third
disjunct to [54]: as written, a ladder that never climbs "falsifies either the phasing or the
curriculum," which forecloses the live third possibility — that the internalization ordering does not
describe this domain, or does not describe it on this timescale. A design that publishes its
disconfirmation conditions ought to put its borrowed framework at risk too, not only its own two
moving parts.

### Finding 5 — Group-level application is asserted rather than declared as an extension. **MEDIUM**

[12] "the group, when it first forms, is assumed to be at group level one, where they are listening
and attending to the leader, and the process itself quickly invites them into participating and
contributing at level two." [41] "The identification stage is internalization by another name,
visible at group scale."

We wrote about individual learners. The taxonomy contains no theory of a group's position on the
continuum, and none of the reasoning by which we ordered the categories transfers automatically to a
collective. **Is the extension defensible?** Yes, in principle — a group's observable conduct can
sensibly be described as attending, or as responding, and the paper's operationalizations at [12]
are recognizable. But it needs an argument it does not receive, chiefly on the aggregation question:
is a group at "level two" a group whose members are mostly at level two, or a group whose *corporate
behavior* is level-two-like regardless of where members sit? These come apart immediately — a room of
individually committed people can behave compliantly, and a room of beginners can be carried into
responding behavior by a strong liturgy. The paper needs to say which it means, because its two
ladders [12] are explicitly "separate but interdependent" and the interdependence is exactly what is
unspecified.

**Does the paper acknowledge the reach?** Partially, and I want to give credit for it: [14] says
"Four of those five principles are unchanged; the second is new to this article," which flags the
group ladder as new. But it flags it as new *relative to the author's own earlier design papers*,
not as new *relative to the source*. A reader who does not know the 1964 volume will finish this
paper believing group-level internalization is something Krathwohl described. It is not.

**Fix.** One clause: "Krathwohl's categories describe individual learners; applying them to a group
is this project's extension, and the aggregation rule we adopt is —."

### Finding 6 — The "structural ceiling" is a stronger claim than the affective volume makes, and the footnote yokes my name to it. **HIGH**

[6] "existing instruments for measuring spiritual maturity cluster at the lower levels of
Krathwohl's affective taxonomy because self-report meets a structural ceiling: above the middle
levels, social compliance and transformed character produce the same observable behavior."
Footnote 1, attached at that point, cites the author's companion article and our volume together in
one note.

I am asked whether this is consistent with what Handbook II says about the difficulty of affective
measurement. It is not; it is considerably stronger, in two distinct ways.

*First, difficulty is not impossibility.* We documented at length that affective measurement is hard
and that it is peculiarly vulnerable to distortion — a respondent can generally tell which answer the
institution wants and supply it, and the more the institution visibly cares, the worse the
contamination. That is a validity threat, and it is real, and the author is right to take it
seriously. But it is a claim about *degraded* measurement, not about a structural ceiling above which
measurement is impossible in principle. The 1964 volume does not conclude that the upper categories
cannot be measured; it carries illustrative objectives and illustrative test items across the
categories — thinning markedly toward the top, which is itself informative — and it points to attitude
scales, interest inventories, observational and unobtrusive techniques as the way up. A claim of
structural unmeasurability is not one we made and not one the volume supports.

*Second, the causal diagnosis is different.* The paper says instruments cluster low *because* of the
ceiling. Our account of why affective assessment thinned out at the upper end was substantially
institutional: schools would not grade affective outcomes and rightly feared invading privacy; the
time-scales were longer than any evaluation cycle; the objectives were stated in curricula and then
quietly abandoned in practice. Instruments cluster at the lower categories partly because that is
where the psychometrics are tractable and partly because nobody was willing to build the other kind.
Attributing the clustering wholly to a structural ceiling makes the author's own project look more
necessary than it needs to look — and his project is well enough motivated without it.

I recognize the paper attributes this argument to the companion article rather than to me, and that
is honest as far as it goes. But footnote 1 places our volume in the same note as the claim, with no
separation, and the body sentence puts my name in the same breath as the word "because." A reader
will carry away that Krathwohl held affective maturity to be unmeasurable above the middle. I did
not, and would not.

**Fix.** One sentence at [6], and split footnote 1 (see section 4). Something in the register of:
"Handbook II documented the difficulty and the distortion-vulnerability of affective measurement; the
stronger claim that the upper levels are structurally inaccessible to self-report is the companion
article's, not Krathwohl's."

### Finding 7 — The subcategories are never used, and the one the author most needs is 2.3. **MEDIUM**

The whole paper turns on a single transition — [12] "the threshold of the level two to level three
transition," [51] "the level-three transition, intrinsic valuing displacing external structure." Yet
the taxonomy is used only at the level of the five major headings, and the subcategories never
appear.

This costs the author something concrete. Responding is not one thing: 2.1 is acquiescence — doing it
because one is asked; 2.2 is willingness — doing it without being asked; 2.3 is *satisfaction in
response*, the point at which the behavior begins to carry its own affective reward. 2.3 is the
hinge. It is where Responding starts turning into Valuing, and it is observable in exactly the
register this design already works in — a person who returns to the practice with evident pleasure,
who speaks of it as something they enjoy rather than something they completed. The author's aged
sign-forms at [37] — answering, offering, costly telling — are in fact tracking something close to
the 2.1 / 2.2 / 2.3 progression without knowing it. Naming the correspondence would strengthen both.

Using the subcategories would also repair Finding 2: movement expressed as 2.1 → 2.3 is a
defensible, describable claim in a way that "one level" is not.

**Fix.** Use the subcategory structure at least for the two-to-three transition the paper cares
most about, and consider mapping the three aged forms of each sign onto it explicitly.

### Finding 8 — "Internalization by another name" equates two constructs from different literatures at different units of analysis. **MEDIUM**

[41] "The identification stage is internalization by another name, visible at group scale."

Lewicki and Bunker's identification-based trust is a property of a *relationship* — I trust you
because I have internalized your preferences and can act for you. Internalization in our sense is a
property of a *person's relation to a value* — the value has become part of how they are organized.
These are structurally parallel and it is worth noticing the parallel; they are not the same
construct, and "another name" claims identity.

I note in fairness that [58] handles the same relation far better — "the proposed mapping between
trust stage and internalization level, which this project has begun registering as a formal research
trail." That is the right hedge. [41] should match it: "structurally parallel to," or "we propose to
read as an analogue of."

### Finding 9 — The relation between the two ordinal ladders is unspecified. **MEDIUM**

[37] describes a second ordinal system entirely — first / growing / full forms of three signs, with
"a rung after three consecutive weeks of seen" — layered over our five categories, and the two are
never mapped. Does a rung promotion indicate a level change? Do all three signs have to advance? Can
a room advance a rung on one sign and be judged to have moved a level? The paper needs this because
its level claims [12] and its sign claims [54] are its two sources of evidence and they can
disagree. [58] anticipates one such disagreement ("stagnant vocabulary alongside advancing
sign-readings") and treats it as informative, which is good; the level-versus-sign disagreement gets
no such treatment.

The three-consecutive-weeks promotion rule is, by the way, a decision rule with an error rate, and
a reader in my discipline will want to know what happens on a two-then-miss-then-two pattern.

### Finding 10 — Terminology drift: "levels" for what we called categories or classes. **LOW**

Throughout, e.g. [12] "the levels of internalization." We spoke of major categories and classes
ordered along the internalization continuum. "Level" is now common usage and I am not going to fight
it, but it does quiet work: "level" invites a number, a number invites a mean, and a mean produced
Finding 2. The author might notice that the word is doing some of the damage.

### Finding 11 — The fifth category is described but never named. **LOW**

[12]'s parenthesis names four — "(Receiving, Responding, Valuing, Organization)" — and the fifth
principle describes Characterization by a Value or Value Complex without naming it. Naming it, and
saying plainly that the pilot does not target it, would make the paper's restraint legible as
restraint rather than as an omission.

### Finding 12 — My name appears once in the body of a paper built on my book. **LOW**

[6] is the only body mention; footnotes 1 and 7 and the bibliography carry the rest. Meanwhile the
architecture is in [12], [18], [37], [41], [51], [54], [56], and [63]. A reader cannot tell where
the source ends and the author's construction begins — which is the whole of section 3 below, stated
as a citation problem.

## 3. Where the author goes beyond me, and whether he says so

I count seven reaches. The pattern is consistent and worth stating as a pattern: **this author is
scrupulous about flagging what is new relative to his own prior papers, and silent about what is new
relative to his sources.** [14] is the tell — "Four of those five principles are unchanged; the
second is new to this article" — a sentence that carefully tracks the internal provenance of an idea
while saying nothing about its external provenance. A reader of this paper can reconstruct the
author's development history and cannot reconstruct where Krathwohl stops.

| # | The reach | Declared? |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Objectives → persons. Our categories classify statements of intended behavior; the paper classifies people ([12] "individuals are assumed to be a mix of one, two, or three"). | **No.** Nowhere. |
| 2 | Classification → developmental timetable. A logical ordering becomes a phased year with expected arrival times ([54] "first forms steady by the end of the first quarter"). | **No.** And [54] treats the phasing as falsifiable without listing the framework itself among the things that could be wrong. |
| 3 | Ordinal categories → averaged movement ([12] "on average, one level or more"). | **No**, and this one is not merely undeclared but unsound. See Finding 2. |
| 4 | Individuals → groups ([12] group levels one and two; [41] "visible at group scale"). | **Partially.** [14] flags the group ladder as new to this article. It does not say it is new relative to the taxonomy. |
| 5 | Organization → corporate discernment ([12] "at the level of Organization, make the corporate turn"). | **No.** Presented as an instance of the category rather than a substitution for it. See Finding 3. |
| 6 | Measurement difficulty → structural ceiling ([6]). | **Partially.** Attributed to the companion article, but footnoted alongside our volume with no separation, and the body sentence names me. See Finding 6. |
| 7 | Domain-indexing ([18]). | **Not a reach at all.** This is inside our intent and I would not have the author qualify it. Listed here only so the audit is complete. |

Reaches 1, 2, 3, and 5 are the ones I would require fixed before publication. Every one of them is
fixable with a sentence; none of them requires the author to give up anything he wants to do. That
is worth saying to the editor, because a review of this length can read as if the paper needs
rebuilding, and it does not.

## 4. What I would want cited differently

**a. Split footnotes 1 and 7, and let the taxonomy stand on its own.** Both notes read: the author's
forthcoming article, then "The taxonomy is David R. Krathwohl, Benjamin S. Bloom, and Bertram B.
Masia, *Taxonomy of Educational Objectives, Handbook II: Affective Domain*." Bundling the source
inside a citation of the author's own article makes our volume look like a supporting reference for
his argument rather than the framework his architecture rests on — and, at footnote 1, puts our
volume in the same note as the structural-ceiling claim (Finding 6). Cite the 1964 volume in its own
note at first substantive use, then short-form it.

**b. There is not a single page citation to my book anywhere in the paper.** For a paper whose
skeleton is our five categories, that is a real defect and an easy one. The category definitions,
the internalization discussion, and the material on affective evaluation are all locatable, and a
reader who wants to check the author's reading currently cannot.

**c. The 1964 edition is correct.** Bibliography entry [75] — Krathwohl, Bloom, and Masia, David
McKay, 1964 — is right, including the co-author order and the publisher, which people get wrong
about as often as they get it right. No correction needed.

**d. Cite the 2001 revision, but cite it for what it is.** Anderson, L. W., and D. R. Krathwohl,
eds., *A Taxonomy for Learning, Teaching, and Assessing: A Revision of Bloom's Taxonomy of
Educational Objectives* (New York: Longman, 2001), and my summary of it in "A Revision of Bloom's
Taxonomy: An Overview," *Theory Into Practice* 41, no. 4 (2002): 212–218. The revision covered the
*cognitive* domain only. That is precisely why it is worth a footnote here: the author can note that
the cumulative-hierarchy assumption was loosened even in the better-evidenced cognitive domain, and
that no comparable revision of the affective volume was ever undertaken. That single footnote does
most of the work of Finding 4.

**e. For affective measurement in practice, there is a better modern source than us.** L. W.
Anderson and S. F. Bourke, *Assessing Affective Characteristics in the Schools*, 2nd ed. (Mahwah, NJ:
Erlbaum, 2000). If the author wants a current statement of what is and is not measurable in the
affective domain, and of the distortion problems that motivate his ceiling argument, that is where he
should send readers rather than to a 1964 handbook.

**f. He is entitled to cite us in support of his own ethics, and he does not.** The refusal to grade
persons on affective outcomes at [21], [34], and [45] is our position from Handbook II, argued on
both measurement-corruption and privacy grounds. As the paper stands, that commitment reads as
pastoral conviction alone. It is pastoral conviction *and* the settled view of the measurement
literature that gave him his framework, and saying so costs him nothing and gains him a reviewer.

**g. On Goodhart's law at [45] and footnote 13.** Strathern is the right citation for the audit
critique and the note is careful. I would add only that the corruption-of-measures point has a
direct measurement-literature home in Donald T. Campbell's work on the corrupting effect of
quantitative social indicators used for decision-making — closer to the author's actual concern than
the British university audit regime, and it strengthens the point.

---

## 5. My single biggest concern

If I could say one thing to the editor it would be this: the paper's entire architecture rests on
treating a 1964 classification of *educational objectives* as a validated developmental ladder that
persons and groups ascend on a school-year schedule, and it never tells the reader that this is what
it is doing. The taxonomy did not establish that; it proposed an ordering principle and said, with
more candor than most readers noticed, that the ordering rested on weaker ground than its cognitive
predecessor and was offered for testing. The place where the silence becomes a substantive error
rather than a rhetorical one is the sentence at [12] — "each individual will move, on average, one
level or more" — which computes a mean over categories that are ordinal, unequally spaced, and
internally subdivided, and which, taken at face value, promises for some participants the very
Characterization-level outcome the paper's own fifth principle says a syllabus cannot produce. I want
to be clear that the fix is cheap and the underlying design is not in jeopardy: one paragraph
declaring the taxonomy's status and the extensions being made to it, one numeric target replaced by a
described transition that the paper's own aged-sign and break-survival instruments already measure
better, and one honest sentence separating the author's structural-ceiling claim from anything I ever
argued. Make those three changes and this becomes an unusually careful use of my work — and I would
rather see it published carefully than see the book misused politely one more time.
