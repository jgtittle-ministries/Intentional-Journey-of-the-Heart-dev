# Source-Fidelity Review — Amy C. Edmondson

**Paper:** "Twenty-Two Wednesdays as a Fellowship of the Heart," draft v2.3 (2026-08-18)
**Scope of my review:** the paper's use of psychological safety and of my own published work. Nothing else.

---

## 1. What the paper gets right about my work

I want to start here, because there is more to credit than I expected, and because two of the things
the author gets right are things that professional organizational scholars routinely get wrong.

**He read the scale, not a summary of the scale.** At [17] the paper renders the construct's markers as
"whether mistakes are held against people, whether hard issues can be raised, whether being different
is punished." Those are, in substance, three of the seven items in the 1999 measure, and they are
rendered in the right direction and with the right sense — including the two that are reverse-worded,
which is where popularizations usually go wrong. Later, at [50], he adds "help asked for," which is a
fourth item on that same instrument. He did not get those from a management blog. That earns him a
hearing on the rest.

**He does not claim derivation, and he says so unprompted.** [17]: "I did not design the container from
that literature, but the convergence is striking." This is exactly the right disclosure and it is
volunteered rather than extracted. A great many practitioner papers reverse-engineer a genealogy for a
practice they invented by instinct. This one declines to. I will hold him to it in Finding 1, because
[50] does not keep the promise [17] makes — but the promise itself is honorably made.

**He owns the leadership-absence idea as his own before he borrows for it.** [19] names the teen-led
second running as "the group-level measurement the companion article proposed under the name of the
leadership absence test." That is his construct, correctly attributed to himself. See Finding 6 for
what happens to it thirty paragraphs later.

**The "response to load" sign is the mechanism, and he found it without me.** This is the single most
impressive thing in the paper from where I sit. [36]: "response to load (when something hard lands,
what does the room do in its first ten seconds)." That *is* psychological safety. Safety is not a
property of a room's furniture or its stated rules; it is the residue of what happened the last time
somebody took a risk. My teams learned what was safe by watching what happened in the seconds after a
nurse reported an error or a junior engineer raised an objection. The author has built a weekly
observation on precisely that moment. He should know that this is the strongest warrant in his design
and that it does not depend on my literature at all.

**The counterfeit warning is right, and if anything it is too weak.** [42]: "the sweetest-looking room
can be the lowest-trust room in the building, its harmony maintained by hiding differences." I have
spent twenty-five years telling audiences that psychological safety is not niceness, and this paper —
written by a pastor, in a church setting, where niceness is practically a load-bearing wall — says it
out loud and builds instruments against it. He credits Peck's pseudocommunity, which is fair, and does
not reach for my name. He could have. See Section 4 for the sharper warrant, and Finding 11's
absence — I am not charging this as a fault, because it is not one.

**The footnote describing my two works is accurate.** Footnote 6 calls the 2018 book "developed for
practitioners," which is what it is. It is not presented as a second empirical validation. That is a
distinction most citers do not bother to make.

## 2. Findings

### Finding 1 — "the safety research validates" the six markers. It validates, at most, two and a half. **HIGH**

[50]: "watch for six small events that **the safety research validates** and any Companion can see: a
mistake admitted unprompted; help asked for; a hard thing raised without invitation; a disagreement
processed without the leader; vulnerability answered with vulnerability across a generation line (a
parent's costly tell answered by a teen's); and the leader's own presence and guidance reduced."

Taking them in order, against what I actually established:

1. **Mistake admitted unprompted** — grounded. This corresponds to the mistakes item and to the
   error-discussion component of learning behavior. One important caveat below.
2. **Help asked for** — grounded. Asking for help is both a scale item and one of the learning
   behaviors the 1999 study measured as the dependent variable.
3. **A hard thing raised without invitation** — mostly grounded. The item concerns whether members
   *are able to* bring up problems and tough issues. "Without invitation" adds a spontaneity
   requirement my measure does not contain. A small, sensible extension; call it the half.
4. **A disagreement processed without the leader** — not mine. Safety enables voice and dissent, yes.
   Nothing in my line establishes leader-absent conflict processing as a marker of safety. This
   belongs to the group-development literature the paper already cites at footnote 11, or to shared
   leadership.
5. **Vulnerability answered with vulnerability across a generation line** — not mine. This is
   disclosure reciprocity, which the author correctly attributes to Altman and Taylor at [16] and
   footnote 5. It should not be re-attributed to me eight sections later.
6. **The leader's own presence and guidance reduced** — not mine, and arguably in tension with my
   findings. See Finding 6.

So: two grounded, one half, three not. And "validates" is the wrong verb for even the grounded ones —
see Finding 3. The author cannot write at [17] that he noticed a convergence after the fact and then
write at [50] that the safety research validates his marker list. The second sentence launders the
first.

**Fix.** Attribute per marker. Something like: markers 1–3 correspond to items on Edmondson's team
psychological safety scale; marker 4 draws on group-development work; marker 5 on disclosure
reciprocity (Altman and Taylor); marker 6 is this project's own leadership absence test. Four
half-lines of prose and the problem is gone. The list is not weakened by honest attribution; it is
strengthened, because a reader can then check each one.

### Finding 2 — Psychological safety is a *team-level shared belief*. The paper never says so, and its design cannot see when the belief is not shared. **HIGH**

[17] quotes the construct as "a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking" —
and then never uses the word "shared" again in any operational sense. Every marker at [50] is an
individual event performed by one person: *a* mistake admitted, *a* hard thing raised, *a* teen
answering *a* parent. [41] reasons at room level about what "every participant is an eyewitness to,"
but the safety construct is nowhere on that page.

This matters more here than it would in a manufacturing plant, for a reason specific to his design.
The cohort deliberately contains an authority relation *inside the unit of analysis*: parents and their
own teenagers in one circle, with a standing rule at [16] that "in every practice the parents start."
In my own work on status differences within teams, the members with lower status are systematically
less likely to speak up, and the aggregate team score can look healthy while the lower-status subgroup
holds a materially different belief about what is safe. A room in which four articulate parents admit
mistakes, ask for help, and raise hard things every week will light up all six of the author's markers
while the teenagers hold an entirely different belief about what would happen to *them*. On his
instruments, that room reads as a success.

Worse: the design has removed the one thing that would detect it. Nothing in the five permitted pages
[34] ever asks anyone whether the room feels safe to them. The construct is invoked as warrant and
never measured — while an elaborate observational apparatus measures everything else.

**Fix**, and I think this is the most useful thing in my review. Put three or four safety items on the
quarterly pulse page — the mistakes item, the hard-issues item, the help item, the different item,
plainly worded — and look at the *gap between the teen distribution and the parent distribution*. Do
not score anyone. Do not name anyone. This is precisely the paper's own doctrine at [44]: "Every tier
above exists so that sources can disagree." The author has built a whole epistemology on discrepancy
as data and then exempted the one construct on which his entire container rests. Four items on a page
that already exists, under a permission process that already covers it, and the design gains the
ability to falsify its own foundation. That also gives [58] a companion disconfirmation condition: if
the teen–parent safety gap does not close across the year, the container is not doing what the paper
says it does.

### Finding 3 — My scale is a self-report perception measure. It did not validate a set of *observable* markers, and this paper of all papers should notice that. **MEDIUM**

[17]: "The workplace literature calls the underlying construct psychological safety ... and **has
validated its markers**: whether mistakes are held against people, whether hard issues can be raised,
whether being different is punished."

Those are not markers. They are survey items — perceptions, reported by team members, aggregated to
team level only after the within-team agreement justifies aggregation. What the 1999 study validated
was a *measure of a perception* and its relationship to learning behavior, where learning behavior was
rated by others. The study did not establish that any particular observable event is a valid indicator
of safety, and I would not defend such a claim if asked.

The irony is sharp enough that the author should want to fix it on his own terms. His companion
article's central argument is that self-report meets a ceiling [6]. He then reaches for a construct
whose validation rests on self-report, and converts it into observable behavioral markers, without
noting that he has changed the measurement modality entirely. That conversion is a real contribution
if he claims it — behavioral indicators of safety are genuinely underdeveloped in my field, and a
practitioner with a weekly debrief sheet is well placed to propose some. It is a liability if he
implies I did it for him.

**Fix.** "The construct is measured in that literature by self-report perception items; what follows is
this project's own attempt to render it as observable events, which the literature has not validated
and which this pilot's own record will begin to test."

### Finding 4 — Safety as a precondition to be built first. My data say it is continuously re-earned, and mostly by what happens *after* the first risk. **MEDIUM**

[17]: "**The container is the first curriculum.** Before anything can be told, the room must be safe to
tell it in, so the first thing the curriculum builds is what we call the container: four spoken
conditions (safe, present, clear, intentional) opened and closed aloud at every session."

Two things here. First, the sequence claim. Safety and risk-taking are reciprocal, not sequential. A
room does not become safe and then start telling; it becomes safe *because* someone told and the room
held it. This is not a quibble about ordering — it changes what a practitioner watches in week one.
Under the paper's framing, week one is a success if the conditions were spoken and the room settled.
Under mine, week one contains no information about safety at all until someone risks something and the
room responds.

Second, and more sharply: *declaring* a room safe is not a mechanism my work supports. In my field
data, stated norms were routinely contradicted by lived experience, and members believed the
experience. Announcing four conditions aloud produces, at best, an invitation; if the first real
disclosure lands badly, the announcement makes things worse, not better, because it adds a broken
promise to the injury.

I note in fairness that the author's own practice is better than his framing. "The container is rebuilt
every single week" [17] and the re-forming at [27] are exactly the continuous re-earning I would
prescribe, and "the container holding **without enforcement**" [49] shows he knows the difference
between a declared norm and a held one. He should say the mechanism out loud rather than let the
opening liturgy carry it.

### Finding 5 — Psychological safety and trust are run as one construct. They are two, and the distinction is load-bearing for this design. **MEDIUM**

[13] "It is seen in trust." [17] the container's construct is psychological safety. [41] "the trust
literature supplies the stages," with the four container conditions ("safe, present, clear and
intentional") treated as the thing members witness, then mapped onto calculus-, knowledge-, and
identification-based trust. [42] "the sweetest-looking room can be the lowest-trust room."

The paper slides between the two vocabularies without ever saying they are different constructs. They
are. Trust, in the Mayer/Rousseau tradition the author cites at footnote 10, is largely dyadic, is
directed at another party, and concerns the future consequences of *that party's* actions.
Psychological safety is group-level, concerns *one's own* immediate interpersonal risk, and is about
what will happen to me in the next thirty seconds if I say this. They correlate. They are not the same
thing, and I have written specifically on keeping them apart.

This is not pedantry for this paper, because the author's own held → known → owned progression at [41]
straddles the seam. "It's safe and well-run" (held) is safety language. "They know me there" (known)
and "these are my people" (owned) are trust and identification language. Reading the first rung as a
lower stage of trust rather than as a different construct will cost him the diagnostic value of the
migration he most wants to observe. It would be cleaner, and truer to both literatures, to say: safety
is the floor condition that makes the trust ladder climbable, and the room's language moves from
reporting the floor to reporting the climb.

### Finding 6 — The leader's declining necessity is presented as a safety marker. It is his own construct, and in a low-safety room it points the wrong way. **MEDIUM**

[50] includes "the leader's own presence and guidance reduced, which the teen-led nights measure on
purpose" among "six small events that the safety research validates."

He already owned this at [19] as "the leadership absence test," from his own companion article. He
should keep owning it. In my line of work, leader behavior is an *antecedent* of psychological safety,
not an inverse indicator of it — coaching, inclusiveness, and visible fallibility from the leader are
among the strongest predictors of whether members will speak up, and status-differentiated teams need
*more* leader inclusiveness, not less, before voice appears. Nothing in my research says that a team
needing its leader less is a team that has grown safer.

There is also a practical misreading risk, which is why I mark this MEDIUM rather than LOW. In a
low-safety room, removing the leader reduces voice — people go quiet, not free. A teen-led night that
runs smoothly with a silent room is a null result that will read as a positive one. The disconfirmation
condition at [57] ("if the container collapses in younger hands") only catches the loud failure mode,
not the quiet one. I would add: *a teen-led session with markedly less disclosure than the surrounding
weeks is a warning, not a success.*

For what it is worth, the honest warrant for marker 6 exists — the shared-leadership literature treats
an internal team environment of shared purpose, support, and voice as an antecedent of leadership
distributing itself. That is a citable claim. Mine is not.

### Finding 7 — The "different" item transports least well of the three, and in a voluntary room it will read falsely positive. **MEDIUM**

[17]: "our standing welcome for the honest skeptic is her 'different' item given a chair."

The item concerns whether people on a team reject others *for being different*. In my samples,
"different" meant functional background, tenure, demographics, personality, professional status. In a
cohort whose declared purpose is convergence — one domain, hearing and obeying God, a shared practice
sent home weekly — difference acquires a content dimension my item was never built to carry. A room
with a normative telos exerts structural pressure toward agreement that a furniture plant's product
development team simply does not.

Compounding it: attendance here is voluntary. In my teams, people who felt unsafe stayed and went
quiet, and I could measure them. In this cohort, the family for whom the room is not safe *stops
coming*, and their absence looks exactly like a smaller, warmer, more cohesive room. Selection removes
the disconfirming evidence.

**Fix**, and it costs nothing. Read attrition and drifting attendance as safety data, explicitly, in
the disconfirmation section [53]–[59]. A quiet departure after a hard week is the single strongest
negative signal this design can receive, and at present the paper has no place to put it. I note that
the "sought stray" sign [36] is already reaching in this direction; it needs to be connected to the
safety claim rather than only to the pastoral one.

### Finding 8 — The construct is imported without its dependent variable. **LOW**

Psychological safety matters in my work because of what it predicts: learning behavior — seeking
feedback, sharing information, asking for help, discussing error, experimenting — which in turn
mediates the relationship to team performance. Safety is not valuable in itself; it is valuable
because groups that have it learn and groups that lack it cannot. The paper invokes the construct at
[17] and never states what it is supposed to predict here.

It has an obvious answer available: the telling, asking, correcting, and disagreeing through which this
room learns to hear God together. Naming that turns a borrowed label into a working hypothesis, and
supplies a further disconfirmation condition — if safety markers rise across the year while the
learning behaviors the curriculum cares about do not, the construct is not doing the work claimed for
it.

---

### Finding 9 — At [42], the mechanism clause is mine and is credited elsewhere. **LOW**

[42]: "open disagreement tends to arrive before mature trust, not after it, **because people risk
conflict only in a room they believe will hold it**."

The clause after "because" is a psychological safety claim — a belief about interpersonal
risk-taking, stated almost in the construct's own terms. The footnote (11) sends the whole sentence to
Tuckman and Wheelan, who supply a stage *sequence* but not a belief *mechanism*.

I will be careful about the other half. I do not endorse "disagreement arrives before mature trust" as
established in my line; I have no temporal finding of that kind, and I would not want my name attached
to the sequence claim. But if the author wants a warrant for *why* people risk conflict only in certain
rooms, that warrant is the construct he already cites at [17], and the sentence should say so.

## 4. What I would want cited differently

1. **Restore the elided clause in the definition.** [17] quotes "a shared belief that the team is safe
   for interpersonal risk taking." The published wording specifies that the belief is *held by members
   of a team*. The elided phrase is the whole team-level claim, and it is precisely the part this paper
   needs (Finding 2). Footnote 6 gives "quotation at 350"; the author should confirm whether he is
   quoting the abstract or the construct definition in the body, and cite accordingly.

2. **Add Edmondson (1996), "Learning from Mistakes Is Easier Said Than Done," *Journal of Applied
   Behavioral Science* 32(1).** This is the correct source for marker 1, and it carries a caveat the
   author's team needs in the field: higher-safety units in that study showed *higher* rates of
   detected and reported error. A rise in admitted mistakes is a success signal. Without this, a
   Formation Companion team could read an autumn of increasing confession as deterioration.

3. **Add Edmondson (2004), "Psychological Safety, Trust, and Learning in Organizations: A Group-Level
   Lens," in Kramer and Cook, eds., *Trust and Distrust in Organizations*.** This is the source for
   Finding 5. The paper runs a trust framework and a safety framework side by side and needs one
   citation that distinguishes them.

4. **Add Nembhard and Edmondson (2006), "Making It Safe," *Journal of Organizational Behavior* 27(7).**
   The most useful missing citation in the paper. It is about status differences within teams, leader
   inclusiveness, and whether lower-status members speak up. Parent and teen in one circle is that
   problem exactly, and the paper currently has no literature for it.

5. **Add Edmondson and Lei (2014), *Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational
   Behavior* 1: 23–43,** for construct boundaries and level-of-analysis, if the author wants one
   citation to carry Findings 2, 3, and 5 economically.

6. **For meta-analytic warrant, Frazier et al. (2017), *Personnel Psychology* 70(1),** parallel to the
   de Jong et al. trust meta-analysis already used at footnote 9. If the author is willing to lean on a
   meta-analysis for trust, symmetry argues for one on safety.

7. **For the "sweet frictionless room" at [42], the sharper warrant is the two-by-two in *The Fearless
   Organization*** — psychological safety against accountability for demanding standards, producing an
   apathy zone, a comfort zone, an anxiety zone, and a learning zone. This does more for the author
   than Peck alone, because it refines his warning in a way his design has already anticipated. A
   frictionless room is ambiguous between two states: low safety with hidden difference, and *genuine*
   high safety with nothing at stake. The diagnostic is not the absence of friction; it is what happens
   when friction appears — which is his own "response to load" sign. His costly-telling and load
   architecture is the accountability axis. He is building for the learning quadrant and does not
   appear to know that there is a named quadrant to build for. **The answer to the question as posed:
   the warning is consistent with my findings and does not overstate. It under-specifies.**

8. **Lineage, for accuracy rather than credit.** [17]'s "the workplace literature calls the underlying
   construct psychological safety" is appropriately generic, but footnote 6 attaches the entire idea to
   me. The term precedes my work — Schein and Bennis (1965) and, at the individual level, Kahn (1990,
   *Academy of Management Journal* 33(4)). My contribution was the team-level construct, its measure,
   and its link to learning behavior. A half-sentence fixes it.

9. **For marker 6, if he wants a warrant other than his own:** the shared-leadership literature, e.g.
   Carson, Tesluk, and Marrone (2007), *Academy of Management Journal* 50(5), on internal team
   environment as an antecedent of shared leadership. Not my line, and a better fit than mine.

10. **For the teenagers,** he should look for psychological safety work in educational settings and with
    adolescent samples rather than transporting a manufacturing sample onto minors. Such work exists;
    it is not mine and I will not pretend to specify it. But the honest sentence is "the construct has
    been extended to educational settings; this pilot extends it further, to a voluntary,
    family-integrated, faith-formation setting, where it has not been tested."

## 5. My single biggest concern

The paper rests its whole architecture on a container whose underlying construct is, by the paper's own
quotation, a *shared* belief — and then never measures it, never disaggregates it, and never asks the
one subgroup most likely to hold a different belief than the room's average. This is not an abstract
worry about levels of analysis. The design places parents and their own teenagers in a single circle
and instructs the parents to go first every time, which is a status asymmetry inside the unit of
observation, and every one of the six markers at [50] can be fully satisfied by the adults alone while
the teenagers hold an entirely different view of what would happen to *them* if they told the truth.
That room would read as a success on every instrument in the pilot, and the families for whom it was
not safe would quietly stop attending, leaving behind a smaller, warmer, more cohesive-looking cohort —
which the paper would have no way to distinguish from the outcome it hopes for. The author is one of
the very few practitioners I have read who understands that psychological safety is not comfort; his
"response to load" sign and his "sweet frictionless room" warning are better than most of what my own
field produces. All the more reason to say that the fix here is cheap and entirely within his own
ethic: put four plainly worded safety items on the quarterly pulse page that already exists, under the
permission he already asks for, and read the *gap* between the teen and parent answers as data — which
is, after all, exactly what [44] says this whole design is for.
