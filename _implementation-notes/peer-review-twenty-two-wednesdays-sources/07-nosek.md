# Source-Fidelity Review — Brian A. Nosek

**Paper under review:** "Twenty-Two Wednesdays as a Fellowship of the Heart," draft v2.3 (2026-08-18)
**My exposure:** Nosek, Ebersole, DeHaven, and Mellor, "The Preregistration Revolution," *PNAS* 115, no. 11 (2018): 2600–2606 — cited at footnote 2, invoked at [8], and operative across [2], [52]–[59].

A framing note before I start. I am seated here to say whether my own work is represented correctly, and I want to be clear at the outset that I think the author is doing something I would like to see more of, not less. He has written down how he could be wrong before he knows whether he is, in a field where almost nobody does that, and he has done it at length and in public. Most of what follows is about the distance between that instinct and the specific technical thing my co-authors and I were arguing for. The instinct is right. The label is not yet earned. And the gap between them is closable this month, cheaply, without giving up anything the author cares about.

---

## 1. What the paper gets right about my work

**The citation is clean and the paper does not conscript me.** Footnote 2 gives the article, journal, volume, issue, year, and page range correctly. Nothing in the paper claims I endorse this project, this design, or this field. The invocation at [8] is properly attributed to a body of practice — "The academic literature calls publishing a design and its failure conditions before the results 'preregistration'" — rather than to me personally. That is the right register, and I appreciate it.

**The governing instinct at [52] is stated better than most methods sections manage.** "A protocol that cannot fail is a testimony, not a design." That sentence is the whole argument of our paper in nine words, and I would be glad to have written it. It is exactly the distinction between a claim that has been exposed to risk and a claim that has merely been asserted with confidence.

**The paper states its disconfirmation conditions before results exist, in the body of the article, at length.** [53]: "The following are the disconfirmation conditions this pilot carries into its year, stated before the year can answer." Six of them, at [54]–[59], each given its own paragraph rather than a gestural sentence in a limitations section. Whatever my objections below to their specificity, the structural act — publishing the failure conditions in advance, in a venue where they can be held against you — is the substance of what we were asking for. Very few practitioners do this.

**[59] is the best paragraph in the paper on my subject.** "One room, one year, one school, one founder. There is no control cohort; families self-selected; the observer who designed the instruments is also the shepherd who loves the room, a conflict I have tried to bind with covenant and disconfirmation rather than pretend to escape." And then the pre-committed claim language: "The honest claim available at the end of this pilot will not be 'this works' but 'this ran, here is everything it recorded against the predictions it published, and here is the revised design.'" That is a *pre-specified inference constraint*, and it is the single most preregistration-shaped thing in the article. A great many registered protocols never manage to say in advance what conclusion the design will and will not license. This one does, and it calibrates it downward, which is the harder direction.

**Two genuine a priori decision rules already exist at [37], and I suspect the author does not know how much credit he deserves for them.** First: the team "promotes a rung after three consecutive weeks of seen." That is a stopping-and-classification rule fixed before the data, and it removes exactly the degree of freedom that would otherwise let an invested observer promote a room on the strength of one good Wednesday. Second: the team marks "no occasion (an event-contingent sign with no event is missing data, not failure)." That is a missing-data rule specified in advance, and it is specified in the *conservative* direction — it prevents the author from scoring absence as evidence of anything. Those two rules are worth more, methodologically, than the word "preregistration" appearing anywhere in the paper. They should be foregrounded.

**[45] is a demand-characteristic control stated in advance.** "No target is ever announced, because the moment a mirror becomes a goal it stops being a mirror." Whatever else is true, the author has thought about how the act of measuring deforms the thing measured, and has built a rule against it rather than a paragraph acknowledging it. The Strathern citation for that is apt.

**Footnote 8 is open science correctly understood, and correctly reasoned.** "The complete curriculum, handbook, journal, and instruments are published at [URL withheld for blind review]. Publication of full materials is deliberate: a protocol whose instruments cannot be inspected cannot be replicated, extended, or corrected." That rationale is right, it is stated as a rationale rather than a gesture, and it is a distinct contribution from preregistration — see Finding 9.

**[40] reports two failed designs.** "The mechanism was redesigned twice in the field, and the failures are instructive, so I will report them." Publishing what did not work, including the author's own colleague telling him his instrument "felt like being graded," is the behavior the reform literature keeps asking for and rarely gets.

**[9] makes the researcher's position visible at the level of prose.** The first-person/third-person discipline — "A reader should be able to tell at a glance which sentences are a practitioner's testimony and which are warrants that would stand whoever ran the room" — is an unusually thoughtful device for keeping the author's degrees of freedom legible to a reader. I would not have thought of it. It is not a substitute for the fixes below, but it is real.

## 2. Findings

### Finding 1 — "Keeps a researcher honest" is the most common misunderstanding of what preregistration is for. **HIGH**

**[8]:** "The academic literature calls publishing a design and its failure conditions before the results 'preregistration,' and commends it because it keeps a researcher honest."

This is not what our paper argues, and the difference is not pedantic. Preregistration is not primarily a guard against dishonesty. It is a guard against *the ordinary, well-intentioned, invisible flexibility that honest researchers exercise without noticing.* The specific function is to distinguish **confirmatory** analysis (a test the data could fail, because the prediction and the analytic path were fixed before the data were seen) from **exploratory** analysis (hypothesis generation, which is essential and good, but whose apparent evidential strength is inflated because the path was chosen after seeing the data). The mechanism it constrains is *researcher degrees of freedom*: which cases to exclude, which outcome to feature, when to stop collecting, which comparison to report as the one that mattered. None of those choices require bad faith. They are made in good faith, one defensible judgment at a time, and they still hollow out the diagnosticity of the result.

Framing preregistration as a device that "keeps a researcher honest" does two kinds of damage. It tells honest researchers the tool is not for them — which is the single largest reason adoption stalls — and it lets a paper that has adopted the *virtue* of candor believe it has adopted the *mechanism* of constraint. Those are different things, and this paper has the first without the second.

The plain-language gloss that follows — "I am telling you how I could be wrong before I know whether I am" — is a decent statement of the spirit. But notice what it omits: it says nothing about *committing to how the answer will be determined.* A researcher can tell you sincerely how he could be wrong and then, a year later, in complete good faith, determine wrongness by a route he selects from what the year happened to produce.

**Fix.** Replace the clause with something like: the literature commends preregistration because fixing the prediction and the analysis in advance is what makes a test capable of failing — it separates the analyses that could have come out otherwise from the ones discovered in the data. Then keep the kitchen-table sentence, but extend it: "I am telling you how I could be wrong before I know whether I am — and how I will decide."

### Finding 2 — This is not a preregistration, and the paper should not claim the word without qualification. **HIGH**

**[8]:** "The academic literature calls publishing a design and its failure conditions before the results 'preregistration.'"
**[2]:** "The design is presented before its results, with disconfirmation conditions stated in advance."

Publishing a design before results is a *necessary* condition of preregistration and nowhere near a sufficient one. Measured against what the word denotes, this paper is missing every operative element:

- **No registry and no timestamp.** There is no third-party-held, time-stamped, immutable deposit. Without one, "stated in advance" is a claim about the author's own manuscript history, which is precisely the claim preregistration exists to stop requiring readers to take on trust. This is not an accusation of anything; it is the structural point.
- **No specified analysis plan.** How the debrief-sheet readings, counts, and verbatim notes will be aggregated, compared across time points, and evaluated against each condition is nowhere stated.
- **No sample size, anywhere in the paper.** See Finding 3.
- **No designated primary outcome.** See Finding 6.
- **Data collection is already underway.** [2]: "now underway as a school-year pilot." [8]: "a twenty-two-week, family-integrated pilot now underway." [5] narrates the room as it currently runs, and [16] reports "a year and a half of prototyping." So even the temporal condition — registration prior to observation — does not hold. The author has already watched this room.

I want to be careful here, because I think the author is entitled to *more* credit than "not a preregistration" sounds like, and I would rather he claim something accurate and strong than something inflated and weak.

**What he is entitled to call it right now:** a **published protocol** with **pre-stated disconfirmation conditions**. That is an honest, specific, and genuinely uncommon thing. Alternatively — and I think this is the better fit — the genre he has actually written is close to a **Registered Report-style design paper**: a design submitted for review *before* the results exist, against which results are later reported. If JSFSC will accept it in that spirit, that is a stronger claim than "preregistration," not a weaker one, because it adds peer review of the design to the advance commitment.

**What would make the stronger claim earned, and yes, it is feasible now.** A registration after data collection has begun is not disqualifying if the fact is disclosed; it is disqualifying only if it is concealed. An OSF registration is free, takes an afternoon, and produces an immutable time-stamped deposit with a DOI. Deposit today, disclose the start date, and the author has an honest, qualified claim: *a protocol registered mid-pilot, with the pre-registration date and the extent of prior observation stated on the face of the deposit.* Minimum contents I would want in that deposit:

1. **Date data collection began**, and a plain statement of what the author has already observed at the moment of deposit (which sessions have run, what he has already seen in the debrief sheets).
2. **n.** Families, adults, teens, expected sessions, expected consenting respondents for the five pages.
3. **The three break counts**, defined operationally, with **one designated primary**.
4. **The rung promotion rule** ("three consecutive weeks of seen") and the **missing-data rule** ("no occasion" = missing) frozen exactly as written at [37] — they are already good; freezing them costs nothing.
5. **The held / known / owned codebook**, with worked examples, plus who codes, and whether coding is blind to time point (see Finding 7).
6. **An analysis plan, even a fully descriptive one.** "We will report raw counts and trend directions across three time points; no inferential tests will be conducted; no p-values will be reported." That sentence, registered, is a real constraint and it is the honest one for an n of this size.
7. **An attrition and attendance rule**, including a numeric definition of "attends faithfully" (see Finding 5c).
8. **The six disconfirmation conditions**, revised per Findings 4–6.
9. **A statement of what will be reported regardless of outcome** — the author has essentially already written this at [59]; move it into the deposit verbatim.

For blind review, OSF supports anonymized view-only links, which solves footnote 8's "[URL withheld for blind review]" problem and the timestamping problem in the same move.

### Finding 3 — n is never stated. **HIGH**

Searched the paper; there is no number anywhere. [16] says "the cohort is one band of whole families." [21] says "I sit with each family." [34] says "Across the entire year a consenting family member fills out five pages." [50] says "how many walked back in with the return card." Every count in the measurement architecture — break survival, return cards, settle time, participation breadth, survey returns, sign readings — is reported without a denominator, and the denominator is never given.

This matters more, not less, for a design that is honest about being a single case. If the cohort is six families, "flat break-survival" and "improving break-survival" are separated by one family's kitchen table, and a reader needs to know that in order to weigh the year's report at the right strength. If it is twenty-five, some of the counts start to mean something. As written, no reader can calibrate any of it, and — this is the part that touches my subject — neither can the author, in advance, decide what result would count as a change.

**Fix.** State n in [23] or [34]: families, adults, teens, and the number who consented to the five pages. State expected attrition. If consent counts are not final at press time, state the enrolled cohort size and note that the consenting subset will be reported.

### Finding 4 — Preregistration and mid-course adaptation are in real tension, and the paper does not acknowledge that specific tension. **HIGH**

**[44]:** "Pastoral but still listening for what works for this group, updating content as the train is moving as necessary to best serve this group at this juncture."
**[9]:** "the inquiry aims at improving that practice rather than holding it still, and looking and doing alternate in cycles... I changed it after it failed in the field."

The paper acknowledges the *observer-inside-the-room* conflict at [9] and [59], clearly and well, and I will not charge it as unacknowledged. But that is a different conflict from this one. The tension I am naming is: a design that may be revised in response to observations made during the observation period cannot, without further specification, be tested against predictions made before it. If the curriculum that produced the spring readings is not the curriculum described in this article, then "the ladder climbed" is a statement about an unspecified intervention.

Let me be plain about my own position, because it is more permissive than people expect: **preregistration does not prohibit deviation, and it does not prohibit exploration.** Our argument was never that plans must be followed. It is that departures must be *recoverable* — that a reader can tell which parts of the final report were fixed in advance and which were shaped by what was seen. Adaptive designs are entirely compatible with preregistration when the adaptation rules, or at minimum the boundary between frozen and adaptive elements, are specified in advance.

**Fix — and I think this is the most valuable single change available to the paper.** Add a short subsection, most naturally at the end of [52]–[53] or adjacent to [44], with two columns:

- **Frozen for the year** (candidates, all already in the paper): the three vital signs and their aged rungs [36]–[37]; the promotion rule and missing-data rule [37]; the three break counts [50] and their definitions; the five family-facing pages and their items [34]; the permission ethic and the no-scores/no-levels-disclosed rules [21], [45]; the three self-read questions [40]; and the six disconfirmation conditions themselves [54]–[59].
- **Explicitly adaptive**: session content and sequence within a quarter; teaching passages; pacing; which teen leads which night; pastoral follow-up.

Then add: **a dated change log, published with the results**, recording every mid-year change, the observation that prompted it, and the date. That single artifact converts the tension from a problem into a finding — the log becomes some of the most interesting data the pilot produces, and it is exactly the record the successor article on companion formation will want.

### Finding 5 — Four of the six disconfirmation conditions are not falsifiable as written. **MEDIUM**

Assessed strictly as preregistered predictions — can this condition fail, and would we agree afterward that it had?

**(a) [54] "If the ladder never climbs."** The prediction is decent: "first forms steady by the end of the first quarter, growing forms appearing across the middle of the year, full forms at least occasionally in sight by spring." The failure condition is much weaker than the prediction: "A cohort still showing *not yet* on first forms in February, or *no growing form all year*, falsifies either the phasing or the curriculum." Three problems. First, the failure condition retains only the catastrophic floor — a cohort whose first forms arrive in January instead of November and whose growing forms appear in exactly one week in April passes, while flatly violating the prediction. Second, "full forms at least occasionally in sight by spring" is not falsifiable in any form; "in sight" has no observable referent. Third, the disjunct "falsifies either the phasing or the curriculum" leaves the framework itself untouched — nothing named here can be wrong, only its calendar.
*What would make it bite:* commit the prediction as the test, not the floor. E.g., "all three signs reach steady first form (three consecutive weeks *seen*) by session 6; at least two signs reach a growing form by session 14; failure of either constitutes disconfirmation of the phasing as specified, and will be reported as such." And say what a phasing failure *implies* for the framework, if anything.

**(b) [55] "If the sources never disagree."** The structure is right and the conclusion does bite — "the ceiling argument loses its practical force in exactly the setting built to display it" is an honest concession to pre-commit to. But "essentially no discrepancies" has no count rule, and, more importantly, **no pairing is specified**: which self-report item is compared against which observational reading? Without pre-specified pairs, discrepancies will be found post hoc, and with a five-page corpus crossed against a year of debrief sheets, some will be found. That is the researcher-degrees-of-freedom problem in its purest form.
*What would make it bite:* name the pairs in advance — e.g., "the pre/post practice-frequency item against observed telling occasions per person; the pulse 'how present have I been' item against the participation-breadth read for that quarter" — and give a count rule: "fewer than X person-level discrepancies across the year."

**(c) [56] "If nothing survives the breaks better in spring than in fall."** This is the strongest of the six, because [50] supplies three genuinely countable measures and the design schedules the occasions in advance. Three residual problems. **No primary.** Three counts across three time points is three chances to find movement; with a small cohort, at least one will move, and the year's story will be told from whichever did. **No threshold.** "Flat or declining" is undefined — is one family's return card a change? **And an undefined moderator that functions as an escape hatch:** "in a cohort that attends faithfully." If break-survival is flat, attendance can be judged unfaithful after the fact, and the condition dissolves. That clause needs a number before the year starts.
*What would make it bite:* designate one primary count (I would take home-practice survival), state a direction and a minimum change, and define "attends faithfully" numerically in advance — e.g., "families present at ≥ 80% of sessions in the relevant quarter."

**(d) [57] "If the container collapses in younger hands."** Currently unfalsifiable on three counts at once. "Consistently" has no threshold. "Adult rescue" has no operational definition, and it is the kind of judgment an invested adult leader is least positioned to make about himself in the moment. And the closing disjunct — "the entrust-early design is wrong, **or the pace is**" — is a complete escape: pace can always be adjusted, so no observation can disconfirm the design.
*What would make it bite:* define adult rescue as a short pre-specified list of leader interventions, marked by the scribe on the debrief sheet in the moment (leader takes back the floor; leader restates the instruction; leader re-forms the container mid-session). Set a count rule: "≥ 2 of the 3 spring teen-led nights show a rescue event." And commit in advance to what a pace revision means — if the honest answer is that pace failure is a real disconfirmation of the design *as specified in this article*, say so.

**(e) [58] "If the trust vocabulary does not migrate."** Structurally the best of the six, because it is conjunctive: "Stagnant vocabulary **alongside advancing sign-readings** would break the proposed mapping." That is a genuinely risky prediction — it cannot be satisfied by a room that simply did not grow. Remaining weakness: "migrate" is undefined. Migration by whom, across what proportion of utterances, at what magnitude? Three self-reads with a handful of speakers each is a very thin base for a claim about vocabulary shift, and the coding problem is serious enough that I have given it its own finding (Finding 7).
*What would make it bite:* pre-specify the statistic. E.g., "the proportion of utterances coded *owned* at self-read 3 exceeds that at self-read 1, and at least half the speaking members produce at least one *owned*-coded utterance at read 3."

**(f) [59] "And what this year cannot show regardless."** No objection. This is the paragraph doing the most honest work in the section, and it should be moved verbatim into the registry deposit.

**One further note, offered carefully because it is about theology and not method.** [12] states that each level change is "considered a gift of the Holy Spirit," and that arrival at level four is "designed for but never claimed," with its "absence at year's end... not read as failure." The second of those is exactly right and exactly the correct use of preregistration — declaring *in advance* which outcomes are outside the test's scope is the honest move, and I credit it without reservation. My concern is only about interaction: an attribution available for every level change is also available, after a disappointing year, as an explanation for every level change that did not occur. I am not suggesting the author would reach for it in bad faith. I am suggesting that the reason we write these things down beforehand is that none of us can tell, afterward, whether we would have. One sentence stating that the pneumatological frame is not invoked to explain away a negative reading against a stated condition would close it.

### Finding 6 — No primary outcome is designated, and the design produces a large trace set. **MEDIUM**

Counting what the year will generate: three vital signs × three rungs × twenty-two weeks [36]–[37]; three break counts × three re-entries [50]; settle-time trend [27]; participation breadth reads [29]; survey return counts [27]; five family-facing pages pre/post plus three pulses [34]; three self-reads scribed verbatim [40]; six named middle-quarter events [50]; follow-up closure rates [38]. That is a rich and, I want to say clearly, a *well-designed* corpus for a practitioner. It is also a large multiplicity with no designated primary, no pre-specified aggregation, and no correction of any kind — including the informal kind, which is simply saying beforehand which trace is the one that counts.

Here is the specific risk, and it is not a statistical risk, because there will be no statistics. **It is a narrative risk.** At the end of a hard and loving year, the author will sit down to write what happened. Every trace above will have moved in some direction. A true, moving, and entirely sincere account of the year can be assembled from whichever ones moved favorably, and neither the author nor any reader will be able to tell afterward whether that account was found or selected. That is the failure mode preregistration exists to prevent, and in a single-case pastoral study it is the *dominant* failure mode — far more consequential than anything the absence of p-values would cost.

**Fix.** One designated primary trace per disconfirmation condition, named in the deposit and in the paper. And a commitment to report all six conditions in the results article whether or not they came out well — which [59] already implies and should state outright.

### Finding 7 — The trust-vocabulary coding is unblinded and non-independent, and the fix is nearly free. **MEDIUM**

**[41]:** "the scribe's verbatim notes from three self-reads will show whether it did." **[41]:** "Our team keeps the mapping in house words on its side of the paper."
**[58]:** "Three self-reads, scribed verbatim, will show whether the room's unprompted language moves from held toward owned."

The team that formulated the migration hypothesis will code the transcripts that test it, knowing which transcript is from September and which is from April, having lived every session in between, and hoping — reasonably and humanly — for a particular result. Verbatim scribing is a real methodological asset here and I credit it; it makes the following fix possible at essentially zero cost.

**Fix.** Strip dates and identifying context from the three transcripts, shuffle their order, and have them coded held/known/owned by someone who was not in the room and does not know which is which — a colleague, a seminary student, anyone outside the team. Have a second person code a subset and report simple agreement. Register the codebook, with worked examples, *before* the coder sees anything. If the vocabulary genuinely migrated, blind coding will find it, and the finding will then be worth reporting. If only unblinded coding finds it, that is worth knowing too.

### Finding 8 — "Registering" is used in a project-internal sense four paragraphs from an invocation of preregistration. **LOW**

**[58]:** "which this project has begun registering as a formal research trail."

I take this to mean an internal research register maintained by the project. Sitting this close to [8]'s preregistration claim, it reads to a methods-attentive reader as though a registry deposit exists. Change the verb — "which this project has opened as a formal research trail" — or, better, make the sentence true by actually depositing it.

### Finding 9 — Open materials are a distinct contribution from preregistration and are currently folded into it. **LOW**

**[24] and footnote 8:** "The full session plans, handbook, journal, and every card and instrument this article mentions are published openly."

This is genuinely good practice and I do not want it lost inside the preregistration discussion, because it does different work. Open materials serve inspection, replication, and correction — footnote 8 says exactly this, correctly. They do nothing for the confirmatory/exploratory distinction. Preregistration serves the second and nothing for the first. A paper that has both should say it has both, in two sentences rather than one, and take credit twice. See Section 4 for the citation I would attach to the open-materials strand.

## 3. Where the author goes beyond me, and whether he says so

**Extension 1 — from a mechanism for protecting confirmatory inference to a general posture of advance candor.** [8]. **Under-declared.** The phrase "The kitchen-table version is simpler" signals informality of *expression*, which is not the same as informality of *application*. A reader is left with the impression that the author has done a plain-spoken version of preregistration, when what he has done is a different thing that shares its motivating spirit. One sentence — "I am not preregistering in the technical sense; there is no deposit and no analysis plan, and what follows is the spirit of the practice rather than its machinery" — would convert this from an undeclared extension into a declared one, and would cost the paper nothing but a line.

**Extension 2 — a redefinition of the term, not an extension of it.** [8] defines preregistration as "publishing a design and its failure conditions before the results." That is not an extension of the concept into a new setting; it is a substitution of a weaker concept under the same name. **Not flagged at all.** This is the one I would most want fixed, because a reader in spiritual formation encountering the word here will carry that definition forward into other work.

**Extension 3 — applying the term while data collection is already underway.** [2], [8], [16]. **Not acknowledged anywhere.** The paper is candid about nearly every other limit it carries — [59] is exemplary — which makes the silence here look like oversight rather than evasion, and it is easily repaired: state the start date and say the protocol is being published mid-pilot.

**Extension 4 — from constraint on analysis to constraint on nothing operational.** The disconfirmation conditions at [54]–[58] do not constrain any analytic decision, because no analysis is specified. **Not flagged.** The author may reasonably reply that there is nothing to constrain in a descriptive case study. My answer is Finding 6: the thing being constrained is not the statistic, it is the story.

**Extension 5 — legitimate and properly declared, credited here so the audit is balanced.** [59] declines to lean on my name, or anyone's, for generalizability: "A quarter's worth of readings will be thin; a year's will still be one case." He does not claim that publishing failure conditions makes a single case generalizable. He explicitly says it does not. That is the reach he *could* have made and did not, and in my experience it is the reach most authors make.

**Extension 6 — the two a priori rules at [37] are, in substance, preregistration practice arrived at independently.** The rung-promotion rule and the missing-data rule were not derived from my literature and are not attributed to it. That is fine — they are the author's own, developed from pastoral necessity. I note them here only to say that the paper's strongest claim to the practice comes from a place it does not think to point at.

## 4. What I would want cited differently

1. **Keep the 2018 PNAS citation at footnote 2; change the claim it supports.** Attach it to the confirmatory/exploratory distinction and to the constraint on researcher degrees of freedom, not to researcher honesty.

2. **If the author wants the "keeps you honest" idea done properly, the right sources are about honest researchers, not dishonest ones.** Simmons, Nelson, and Simonsohn's 2011 *Psychological Science* paper on false-positive psychology is the canonical demonstration that ordinary analytic flexibility, exercised in good faith, produces false positives at alarming rates. John, Loewenstein, and Prelec (2012, *Psychological Science*) documents how widespread the relevant practices are. Either supports the point [8] is reaching for far better than our paper does, and both reframe the moral: the tool is for people who are trying to get it right.

3. **Cite the Registered Report literature, because that is the genre this paper actually occupies.** A design reviewed before results exist, against which results are later reported, is a Registered Report in shape. Chambers' work on Registered Reports is the standard reference, and Nosek and Lakens' 2014 *Social Psychology* editorial introducing a registered-reports special issue is a compact one. If the author reframes the claim per Finding 2, this becomes the citation that carries it — and it is a more flattering frame than the one he currently uses.

4. **Engage the qualitative-preregistration debate rather than importing a quantitative norm wholesale.** There is a live and serious literature on whether and how preregistration applies to qualitative and practice-based research — Haven and Van Grootel's article on preregistering qualitative research (*Accountability in Research*, 2019) is the usual entry point; the author should verify the exact citation. Some of that literature would push back on this paper's use of the term, and some would supply him with a defensible version of it. Either way, a single footnote showing he knows he is entering a contested area would immunize the paper against the most obvious methodological objection — and it fits the author's evident temperament, which is to name the objection before a reviewer does.

5. **Do not cite me for the open-materials practice at [24] and footnote 8; there is a better source.** Nosek et al., "Promoting an Open Research Culture," *Science* 348, no. 6242 (2015): 1422–1425 — the TOP Guidelines paper — is the appropriate citation for materials transparency, and it separates the transparency dimensions (data, materials, design and analysis, preregistration) in a way that would help the author see that he currently scores well on one and not yet on another.

6. **[58]: change "registering."** See Finding 8.

7. **A small structural suggestion, not a citation.** If the author deposits per Finding 2, footnote 8's "[URL withheld for blind review]" can become an anonymized OSF view-only link, which gives reviewers the materials, preserves blinding, and establishes the timestamp in one action.

## 5. My single biggest concern

The paper claims the word "preregistration" while lacking every mechanism the word denotes — no deposit, no timestamp, no analysis plan, no sample size anywhere in the manuscript, no designated primary outcome, and a pilot already running — and it does so in a design whose real vulnerability is not statistical but narrative. This year will generate three vital signs on three rungs across twenty-two weeks, three break counts at three re-entries, three scribed self-reads, and a dozen other traces, all of them recorded by a team that designed them, loves the room, and will write the year's account. Something in that corpus will have moved favorably; a sincere, accurate, and genuinely moving story can be assembled from whichever parts did, and afterward neither the author nor any reader will be able to tell whether that story was discovered or selected. That is exactly the harm preregistration exists to prevent, and it is more acute here than in the psychology experiments the practice was built for, not less, because there are no p-values to make the problem visible. The remedy is small and available this month: deposit the protocol on OSF with an honest note that the pilot began on such a date and that the author has already observed X sessions; state n; name one primary trace per disconfirmation condition; define "adult rescue," "attends faithfully," and "migrate" numerically before the spring; declare which commitments are frozen and which are explicitly adaptive, and keep a dated change log; and have the three self-read transcripts coded blind to time point by someone outside the team. Do those six things and the author will have earned a qualified but genuine claim — a protocol registered mid-pilot, with its predictions fixed and its constraints public — which is both more accurate and, in a field where nobody does any of this, considerably more impressive than the unqualified word he is currently using.
