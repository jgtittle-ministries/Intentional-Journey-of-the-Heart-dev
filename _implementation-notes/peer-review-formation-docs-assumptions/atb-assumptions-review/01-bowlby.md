# Foundational-Assumptions Review: "ATB — Attachment Theory and the Biblical Triad"

**Reviewer: John Bowlby, MD**

I have read this working draft with interest and, I confess, some pleasure. It is not often one sees one's theory handled by a religious writer who has actually read the literature rather than borrowed its vocabulary. I review it on its scientific merits, as I would any application; the theological claims I leave to theologians, noting only where they lean on the science.

## 1. The load-bearing assumptions as I read them

1. **Attachment theory correctly describes a durable relational substrate** — an evolved behavioral system producing internal working models that shape adult relating, classifiable into four patterns.
2. **God can function as an attachment figure**: the believer's relationship with God engages the attachment system proper (safe haven, secure base), not merely resembles it poetically.
3. **The substrate shapes receivability**: attachment patterns color how a person receives the Word, prays, and engages formation — the paper's central diagnostic claim.
4. **Working models are revisable** — "earned security" is attainable through gospel encounter and reliable church community.
5. **The Sower's four soils correspond to the four patterns**, region by region of a heart, and a person may run different patterns in different "regions."
6. **Mature attachment is the held capacity for all four sensitivities** with secure as regulating baseline (Section 11).

## 2. Affirmations

Section 2 is a creditable summary. The behavioral system, the working models, the caregiving conditions producing each pattern, the internal-working-model formulations ("I am only safe when proximity is verified") — these are accurate and well-phrased. The paper says plainly that patterns are "tendencies, not destinies," shaped by experience and revisable by experience. That is precisely what I argued, against the determinists on one side and the dismissers on the other. Section 13's cautions — that temperament, culture, and much else shape behavior; that attachment is "one true lens among several" — perform the self-limitation most enthusiasts omit. The insistence on collaboration with licensed clinicians, on consent, boundaries, and rupture repair, reflects sound clinical instinct. And the operational emphasis throughout — consistency, attunement, predictable presence — correctly identifies sensitive responsiveness as the active ingredient. The author has grasped the theory's mechanism, not merely its taxonomy.

## 3. Challenges

**C1. Attribution and provenance of the fourth pattern.** *Assumption 1.* Section 2 states that Ainsworth's work "identified the now-classic four attachment patterns." It did not. Ainsworth's Strange Situation yielded three classifications — secure, anxious-ambivalent, avoidant. The disorganized category is Main and Solomon's later addition, resting on different observational criteria and carrying a distinct evidence base (heavily trauma-linked, less stable, differently distributed). The paper's own bibliography gets this right ("later supplemented by the disorganized category"), so the body text contradicts its own citation. **Severity: Minor** — but a foundational document must be exact, and the correction matters for C4 below.

**C2. God as attachment figure — the criteria must be argued, not assumed by metaphor.** *Assumption 2.* An attachment figure is defined functionally: proximity-seeking under threat, safe haven, secure base, separation distress. The paper maps biblical images (rock, fortress, shepherd) onto my terms, which is resonance, not demonstration. The hard question is whether an unseen, non-responsive-in-the-ordinary-sense figure can satisfy the criteria. Kirkpatrick and Granqvist — whom the paper cites — argued yes, with qualifications: proximity is symbolic, maintained through prayer and ritual; availability is *believed* rather than perceived. I find their case respectable. But the qualification has a consequence the paper underplays: because divine responsiveness is mediated, the *human* community carries a disproportionate share of the corrective load. The paper's own emphasis on the church partly concedes this; it should own it explicitly. **Severity: Significant.**

**C3. Category discipline — by what measurement is a pattern assigned?** *Assumptions 3 and 5.* This is the famous fault-line in my field. The Strange Situation classifies infants; the Adult Attachment Interview classifies discourse about childhood; self-report scales (ECR and kin) measure something correlated with but not identical to either — the developmental and social-psychological traditions notoriously disagree. Moreover, modern adult research treats attachment dimensionally (anxiety × avoidance), not as four boxes. When ATB proposes that a facilitator, a practice-card tracker, or a Sower diagnostic assigns a person — let alone a *region of a heart* — a pattern, no validated instrument warrants the assignment. The Future Work section gestures at Beck and McDonald's Attachment to God Inventory, which is the right instinct, but until then every diagnostic claim in Sections 8–10 rests on informal impression. **Severity: Foundational** for the empirical program; Significant for pastoral use if held loosely.

**C4. The soils-to-patterns table is elegant beyond its evidence.** *Assumption 5.* The paper says the mapping is "not one-to-one or causal," and I credit that sentence — but the table will be read as one-to-one; tables always are. The weakest cell is thorny soil = disorganized. Disorganization is the rarest pattern, tightly bound to frightening caregiving; the parable's thorns — riches, cares, competing desires — afflict many people who are secure by any measure. A wealthy, distracted, securely attached man is thorny soil with no disorganization whatever. **Severity: Significant.**

**C5. "Disorganized awareness" as a retained virtue.** *Assumption 6.* Section 11's integration move works tolerably for anxious sensitivity and avoidant discernment, but disorganization is not a sensitivity — it is the *collapse* of strategy, fright without solution. Rebranding it as "capacity to hold contradictions" romanticizes a breakdown. Capacity for paradox is real and valuable; it is not the residue of disorganized attachment, and survivors deserve not to have their injury renamed a gift. **Severity: Significant.**

## 4. Confirmations

The core clinical hope holds. Working models *can* be revised through later relationships — I said so repeatedly, and the therapy-outcome and earned-security literatures since have borne it out. A reliable, boundaried, attuned community functioning as a corrective secure base is a legitimate transposition of what I claimed a therapist provides. The correspondence/compensation summary is accurate to Kirkpatrick. The distinction between saving faith and psychological trust capacity keeps the theory in its lane admirably. Domain-specific attachment (Assumption 5's better half) is genuinely supported: relationship-specific working models are established findings, though "regions of a heart" is a new construct requiring operational definition. And the caution against attachment explaining too much is present and sincere.

## 5. My single biggest concern

It is the measurement question, and I say so as one who spent his life insisting that observation precede theory. This paper's diagnostic architecture — soils, regions, pattern-signatures, facilitator judgments — will be operated by pastors and lay companions assigning categories to souls, and there is at present no instrument behind the assignment. In my experience the danger of a good taxonomy is not that it is wrong but that it is *used* — confidently, kindly, and without evidence — until the label precedes the person. The author's instincts are humane and his cautions genuine; let him now bind the diagnostic claims to the empirical test he himself proposes in Section 15, and hold every pattern-assignment as a hypothesis about a person rather than a fact about one. The theory can bear the weight he places on it; the measurements, as yet, cannot.
