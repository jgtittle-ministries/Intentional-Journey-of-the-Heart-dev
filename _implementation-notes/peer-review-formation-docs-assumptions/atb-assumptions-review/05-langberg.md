# Foundational-Assumptions Review: ATB — Attachment Theory and the Biblical Triad
**Reviewer: Diane Langberg, Ph.D. | Lens: safeguarding and the wounded person**

## 1. The load-bearing assumptions as I read them

1. **The variance is relational, not culpable.** Differences in how hearts receive the Word are shaped by relational history — attachment substrate — and this substrate is not sin and does not affect one's standing before God.
2. **The local church can be the ecology of earned security.** Reliable, boundaried, attuned Christian community is the ordinary means by which the Spirit repatterns insecure attachment.
3. **The substrate work is lay-deliverable.** TPM, practice cards, and the integration matrix can be run in FotH small groups, with covenant safeguards and referral to licensed counselors handling what exceeds the group.
4. **Attachment patterns can be safely named and observed.** Facilitators can learn the four-pattern vocabulary, recognize patterns in group members, and record observations for both pastoral care and research without harm.
5. **The Sower soils correlate with attachment patterns** — including thorny soil with disorganized attachment "most often."
6. **Healing is directional, not promised.** Some patterns will not fully repattern in this life; grace is patient with the substrate.

## 2. Affirmations

I want to say plainly what this paper gets right, because much of it is what I have spent fifty years asking the church to say. The distinction between saving faith and psychological trust capacity — "a profoundly insecurely-attached believer is no less saved" — is stated not once but twice, and made structural (substrate of sanctification, not ground of justification). The paper names that treating therapy as spiritual failure "is itself a pastoral malformation." It refuses prosperity healing timetables: "some attachment patterns will not fully heal in this life," anchored in 2 Cor 12:9. It knows charismatic practice "can also retraumatize when done badly" and names the specific mechanisms — pressuring the anxious to perform, demanding vulnerability from the avoidant, dysregulating the disorganized. Component 4's covenant elements — consent for touch, prayer, and questions; time limits; rupture repair; the Exit/Return plan giving a disorganized person a pre-agreed, dignified way to leave the room — are real safeguards, not decoration. And the disorganized pattern is routed, repeatedly, to trauma-informed care and a competent Christian counselor. The instincts here are sound and, frankly, rare.

## 3. Challenges

**Challenge 1 — Safeguarding is described, not architected.** *Assumption at stake: #3.* Section 13, which the framework treats as the cautions section, is entirely theological caution — scope of theory, Creator-creature distinction, cultural variability, cessationist charity. Every one is wise. None is a safety system. The actual safeguarding lives scattered in Section 10 Component 4 and Section 12, and what is present (covenant, consent, SUDS, referral language) is necessary but not sufficient. Missing: screening or intake before a person enters attachment-focused group work; a competence floor and training pathway for facilitators; supervision of facilitators; an explicit protocol for when TPM surfaces more than the group can hold — and it will, because TPM's MAP deliberately descends through EMOTION to MEMORY, and memory work with trauma survivors is precisely where lay competence ends; an explicit right to stop (Exit/Return is a right to pause; a person must also be free to leave the work entirely without spiritual penalty). This is the same finding as the standing LANGBERG-2 safeguarding-architecture item from the Formation Companion review: the project keeps writing the right heart and deferring the load-bearing structure. ATB raises the stakes, because it brings trauma histories into the room by design. **Severity: Foundational.**

**Challenge 2 — Pattern-naming is treated as benign; it is power.** *Assumption at stake: #4.* Future Work #9 trains facilitators to recognize the four patterns in group members and "record observations in a way that supports both pastoral care and research." Practice cards, 8-week trackers, matrices — these are records about people's inner wounds, held by lay leaders, with dual pastoral/research use and no stated governance: who holds them, who may see them, how consent to the research use is obtained separately from consent to the care. And the vocabulary itself: a leader who can say "she's disorganized" holds diagnostic-sounding power over a member. The paper models non-shaming language beautifully ("It makes sense you hesitate...") but has no rule preventing labels from becoming verdicts, gossip, or leverage — no self-identification-only norm, no prohibition on leaders assigning patterns aloud or in records the member hasn't seen. **Severity: Significant** (Foundational if the research use proceeds without a consent architecture).

**Challenge 3 — The thorny-soil/disorganized mapping risks re-moralizing trauma.** *Assumption at stake: #5, threatening #1.* In Jesus' parable, the thorns are cares, riches, and desires — the reception failures read culpably in most preaching. Mapping disorganized attachment (which the paper correctly says arises from abuse, severe neglect, caregiver trauma) onto thorny soil means an abuse survivor sitting in a group may hear: your trauma response is the thorn choking the Word. The paper's hedges — "most often," "not one-to-one or causal," correlational — are correct, but hedges in a working draft do not survive into a Tuesday-night small group. The paper's own deepest claim, that the variance is relational and not culpable, is undermined by its most quotable table unless the non-culpability is printed into the pastoral surface itself. **Severity: Significant.**

**Challenge 4 — The church-as-safe-ecology assumption is asserted, not conditioned.** *Assumption at stake: #2.* The paper says the church "when reliable and boundaried" echoes the safe haven — the conditional is right, but nothing addresses when the leader is the rupture. It even notes that disorganized attachment is especially triggered by spiritual-authority figures, then builds no accountability for those figures: no independent complaint path, no leader-abuse provision, no acknowledgment that for many survivors the church was the original wound. An ecology of earned security must plan for its own failure modes. **Severity: Significant.**

**Challenge 5 — Referral is invoked but not triggered.** *Assumption at stake: #3.* "Almost always collaboration with a competent Christian counselor" is the right instinct, and I credit it as load-bearing in intent. But who decides, on what criteria, and what happens if the person declines? Without named triggers (e.g., disclosed abuse history, dissociation in session, SUDS that won't come down, suicidality) and a named decision-holder, "almost always" becomes "when the facilitator happens to notice." **Severity: Significant.**

## 4. Confirmations

Assumption #1 holds, and it is a mercy — the church has punished trauma responses as rebellion for generations, and a framework declaring the variance relational and non-culpable protects the very people it serves. Assumption #6 holds: "hope does not rest on achieved secure attachment" is the sentence a survivor most needs, and the paper puts it in the conclusion where it belongs. The empirical base (Granqvist, Mikulincer & Shaver) is honestly handled — tendencies, not destinies; common grace, not revelation. The integration-not-replacement reading of the four patterns, which honors what the wounded have learned rather than treating them as deficient copies of the secure, is genuinely humane. And Section 13's theological modesty is real self-critique; it should be credited even as I ask it to grow a second, structural half.

## 5. My single biggest concern

The gap between this paper's heart and its architecture. The heart is right — I have rarely read a formation document so careful to say that the wounded are not less saved, less loved, or less soil. But the paper hands lay facilitators a memory-descending prayer practice, a diagnostic vocabulary, and record-keeping tools, and covers the risk with instincts rather than structures. Instincts do not protect people; structures do. When — not if — a TPM session surfaces an abuse memory in a church basement, the question will not be whether grace is patient with the substrate; it will be whether anyone in that room was screened, trained, supervised, and required to know where competence ends. The person in front of that facilitator is holy ground. Before one group runs, the safeguarding must move from Sections 10 and 12's descriptions into a required, auditable architecture — screening, consent, stop rights, referral triggers, supervision, data governance — and that is LANGBERG-2, again, now with higher stakes. Build it, and this paper becomes what it wants to be: a mercy.
