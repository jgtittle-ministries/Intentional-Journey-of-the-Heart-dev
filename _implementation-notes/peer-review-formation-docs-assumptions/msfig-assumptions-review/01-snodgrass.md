# Foundational-Assumptions Review: MSFIG's Use of the Parable of the Sower

**Reviewer: Klyne Snodgrass** — Document 4 review, Sower mapping

---

## 1. The load-bearing assumptions as I read them

1. **The Sower is "the primary scriptural test" of the Affective Taxonomy** — the parable grounds, not merely decorates, the five-stage model (Abstract; §3.1).
2. **The four soils plus the graded harvest map onto five sequential stages**: path = Receiving; rocky = Responding; thorny = Valuing; good soil at 30/60-fold = Organization; 100-fold = Characterization, "the mind of Christ" (§3.2 table).
3. **Mark 4:13 makes hearing-and-obeying the meta-discipline** — because this parable is the key to all parables, hearing is the foundation of all disciplines (§2.2, §3.1, §8).
4. **The 30/60/100-fold yields signify progressive depths of internalization**, with suffering as "fertilizer enabling movement from 30-fold toward 100-fold" (§2.4).
5. **Soils can change** — the model assumes movement between soil-types is possible and describable (implicit in the whole formation architecture; explicit in the spiral of §6.3).
6. **The taxonomy is descriptive of the human side only**; the Spirit is the irreducible agent, and sovereign work "can occur at any level" (§3.1).

## 2. Affirmations

First, and I want to say this emphatically: the paper has seized the parable's actual theme. The Sower is *about hearing*. It is framed by "Listen!" (Mark 4:3) and "Let anyone with ears to hear, hear" (4:9); the interpretation uses "hear" of every soil-type. A formation model whose core is hearing-and-obeying God has not imported a foreign concern into this text — it has found the text's own concern. Second, the hear-*and-obey* unity is canonically sound even where Mark 4:13 alone won't carry it: *shema* in the Hebrew Scriptures means hearing that issues in doing (Deut. 6:4ff.; 28:1), and the good soil "accepts" and "bears fruit" (Mark 4:20) — Luke's "hold it fast... with patient endurance" (8:15) makes obedience internal to the parable's own definition of good hearing. Third, Luke 8:15's "noble and good heart" (*kardia kalē kai agathē*) is genuine heart-language; the paper's heart-focus has a textual foothold. Fourth, the Pharisee counter-example (§3.3) preserves the parable's warning function — behavioral compliance is not transformation — and that is exactly the kind of self-examination the parable demands of its hearers. Fifth, the paper's repeated epistemic humility (descriptive not prescriptive; 1 Sam. 16:7; the Spirit's priority; sudden change "at any level") is real self-critique, and it inadvertently protects the paper from its own mapping, as I'll show.

## 3. Challenges

**Challenge 1: Failure-types read as progress-stages.** *(Assumption 2 at stake.)* The parable's own interpretation — and this is one of the few parables that comes with one, so we must take it seriously — presents the first three soils as three kinds of *unfruitful* hearing: the word snatched (Satan), the word abandoned under pressure (no root), the word choked (cares, wealth, desires). All three end in failure; none is a way-station. But the paper's table makes rocky soil Stage 2 ("initial response, shallow roots") and thorny soil Stage 3 — "real growth, but distractions still compete" — where Stage 3 is elsewhere defined as the *critical* shift to intrinsic motivation. The text will not bear this. Thorny-soil hearing is not the valuing stage of formation; it is a hearing that "proves unfruitful" (Mark 4:19). The parable classes the thorny hearer with the failures, not the almost-mature. Mapping the taxonomy's ascending ladder onto soils Jesus arranged as a typology of outcomes inverts the interpretation's own verdict. **Severity for IJH: Foundational** — not for the taxonomy itself, which stands on Krathwohl and on broader canonical hear-and-obey theology, but for the claim that the Sower is the model's "primary scriptural test." As currently mapped, the parable does not test the taxonomy; the taxonomy re-sorts the parable.

**Challenge 2: Typology vs. developmental sequence.** *(Assumptions 2 and 5.)* The parable narrates no movement between soils. It is a synchronic picture of how the one word fares in four conditions — a prophetic explanation of the kingdom proclamation's mixed reception and an implicit summons: *what kind of hearer are you?* Now, fairness requires me to add: the summons itself presupposes that hearing can change, and the prophetic background the imagery draws on — "Break up your fallow ground, and do not sow among thorns" (Jer. 4:3; cf. Hos. 10:12) — is precisely an imperative to *cultivate the soil*. So the pastoral tradition of reading the soils as a call to self-cultivation is not illegitimate; it stands on the parable's function, not its plot. But function and plot must be distinguished. The parable *warns* that soils differ; it does not *chart* how a path becomes good ground. What hangs on the difference: a formation model needs stages and transitions; this parable supplies types and a warning. The transitions must be supplied from elsewhere in Scripture (and the paper's Brueggemann/dark-night material actually does this better). **Severity: Significant.**

**Challenge 3: 30/60/100-fold as intra-good-soil maturity grades.** *(Assumption 4.)* Here the text gives partial support and partial resistance. Partial support: the variation is real — good soil is not uniform, and fruitfulness differs among genuine hearers. Matthew even reverses the order (100/60/30), which at least shows the tradition felt free with the sequence. Partial resistance: all three yields are *good soil*; all "hear the word and accept it and bear fruit." The parable pronounces one verdict over all three, and the numbers function rhetorically as abundant harvest assuring that despite the failures, the sowing succeeds gloriously. Splitting the good soil so that 30/60-fold = Stage 4 and 100-fold = Stage 5 ("the mind of Christ") erects a hierarchy inside the one category the parable declines to subdivide. **Severity: Significant** — the softer claim (graded fruitfulness within genuine reception) is defensible; the harder claim (yields = taxonomy stages) is not.

**Challenge 4: Kingdom context thinned.** *(Assumptions 1 and 3.)* In its setting the parable explains the fate of *Jesus' proclamation of the kingdom* amid opposition — why the Messiah's own preaching meets rejection, and why harvest is certain anyway. The paper reads it almost entirely as an anthropology of receptivity. The Spirit-primacy and warning elements mitigate this, but the eschatological assurance — God's sowing *will* succeed — goes unused, which is a loss, since it is the parable's deepest encouragement to any discouraged sower (including a small-group leader). Also, Mark 4:13 makes this parable hermeneutically foundational *for understanding parables*; extending it to "the meta-discipline of all spiritual disciplines" is an inference, not the verse's statement. A defensible inference — but tier it as such. **Severity: Minor.**

## 4. Confirmations

The parable *does* confirm: (a) hearing is the decisive human variable in the word's fruitfulness — the seed and sower are constant, the soils differ; (b) the heart is the receptive medium (Luke 8:15; Matt. 13:19 "sown in the heart"); (c) fruit, not initial enthusiasm, is the criterion of genuine reception — which is exactly the paper's Stage-2-vs-Stage-5 Pharisee point; (d) obstacles are nameable and differentiated (the evil one, persecution, cares and riches) — a genuine diagnostic warrant; (e) differing degrees of fruitfulness exist among true hearers. That is a substantial confirmation set. The hear-and-obey core of IJH is the parable's own theme, and no exegete can take that away from this project.

## 5. My single biggest concern

My concern is that the paper has made its strongest text carry its weakest claim. The Sower confirms the *theme* of this whole corpus — hearing that bears fruit — about as directly as any text in the Gospels could; but the paper stakes the parable instead on the *five-stage mapping*, where the fit is worst, because it must recast three portraits of failed hearing as three rungs of a ladder toward success. Jesus' interpretation, the rare gift of an attached explanation, sorts hearers into outcomes and warns us to examine what kind we are; it does not narrate a path becoming good ground, however much the prophets' "break up your fallow ground" licenses us to preach as if it can. Let the parable do what it does — establish hearing as the meta-discipline, the heart as the soil, fruit as the test, and the named thorns as diagnostics — and let Krathwohl, Deuteronomy 28, John 15, and the dark-night tradition carry the developmental sequence, which they can. The mapping should be re-tiered from "primary scriptural test" to *illustration with thematic confirmation*: the parable confirms the model's center and illustrates its categories, but it does not prove its stages, and a project this honest — and this paper's own humility markers show it knows how to be honest — will be stronger, not weaker, for saying so plainly.
