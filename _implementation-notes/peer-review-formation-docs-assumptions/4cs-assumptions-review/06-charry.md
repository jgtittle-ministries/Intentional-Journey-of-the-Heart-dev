# Peer Review: "Spiritual Formation: Four Connects" (Fellowship of the Heart Teaching Deck, July 2025)
## Reviewer: Ellen T. Charry — Sixth Seating

Before I begin, let me name the genre honestly, because it matters. This is the first document in the series that is not a paper. It is a teaching deck — fifty slides by which a fellowship teaches itself its own practice. I want to say plainly that this genre is not beneath theological review; it is, in fact, the genre my beloved ancient sources knew best. The catechumenate did not hand inquirers monographs. It handed them scripture, exercises, a community, and a rule of life. So I will judge this deck as catechesis, and I will hold it to catechesis's standard: does it teach the faith's *content* alongside the faith's *exercises*?

## 1. The load-bearing assumptions as I read them

1. **Formation is sanctification** (slide 7: "Called spiritual formation. Also called sanctification") — the deck's process and the Spirit's work are presented as two names for one thing.
2. **The heart speaks a different language than the mind**, and so "unusual tools" — imagery, parable, anchoring, relaxation — are warranted alongside propositional teaching (slide 7: "imagery and parable for heart language").
3. **Four relationships exhaust the field of formation**: self, others, God, mission — and working each connect works the whole person.
4. **Scripture grounds and governs the process** — every connect opens with a substantial scripture text (Luke 6, 1 John 4, Matthew 7, John 14, Matthew 13, Matthew 25, 1 Corinthians 3), and the social contract names "Scripture as the basis and grounding for everything" (slide 48).
5. **Individual process precedes and feeds group process** — write, then share; pairs, then group — and confidentiality makes the sharing safe (slide 48).
6. **Mountaintop experiences do not transfer to the valley without deliberate work** — hence the ecology check, the deck's own built-in suspicion of its own exercises (slides 38, 41).

## 2. Affirmations

The scriptural spine of the definition slides is, quite simply, the aretegenic tradition's own canon. Romans 12:2 (transformation by renewal of mind), Ephesians 4:13–16 (growing up into the Head, the body building itself in love), and above all Hebrews 5:14 — *powers of discernment trained by constant practice* — that verse is nearly a one-sentence summary of my entire argument in *By the Renewing of Your Minds*. Whoever chose these texts understands that Christian doctrine was always meant to be aretegenic: to form excellent persons, not merely correct ones. Credit where it is deeply due.

Second: the deck teaches the practice *openly*. Nothing is esoteric, nothing withheld for initiates. The exercises are named, the steps shown, the discernment standards listed (slide 28: "aligns with Scripture, reason, witness of Spirit, peace, community, circumstances" — a genuinely sound composite test). This transparency is what the catechumenate did and what manipulative groups do not do.

Third: the ecology check. "How many times have you been to the mountaintop — but nothing changed back in the valley?" (slide 38). A fellowship that builds skepticism about its own peak experiences into its curriculum, and asks Luke 14's cost-counting question before inviting commitment, has learned something the revivalist tradition often did not. And the closing invitation — "Theosis is the goal. Community is the context. Loving God and neighbor is the expression" (slides 46, 50) — is as fine a three-line summary of patristic soteriology as one could ask from a slide.

Fourth: the Four Questions (slide 17) — "How have I wounded you?" asked of another person — is confession and reconciliation in operational form. That is courage taught as curriculum.

## 3. Challenges

**Challenge 1 — The sanctification equation.** *Assumption at stake: #1.* Slide 7 says formation is "also called sanctification." But the deck's own sanctification texts on slide 4 say otherwise: "God chose you... through sanctification *by the Spirit*"; "I, the Lord, sanctify you"; "may the God of peace *himself* sanctify you completely." In every verse the grammatical subject of sanctification is God. The Four Connects process is a set of practices by which we present ourselves for that work — the training of Hebrews 5:14, yes, but training receives the Spirit's work; it does not constitute it. The equation, read carelessly, elevates the process to the Spirit's office. One clause would humble it: "practices that open us to sanctification, which is God's work." The deck's own slide 4 already contains the correction; slide 7 simply doesn't cite it against itself. **Severity: Significant** — not Foundational, because the deck's texts self-correct, but the teaching line as spoken could form process-confidence rather than Spirit-dependence.

**Challenge 2 — What orders the connects?** *Assumption at stake: #3.* The sequence runs self → others → God → mission, and God is formally the third of four stations. Augustine would ask what I must ask: is God one connect among four, or the center that orders the other three? The *ordo amoris* insists that love of self and neighbor are only rightly ordered when referred to God. There is evidence the deck intends God as center — the self-connect opens by getting "present to the great 'I Am'" and invites Jesus into the garden (slides 10–11); the others-connect names the irritation point as "there for a reason — your sanctification" (slide 16); the mission criteria are theological through and through (slide 36). God is inside every connect. But the architecture doesn't *say* so, and architecture teaches. A single framing slide — "the fourth connect is not one among equals; the other three only work because of it" — would align the stated structure with the actual practice. **Severity: Significant.**

**Challenge 3 — Where is the off-ramp for the one the exercises fail?** *Assumption at stake: #2, #5.* This is my standing question, and at ATB it was finally answered — the sufferer was centered there via the Romans 5 spine. But this is the *operational* deck, the one actually projected in the room, and I cannot find her in it. The imagery exercises assume the member can recall "a time when you felt connected to God" (slide 20) — Mother Teresa could not have completed that slide for fifty years. "Connect with self" opens the garden of the heart; for the traumatized, that garden may hold things a lay group cannot safely tend. The deck has confidentiality and permission-based sharing (good), but no visible category for the participant for whom the anchor won't set, no word that dryness may be God's severe mercy rather than the participant's failure. The theology exists elsewhere in the corpus; the room needs one slide of it. **Severity: Significant** — in a deck, absence teaches.

**Challenge 4 — The unattributed toolkit.** *Assumption at stake: #2, #4.* "Anchoring," visual/auditory/kinesthetic representations, ecology check — these are NLP-derived techniques, serving here under scriptural governance, and I accept the corpus's integration argument. But the deck presents them as simply "unusual tools" without telling the congregation where they came from. Catechesis owes its people honesty about its instruments; a sentence of provenance would inoculate against the discovery-by-Google problem. **Severity: Minor** for the deck; worth a footnote in IJH's foundations.

**Challenge 5 — Slide 28's illustrative questions.** "What does it feel like to be a white male? What does a fish think about water?" The fish works; the first question imports a cultural flashpoint into a slide about divine omnipresence and will, for some hearers, detonate there. **Severity: Minor** — an illustration choice, not an assumption.

## 4. Confirmations

The catechetical instinct holds: this fellowship teaches its practice openly, scripture-first, with individual work feeding communal discernment, under a social contract with confidentiality — that is the ancient pattern in modern dress. The four-connects map itself holds: Augustine mapped God, self, and neighbor; the deck's addition of mission is not an innovation but the Great Commission taking its own seat at the table, and the Matthew 25 grounding earns it. The shadow-mission/gold-mission reversal (slides 32–37) is Augustinian to its bones — sin as *bent* good, gifts curved inward and needing re-direction rather than destruction. And the discernment standards of slide 28 confirm that the "hearing God" material stays under communal and scriptural tests rather than floating free.

## 5. My single biggest concern

My concern is a single equals sign. Slide 7 says formation is "also called sanctification," and slide 4 — the deck's own chosen texts — says sanctification is what *God himself* does. I do not think the authors believe the process saves anyone; slide 43's 1 Corinthians 3, where the builder's work burns and the builder is saved through fire, shows they know the difference between our construction and God's rescue. But a deck is memorized in fragments, and the fragment a congregation will carry out of the room is the slogan, not the proof-text. If the Four Connects come to be spoken of as sanctification itself, then the member whose anchoring exercise produces nothing has not merely had a flat evening — she has, by the deck's own equation, failed at sanctification, and that is a burden no exercise should ever be allowed to lay on a soul the Spirit is quietly, invisibly keeping. Let the process bow, on one slide, in so many words, to the God of peace who himself sanctifies completely; then everything else in this warm and genuinely aretegenic deck can stand exactly as it is.
