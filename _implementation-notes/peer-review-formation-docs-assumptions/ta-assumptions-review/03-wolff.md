# Peer Review: "Theological Anthropology of the Heart, Spirit, Soul, and Mind"
**Reviewer: Hans Walter Wolff** — Old Testament, Heidelberg

I have read this paper with genuine sympathy. Its aim — that formation should know what it is forming — is one I share; my *Anthropology of the Old Testament* was written for preachers as much as philologists. I write to strengthen, and I will be candid where the Hebrew evidence presses back.

## 1. The load-bearing assumptions as I read them

1. **Terminological stability.** Because Scripture is "accurate and consistent," *heart*, *soul*, *spirit*, and *mind* have stable, distinguishable referents recoverable across both Testaments.
2. **Component ontology.** These terms name distinct *components* of the inner man — parts that can be individually identified, addressed, and trained.
3. **The nesting claim.** The components stand in a containment topology: spirit within heart; heart and mind within soul; soul expressed through body (Figure 1).
4. **The both/and warrant.** The Trinity-doctrine developmental method licenses synthesizing divergent construals (Nee, Willard, Mitchell) into one integrated model.
5. **The secular mapping.** After Powlison/Trentham testing, Kahneman's System 1 may be identified with the biblical heart and System 2 with the mind.
6. **Formation entailment.** Because the parts are distinct, formation requires distinct, part-specific tools — "the right tool for the right job."

## 2. Affirmations

Let me say warmly where the paper stands on solid ground. The engagement with Mitchell on *leb* is the best moment in the Old Testament section: her finding that the word is polyvalent — will, thought, emotion, vital center, with all aspects co-present and one foregrounded — is exactly right, and the author's willingness to answer "yes to all" rather than force a single sense shows sound instinct. Wherever the paper treats the heart as the seat of *deliberate decision* — the place where hearing becomes resolve — it aligns beautifully with the Hebrew evidence. *Leb* is above all the organ of the "reasonable man": Solomon asks for a *listening heart* to discern (1 Kgs 3:9); the fool "lacks heart" (Prov 6:32) — lacks not feeling but sense. The paper's insistence that formation is more than doctrinal instruction, its noticing of Nee's strange silence on the heart, and above all its own honesty — the model is offered as a *starting point* to be tested against "hundreds of passages" — these are the marks of a careful worker. That proposed test is precisely the right instinct, and I predict below what it will find.

## 3. Challenges

**Challenge 1: Parts versus aspects (assumptions 2 and 3).** The Old Testament does not give us an anatomy of components but what I have called *stereometric* thinking: each term names the *whole person* seen from a characteristic angle. Gen 2:7 is decisive — the man does not *receive* a *nephesh*; he *becomes* a living *nephesh*. *Nephesh* is the needy, desiring creature — throat, hunger, longing, vitality — "man as needy being," not an inner container. When the psalmist cries "Bless the LORD, O my *nephesh*, and all that is within me" (Ps 103:1), he is not addressing one part among others; he is summoning his whole self by its most intensive name. Likewise *basar* is the whole man in frailty and kinship; *ruach* the whole man as empowered. A model in which soul is a vessel *containing* heart and mind reads Greek container-logic into Hebrew aspect-logic. **Severity: Foundational** — for the ontology; see §4 for why it is *not* foundational for the training program.

**Challenge 2: The nesting claim has no Old Testament exegetical footing.** "Spirit within the heart, heart and mind within the soul" cannot be grounded in the Hebrew texts. Ezek 36:26 — "a new heart I will give you, and a new spirit I will put within you" — is *synonymous parallelism*: heart and spirit are two names for the one renewed center, not a container and its contents. Where the texts do use "within," the container is the *body*: Nabal's "heart died within him" (1 Sam 25:37) — a physiological statement. And the proposed hierarchy inverts the actual Hebrew weighting: *leb* is the interior organ *par excellence*, the most inward word Hebrew possesses; *nephesh* frequently sits at the boundary of neediness and desire. If anything, Hebrew would nest soul in heart, not heart in soul. The nesting is a *systematizing construct* — perhaps a useful one, but it must be presented as such, not as exegesis. **Severity: Foundational** for Figure 1 as ontology; **Minor** if reframed as a pedagogical map.

**Challenge 3: "Mind" as a fourth term does not exist in the Old Testament — and the Kahneman mapping runs backwards.** Hebrew has no separate word for mind; where English versions print "mind," the Hebrew is almost always *leb*. The heart *is* the mind: it plans (Prov 16:9), devises, weighs. Therefore the identification of heart with Kahneman's fast, "sloppy" System 1 and mind with deliberative System 2 inverts the Hebrew evidence — if the analogy is wanted at all, *leb* is the System-2 organ, the seat of slow counsel ("the heart of the wise ponders how to answer," Prov 15:28). The heart/mind contrast the paper needs is a *New Testament* resource (*kardia*/*nous*, e.g., 1 Cor 14:15; Rom 12:2), and even there *kardia* remains thoroughly Hebraic — the willing, knowing center, not the seat of quick feeling. The author should say plainly that this pillar rests on Greek, not Hebrew, foundations — and even then handle Paul's *nous* carefully. **Severity: Significant** — the heart/mind training distinction can survive, but its biblical warrant must be relocated and the Kahneman alignment either reversed or dropped.

**Challenge 4: *Ruach* is empowerment, not an innermost organ of reception.** In the Old Testament *ruach* is breath, wind, God-given vitality — "man as he is empowered." It *comes upon*, is *poured out*, is *given* (Ezek 36:26f.); it is not a resident faculty at the center of the heart awaiting training in "hearing." Nee's trichotomy, on which the model leans, reads a later construct back into texts that will not bear it. The New Testament (1 Thess 5:23; 1 Cor 2:11) gives the author more room here — honestly, more than the Old Testament alone ever would — but 1 Thess 5:23 is itself best read as intensifying totality, like Deut 6:5, not as an inventory. **Severity: Significant.**

**Challenge 5: Consistency of Scripture ≠ consistency of terminology (assumption 1).** Scripture's truthfulness does not entail that its anthropological vocabulary functions as a fixed technical system. The words are living Hebrew words, context-bent, polyvalent — Mitchell showed the author this for *leb*. The proposed test of "every passage" will, I predict, find not one stable four-part scheme but overlapping aspect-fields. That result should be welcomed, not treated as model failure. **Severity: Significant** for method; liberating if accepted.

## 4. Confirmations

Now the good news, which is substantial. **Stereometric aspect-language still fully licenses addressing and cultivating the person under each aspect.** Deut 6:5 is intensification — "with all your heart, all your *nephesh*, all your might" heaps up terms to claim the *whole* person — yet it thereby *names* summonable dimensions: the deliberating center, the needy-desiring vitality, the capacity-strength. Scripture itself commands aspect-specific care: "Keep your heart with all vigilance" (Prov 4:23); "I have calmed and quieted my *nephesh*" (Ps 131:2); "teach us to number our days that we may get a heart of wisdom" (Ps 90:12). The psalmist's self-address in Ps 103:1 is, formally, a *practice* — the whole self summoned under the aspect of desire toward praise. So the decisive distinction I can offer this project is this: **training-under-an-aspect survives even though part-ontology does not.** Different exercises for deliberation, for desire, for empowered responsiveness — this the Hebrew Bible not only permits but models. And note: the paper's wish that "the soul decides, the heart decides, the mind decides" all be simultaneously true is delivered *more* cheaply by stereometry than by nesting — if each term names the whole person, overlapping predication is expected, no containment diagram required. The hearing-and-obeying core of the larger project is the Shema's own logic: *hear* — and the command lands on heart, soul, might together. Nothing essential to formation is lost; what is lost is only a diagram's ontological pretension.

## 5. My single biggest concern

My deepest worry is Figure 1 — not the drawing, but its epistemic status. As it stands, a picture born of synthesis (Nee's trichotomy, Willard's circles, a both/and harmonization) is presented as what Scripture *says* about the inner man, and an entire multi-volume edifice is set upon it; yet the Old Testament, examined philologically, knows no nested vessels, no separate mind, and no resident spirit-organ — it knows one needy, frail, empowered, deliberating creature called by different names as God addresses him from different sides. My counsel, offered as a colleague and not a demolisher: let the author relabel the model honestly as a *pedagogical aspect-map* — "four angles from which the one person is addressed and trained" — and the whole formation program stands, I believe *stronger*, because it then rests on what the texts demonstrably do (command the keeping of the heart, the quieting of the soul, the willing spirit, Ps 51:12) rather than on a spatial ontology they never assert. A grandfather handing his family a map of the terrain need not claim the map is the territory; he need only show them where to walk, and this paper, so amended, does that well.
