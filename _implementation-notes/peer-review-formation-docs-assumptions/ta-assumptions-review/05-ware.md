# Peer Review: "Theological Anthropology of the Heart, Spirit, Soul, and Mind"
## Reviewer: Kallistos Ware, Metropolitan of Diokleia

I have read this paper with real gladness. It asks the question the Fathers never ceased asking — what is this creature, man, whom Thou art mindful of? — and it asks it not for the sake of speculation but for the sake of formation, which is precisely where the question belongs. Let me first say plainly what I take the paper to assume, then offer what an old bishop of the Eastern Church may give by way of encouragement and of caution.

## 1. The load-bearing assumptions as I read them

1. **Scripture is consistent in its inner-man vocabulary**, and therefore a single coherent model of heart, spirit, soul, and mind can be recovered from it.
2. **These four are really distinct** — not four names for one thing — and the distinctions matter practically, not merely lexically.
3. **A topology can be drawn**: the spirit within the heart; heart and mind both within the soul; the soul expressed through the body. Concentric containment is an adequate figure for their relations.
4. **Each faculty is trainable**, and trainable *differently* — heart tools differ from spirit tools differ from mind tools — so a complete formation curriculum must be plural.
5. **Development is staged and measurable**: taxonomies on the pattern of Bloom, and instruments on the pattern of the "Move" study, can chart progress in each faculty.
6. **Vetted secular research (Kahneman, Thompson) can inform the model** — specifically, that System 1 may be the heart's decision-process and System 2 the mind's.
7. **The both/and method of Trinitarian doctrine** — holding apparently competing scriptural claims together rather than choosing between them — is transferable to anthropology.

## 2. Affirmations

First and warmest: the paper's centre of gravity — *hearing God and obeying him* — is not an evangelical novelty but the oldest thing in Christian formation. In Greek, obedience is *hypakoē*, from *akouō*, "I listen": to obey is to listen intently, as a novice listens to his elder. The Shema itself — "Hear, O Israel" — which Mitchell's students exegeted, is the charter of both our traditions. A curriculum whose spine is hear-and-obey stands where Antony and Benedict stood.

Second: the refusal to reduce the person to intellect. The author's instinct — with Smith, against the "thinking animal" — is profoundly patristic. The Fathers never confused the *nous* with discursive reasoning; the deepest knowing is not syllogistic.

Third: the both/and instinct, learned from the Trinitarian debates, is exactly right in spirit. Mitchell's "yes to all" on *leb* is how the Fathers read Scripture: the terms overlap, interpenetrate, and resist tidy partition — and the paper honestly records this polyvalence before building.

Fourth: I am touched that Austin's account of theosis is received here without alarm. That an evangelical author looks East for the *telos* of formation — union with God, the restoration of the image — and does not flinch, is a genuine ecumenical grace.

Fifth: the candour. The author says plainly that the model is a starting point requiring testing against hundreds of passages. That apophatic modesty about one's own construction is itself a spiritual discipline.

## 3. Challenges

**Challenge 1: The missing *nous*.**
*Assumption at stake: #2 and #3 — the four-fold inventory and its topology.* The paper's taxonomy has no place for the category on which the entire Eastern ascetical tradition turns. The *nous* — the "eye of the heart," the intellective faculty that knows God by direct apprehension rather than by reasoning — is not the *dianoia* (discursive mind), and it is not simply the spirit either. When Paul says "I will pray with the spirit and I will pray with the mind also" (1 Cor 14:15, which Fokin rightly keeps on the table), the Fathers heard a distinction the paper's model must strain to accommodate. By assigning intuition to the spirit and analysis to the mind, the paper comes *near* the nous/dianoia distinction — but by another road, and without the seventeen centuries of practice attached to the older map. The danger is not error but impoverishment: a curriculum built without the *nous* has no vocabulary for the mode of knowing that contemplative prayer actually trains. *Severity for IJH: Foundational* — though I hasten to add, foundational in the sense of "the foundation should be widened," not "the house will fall."

**Challenge 2: Containment versus descent.**
*Assumption at stake: #3 — the concentric figure.* The Eastern tradition does not say the mind is *beside* the heart within the soul; it says the mind must *descend into* the heart. Theophan the Recluse: "You must descend from your head to your heart... stand in the heart before the Lord with the mind." The relation is not spatial containment but dynamic movement — a fall to be healed. Since the Fall, the *nous* is scattered abroad among thoughts and sensations; prayer of the heart is its return home. A static diagram of nested circles, however heuristically useful (and Willard's caveat that all such diagrams are incomplete deserves to be promoted from a footnote to a principle), cannot represent the one thing formation most needs to say: *the faculties are estranged from one another, and their reintegration is the work*. Nee's soul-as-mediator gestures toward this; the figure itself does not. *Severity: Significant.*

**Challenge 3: Kahneman's System 1 is not the biblical heart.**
*Assumption at stake: #6.* The paper passes Kahneman through Powlison and Trentham and finds no violation; I would gently press harder. System 1 is fast, sloppy, bias-ridden — a bundle of heuristics. The *kardia* of Scripture and the Fathers is the deep centre of the whole person, the place where God is encountered, "the innermost body within the body" as Macarius has it. To identify the heart with the error-prone system is to identify it with precisely what watchfulness (*nepsis*) exists to guard the heart *against*: the unexamined *logismoi*, the automatic provocations. Mark the Monk teaches that thoughts must be met at the gate of the heart and examined before admission — that is System 2 work done *on behalf of* the heart, at its threshold. Kahneman may illumine the psychology of impulse; he does not describe the organ of communion. If IJH's laws of hearing-and-obeying are routed through a heart defined as System 1, the hearing will be confused with the impulse. *Severity: Significant.*

**Challenge 4: Measuring the mystery.**
*Assumption at stake: #5.* The person is not a problem to be solved but a mystery to be entered. Taxonomies for the mind, yes — Bloom has his uses, and I note the author's honest classroom testimony. But a Bloom-style taxonomy *for the heart and spirit*, with "measurable progress," must reckon with the tradition's grave reserve here: the Fathers forbade the monk to measure his own advancement, for the sight of one's progress is the seed of pride, and the saints universally reported themselves the chief of sinners — the closer to the Light, the more the dust is visible. Any instrument will therefore read the deepening of humility as regression. The "Move" study's own most striking finding — the most mature stage contained the most dissatisfied — is not an anomaly to be engineered away; it *is* the dark night, and it is data confirming the tradition. Measure practices and fruits if you must; guard the interior from the auditor. *Severity: Significant* — for IJH Vol 4 especially.

**Challenge 5: Formation without liturgy and sacrament.**
*Assumption at stake: #4 — the curriculum's completeness.* Smith brings the paper to the very door of liturgy — "more moved than convinced," worship as the story enacted — and the paper receives him warmly. But in the East the heart is trained above all *sacramentally*: Eucharist, the rhythm of fasting and feast, prostrations, the breath joined to the Name in the Jesus Prayer. The body is not the outermost circle; it is a full participant in prayer. A curriculum of disciplines and taxonomies without the sacramental and bodily economy risks forming the heart by pedagogy alone. *Severity: Minor* for the paper's argument; *Significant* for a multi-volume formation project.

## 4. Confirmations

Let me be equally plain about where the East says *yes*, for the paper's deepest assumptions have ancient company.

**The heart is trainable.** This is not a modern presumption; it is the working premise of the entire Philokalia — five volumes whose very subtitle is "of the neptic Fathers." Seventeen centuries of the Jesus Prayer, of *nepsis*, of the guarding of the heart, constitute the longest-running curriculum of the heart in Christian history. The author's foundational wager — that the inner man responds to intentional practice under grace — is confirmed by an unbroken living tradition.

**Watchfulness is a curriculum.** The tradition did not merely exhort; it specified: attention at the gate of thoughts, the examination of *logismoi*, the return of the wandering *nous*, the joining of prayer to breath. These are teachable, and were taught, elder to disciple, in exactly the accompaniment structure the paper's FC companion envisages.

**Stages are known to the tradition.** Here I must be honest against my own reserve in Challenge 4: the East itself has a developmental map — *katharsis, phōtisis, theōsis*: purification, illumination, union — running from Origen and Evagrius through Dionysius to the present. The taxonomy assumption is therefore not foreign to us; we too say the journey has a shape and an order. Our caveat is only that the stages spiral rather than stack, and that God is not bound by them.

**Distinct faculties, distinct askesis.** The Fathers prescribed differently for the appetitive, the incensive, and the intelligent aspects of the soul — fasting for one, love for another, prayer and reading for the third. The paper's "right tool for the right part" is Evagrian medicine under an American name.

## 5. My single biggest concern

If I may speak as one grandfather to another — for I understand this work is a testimony handed down to children and grandchildren, and that is a holy purpose — my one abiding concern is this: that in carving the inner man so as to train him, the project may come to treat the heart as a faculty *among* the faculties, one circle among the circles, when in the deep tradition of the Church the heart is not a part of the person at all but the *whole* person gathered to a point — the place where body, soul, and spirit converge and where, in the stillness beneath thought, the Spirit cries "Abba." The paper's own both/and instinct already knows this; I ask only that it be trusted further. Let the taxonomies serve the descent of the mind into the heart, and never substitute for it; let the measurements bow, at the threshold of the inner chamber, to the mystery they cannot enter. What is trained is real; what is met there is greater than all training. If the grandchildren learn from these volumes both to practise and to be silent — to hear, to obey, and then to adore — the anthropology will have done its work, whatever revisions the diagram may yet require.
