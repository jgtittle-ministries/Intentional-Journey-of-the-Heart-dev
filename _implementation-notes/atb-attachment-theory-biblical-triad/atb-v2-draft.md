## Preface — How This Paper Came To Be

For years of small-group work I have puzzled over one question: why is actually trusting God, and trusting his Word, so hard for some people? The parable of the soils helped. It names the hard path, the rocky ground, the thorns and the good soil. But it never told me why I had a hard-path place in my own heart, or a rocky one.

Alongside that question sat another idea, and the two seemed to belong together. Core wounds from childhood shape how we trust people as adults, so it seemed natural that they would shape how we trust God. The research is mixed, and at times argumentative. This paper therefore holds a notion, not a firm conviction. It is something to explore, and the pages that follow explore it.

The starting question is the Sower's question — why does the same Word land in some hearts as hardened path, in others as rocky ground, in others as thorny soil, and in others as good soil that produces fruit thirtyfold, sixtyfold, and a hundredfold? The Affective Taxonomy gives one answer: the variance is developmental, mapping the four soils to internalization levels (V2.Exp1; HFT; MSF). The Foundational Laws give another: the variance is structural, mapping the soils to the operation of specific laws — Sowing-and-Reaping, Confession-and-Restoration, the Obedience Channel, the Hardening Law (V1).

This paper proposes a third complementary answer, one that does not replace the first two but enriches them: the variance is also relational. The capacity of a heart to receive, to keep, and to bring to fruit the Word God plants in it is shaped — though not determined — by the relational history through which the person learned (or failed to learn) that closeness with a trustworthy other is safe. Attachment theory, the body of research initiated by Bowlby and Ainsworth and extended by Hazan, Shaver, Mikulincer, Granqvist, and Kirkpatrick, names that relational history with precision. The Reformed/evangelical/charismatic theological tradition in which this paper is written names the gospel that re-grounds it.

## 1. What This Paper Adds to the Formation Documents

The seven existing Formation Documents form a coherent set:

- TA — Theological Anthropology supplies the doctrine of the person (Spirit, Heart, Soul, Mind, Body) on which the other six are built.

- HFT — Heart Formation Theology identifies the Affective Taxonomy as the developmental framework and trust in scripture as the tractable target.

- SST — Soul and Spirit Taxonomies extends the developmental framework into the soul and spirit dimensions parallel to HFT's treatment of heart.

- MSFIG — Model of Spiritual Formation for Individuals and Small Groups integrates the three inner taxonomies and extends them to individual, small-group, and church-wide application.

- MSF — Measuring Spiritual Formation at Scale scales the framework across five organizational levels (individual, group, congregation, denomination, universal church) and supplies the measurement-instrument analysis.

- FC — Formation Companion proposes the synthesis role of the formation facilitator with a three-level developmental progression.

- 4Cs — Four Connects names the four primary connection points (Self, Others, God, Mission/World) the formation work cultivates.

Each of these papers presupposes that the believer can receive what is being formed. What they have not formally named is the relational substrate that determines how receivable formation is in the first place. A person with a secure attachment history walks into the formation work with the affective scaffolding for trust, vulnerability, lament, and response already partially in place. A person whose history taught that closeness is uncertain, or unsafe to need, walks in with that scaffolding partially missing or actively distorted, and the formation work must address the substrate as well as the building.

This is not a niche concern. Among American college students, the share who described themselves as secure fell from about 49 percent in 1988 to about 42 percent in 2011, while the share describing a dismissing (avoidant) style rose from about 12 to about 19 percent (Konrath et al. 2014). Faith Hill, reviewing Amir Levine's *Secure*, reports that fearful-avoidant has risen too (Hill 2026). In a 2019 Pew survey, 49 percent of Americans said that trust in one another has fallen because people are not as reliable as they used to be (Pew Research Center 2019). These figures are self-descriptions, and they describe a population, never a person. But they suggest that the church is receiving more people for whom closeness has not proved reliable.

ATB names that substrate, describes the familiar attachment struggles and the distortions they bring, and proposes specific operational practices (drawn from John's Fellowship of the Heart work and the TPM Whole-Person packet) by which the Holy Spirit, through reliable relational community and scriptural meditation, progressively repatterns the substrate toward what the New Testament calls the triad of faith, hope, and love (1 Cor 13:13; 1 Thess 1:3; Col 1:4–5; Gal 5:6).

The paper has three structural moves:

1. Descriptive — naming the attachment patterns as pastoral vocabulary: familiar shapes of relational struggle that color how a person receives the Word, prays, relates in community, and engages the formation work. The patterns describe struggles. They do not sort people (Section 2 explains why).

2. Theological — locating the gospel as the ground of growth toward felt security (what attachment researchers, in a narrower technical sense, call "earned security") and naming the church as the relational ecology God ordinarily uses to do that work

3. Operational — proposing the FotH practices (TPM, attachment-style practice cards, whole-person integration matrix) as the concrete formation work that operationalizes the theological claim

The novel structural contribution to the IJH framework: attachment struggles as a loose parallel to the Sower soils, complementary to the Affective-Taxonomy mapping already developed in V2.Exp1: The Heart Soil — A Diagnostic Map. The paper proposes that where a heart shows hard-path or rocky soil in a given region of life, a familiar attachment struggle may often be at work in that same region, and that the work of clearing the soil may include the relational work of addressing the substrate. Section 8 marks where that parallel fits closely and where it fits poorly.

## 2. Attachment Theory: A Brief Orientation

Attachment theory was developed by John Bowlby (1907–1990), an English psychiatrist and psychoanalyst, in response to clinical observations of children separated from caregivers during and after World War II. Bowlby proposed that human infants possess an innate behavioral system whose function is to maintain proximity to a primary caregiver under conditions of threat or distress, and that the quality of that early caregiving experience produces durable "internal working models" of self, others, and the relationship between them. Mary Ainsworth's empirical work in the 1960s–1970s, using the Strange Situation, identified three patterns in infant–caregiver dyads: secure, anxious-ambivalent, and avoidant. A fourth, disorganized, was added later by Mary Main and Judith Solomon (1986; 1990) to describe infants whose behaviour fitted none of Ainsworth's three. The four, as they are now usually described in infancy:

- Secure attachment develops when the caregiver responds sensitively, predictably, and warmly to the child's signals. The child learns that distress is met with comfort and exploration is welcomed back to a reliable base. Internal working model: I matter; others can be trusted; the world is explorable.

- Anxious attachment (also called anxious-ambivalent or preoccupied) develops when the caregiver's response is inconsistent — sometimes warm, sometimes intrusive, sometimes absent — without a pattern the child can predict. The child learns to hyperactivate the attachment system, clinging or protesting to elicit the response that intermittent reinforcement makes precious and uncertain. Internal working model: I am only safe when proximity is verified; loss is always nearby.

- Avoidant attachment (also called dismissive) develops when the caregiver is consistently unavailable, dismissive of distress, or punishing of dependence. The child learns to deactivate the attachment system, suppressing bids for closeness and developing premature self-sufficiency. Internal working model: Dependence is dangerous; needs must be hidden; I am alone.

- Disorganized attachment is seen when the caregiver is both the source of fear and the only available source of safety, often (though not only) in contexts of abuse, severe neglect, or a caregiver's own unresolved trauma. The infant cannot organize a coherent strategy; approach and avoidance compete in the same moment. It is less a fourth strategy than the breakdown of strategy. Internal working model: Closeness is both desperately needed and actively dangerous.

Hazan and Shaver (1987) extended these patterns to adult romantic relationships. Mikulincer and Shaver's three-decade research program has demonstrated that attachment patterns shape emotion regulation, stress response, caregiving capacity, openness to exploration, and helpseeking across the lifespan.

How adult attachment is actually measured matters for everything that follows. Adult research runs in two traditions. The Adult Attachment Interview reads a person's *state of mind* about attachment from how coherently they speak of their history (Hesse 2016). Self-report research, the larger tradition, measures two *dimensions*: attachment anxiety (worry about being abandoned or not loved enough) and attachment avoidance (discomfort with closeness and dependence) (Brennan, Clark, and Shaver 1998). Adults vary continuously along both. When researchers test whether adults fall into types, they find dimensions instead (Fraley et al. 2015). The familiar names are regions of a plane, not boxes. The same person also differs from one relationship to another: secure with a spouse, anxious with a parent, guarded with a pastor.

There is no adult trait called "disorganized." Self-report research has a region high on both anxiety and avoidance, sometimes called fearful-avoidant (Bartholomew and Horowitz 1991), but that is not the same thing. The interview tradition's nearest analogue is a state of mind called *unresolved*: lapses in reasoning when a person speaks of a specific loss or trauma. It is a state about particular memories, not a personality type. The field's 2017 consensus statement on disorganization, led by Granqvist, warns against using the category to classify people outside trained, validated assessment (Granqvist et al. 2017). It also warns that the label invites over-attribution.

**How this paper uses the four names.** ATB keeps the four names as pastoral vocabulary: words for struggles that many people recognize in themselves, in some relationships and some seasons. They are never assignments. No facilitator in IJH practice types, labels or records a participant by pattern. The practices in Section 10 are offered to anyone who recognizes the struggle, not assigned to anyone a leader has sorted. In this paper "disorganized" names a moment rather than a person: the moment when closeness and fear arrive together and no strategy holds, most often where a loss or trauma remains unresolved. This matches the way IJH observes a group: it reads the room's trust, never a person's inner life.

Kirkpatrick (1992, 2005) pioneered the application of attachment theory to religious experience. His work and Granqvist's describe two pathways by which attachment history shapes a person's relationship with God:

- Correspondence: secure childhood attachment tends to go with a secure God-image (loving, accessible, responsive); insecure childhood attachment tends to go with a corresponding insecure God-image (capricious, distant, threatening). Granqvist refined this as *socialized correspondence*. People with secure histories tend to take up their parents' faith (or lack of it) gradually, through the ordinary transmission of a trusted relationship, and their faith tends to be steady (Granqvist and Hagekull 1999).

- Compensation: when human caregivers have been unreliable, some people find in God the secure base that human relationships failed to provide. Their faith more often begins suddenly, in a season of distress, and is more often marked by ups and downs (Granqvist and Kirkpatrick 2004).

The two pathways are two doors into the same church, and they call for two formation logics. Those who came in by socialization often need their inherited faith to become their own: to be tested, owned, and brought into felt relationship. Those who came in by compensation need a community steady enough not to repeat the original wound, and patient with the swings that compensating faith brings.

Crucially, attachment patterns are tendencies, not destinies. They are shaped by experience and can be revised by experience. In the technical literature, *earned security* names adults who describe a difficult childhood yet speak of it coherently in the Adult Attachment Interview (Roisman et al. 2002). The pastoral hope behind the term is a durable change toward security through repeated experience of reliable, attuned, boundaried relationship with someone who can be the secure base the original caregivers were not. What the religion research has established is compensation: some people with insecure histories find in God a real attachment figure, with real comfort (Granqvist 2020). That compensation can mature into durable earned security is a plausible extension. It has some longitudinal support (Bradshaw and Kent 2017, on prayer and attachment to God over time) but it remains under-tested. ATB holds it as a hope with partial evidence, not as a demonstrated finding. Elsewhere this paper speaks of "growing security" to keep that difference in view.

These patterns are descriptive, not prescriptive. They explain some variance in religious experience; they do not exhaust the human person. Temperament, culture, sin, spiritual warfare, providence, and the Spirit's sovereign work all shape behavior. Attachment theory is one true lens among several. ATB receives it as common-grace insight without overreading what it can deliver.

### Before the Theory Had a Name

The church did not learn about attachment in 1969. It has its own literature on the heart that cannot rest until it is held, sixteen centuries deep and older.

Augustine opens the *Confessions* with it: "you have made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you" (*Confessions* 1.1). That is a secure-base sentence written by a man whose own story ran through restlessness, grief and a mother's long prayer.

The Psalms are the grammar of it. They run to God as safe haven ("God is our refuge and strength, a very present help in trouble," Ps 46:1; "under his wings you will find refuge," Ps 91:4). They go out from him as secure base ("You have set my feet in a broad place," Ps 31:8). They protest separation without leaving the relationship ("How long, O Lord? Will you forget me forever?" Ps 13:1), which is the opposite of the avoidant shutting-down and of despair. They long for proximity ("As a deer pants for flowing streams, so pants my soul for you," Ps 42:1). They name compensation outright: "Though my father and mother forsake me, the Lord will take me in" (Ps 27:10). And they give the picture of settled security: "I have calmed and quieted my soul, like a weaned child with its mother" (Ps 131:2). Lament, on this reading, is the believer's attachment protest made into prayer (Brueggemann 1984).

Julian of Norwich, an anchoress walled into one room in the fourteenth century, wrote the tradition's clearest account of being securely held. She was shown a thing the size of a hazelnut and told that it lasts "and ever shall, for God loves it" (*Showings*, Long Text 5). She saw that God looks on our falling with pity and not with blame, and she called Christ our Mother: "as truly as God is our Father, so truly is God our Mother" (Long Text 59). Her summary is the one this paper hopes to earn: "in falling and rising we are ever preciously kept in one love" (Long Text 82; quotations modernized). Calvin (1559) and Edwards (1746), both in the bibliography, stand in the same line.

## 3. The Biblical Triad: Faith, Love, Hope

Three theological virtues are explicitly grouped throughout the New Testament: faith (πίστις, pistis), love (ἀγάπη, agapē), and hope (ἐλπίς, elpis). They are listed together in 1 Cor 13:13, 1 Thess 1:3, Col 1:4–5, and Gal 5:5–6. They are not interchangeable; each names a distinct grace and a distinct posture toward God and neighbor. But they are mutually constitutive: faith works through love (Gal 5:6); faith and love arise from hope (Col 1:4–5); hope does not disappoint because the love of God has been poured into our hearts by the Holy Spirit (Rom 5:5).

Faith (covenantal trust). Biblical faith is reliance on the character and promises of God as fulfilled in Jesus Christ. It rests not on human temperament but on God's faithfulness and Christ's finished work (Rom 5:1–5; Eph 1–2). It draws near because the Son has opened the way and the Spirit assures adoption — "Abba, Father" (Heb 4:14–16; Rom 8:15–17). In Reformed terms, faith is God's gift (Eph 2:8–9; Calvin, Institutes), the instrument by which we receive Christ. It has content (what God has promised), direction (Godward reliance), and practice (obedience, prayer, sacraments, fellowship). Faith is also embodied in the IJH framework as the central operating variable of the Vol 1 Sowing-and-Reaping law, the Drawing Near Reciprocity law (V1 FL-VII), and the Affective Taxonomy's developmental arc.

Love (covenantal fidelity in action). Biblical love (Hebrew ḥesed; Greek agapē) is grounded in God's own self-giving in Christ (1 Jn 4:7–10) and poured into believers' hearts by the Holy Spirit (Rom 5:5). It is patient, kind, truthful, and enduring (1 Cor 13:4–7). It is the telos of the triad: "the greatest of these is love" (1 Cor 13:13). In the IJH framework, love is the content of the great commandments (Matt 22:37–39), the fruit of the Spirit (Gal 5:22), and the operating substance of the Drawing Near Reciprocity, Reciprocal Forgiveness (V1 FL-V), and Generosity-Provision (V1 FL-IX) laws.

Hope (forward-leaning expectancy). Biblical hope is confident expectation grounded in God's promises — resurrection, new creation, the Spirit's down payment of the inheritance (1 Pet 1:3; Rom 8:18–25; Eph 1:13–14). It is realistic about suffering and anchored in God's oath (Heb 6:17–20). It is not optimism; it is theologically moored and Spirit-sustained: "May the God of hope fill you with all joy and peace as you trust in him, so that you may overflow with hope by the power of the Holy Spirit" (Rom 15:13). In the IJH framework, hope is the load-bearing virtue of the Endurance-Hope law (V1 FL-XXII), Brueggemann's disorientation-to-new-orientation movement engaged across Vols 1–3, and the eschatological horizon that gives the formation work its weight.

Scripture orders the triad's interplay in concrete sequences. Romans 5:1–5 supplies the canonical one: justified by faith → standing in grace → rejoicing in hope of glory → suffering produces perseverance → perseverance produces character → character produces hope → "and hope does not disappoint, because the love of God has been poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us." Faith grants access, God's love is experienced, hope is confirmed — each reinforcing the others.

## 4. Trust (Faith) and Attachment

Attachment theory's foundational metaphors — safe haven (the place we return to when distressed) and secure base (the foundation from which we explore) — map directly onto biblical images of God. Psalm 23: "The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not want." Psalm 18:2: "The Lord is my rock, my fortress, and my deliverer." Matthew 11:28: "Come to me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest." Hebrews 4:14–16: "Let us then approach God's throne of grace with confidence, so that we may receive mercy and find grace to help us in our time of need." The Father in Christ is both safe haven and secure base; the Holy Spirit's witness of adoption (Rom 8:15–17) addresses the felt dimension of trust.

Scripture does not speak of God's care only in a father's voice. "Can a woman forget her nursing child, or show no compassion for the child of her womb? Even these may forget, yet I will not forget you" (Isa 49:15). "As one whom his mother comforts, so I will comfort you" (Isa 66:13). "How often would I have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings" (Matt 23:37). The weaned child rests "with its mother" (Ps 131:2). Attachment science was born watching mothers and infants, and the Bible was there first. These images are similes for God's care. They do not rename the God whom the church confesses as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. But they matter pastorally: for the believer whose wound is the father, Father-language alone can bar the very door this paper means to open.

How attachment struggles can color the experience of God. These describe struggles, not kinds of people. Most believers will recognize more than one, in different relationships and seasons.

- Where trust comes easily (secure). God's nearness is generally emotionally accessible. Prayer is expectant; communal discernment can be received; correction can be heard as care; doctrinal truth and felt experience integrate without much friction.

- Where closeness feels uncertain (anxious). God may feel unpredictable, and the heart may swing between fervor and despair. Spiritual dryness may be read as abandonment; reassurance may be sought through religious activity; emotional intensity may be mistaken for the Spirit's confirmation; performance may become a way to secure divine approval. Not every dryness is attachment-driven: the tradition's discernment of consolation and desolation asks other questions too.

- Where closeness feels unsafe to need (avoidant). God may be approached mainly through the mind: doctrinal precision preferred to relational intimacy, God's transcendence stressed over His immanence, orthodoxy kept as a defense against vulnerable engagement, language of affection or felt presence uncomfortable. The same person may be among the most theologically rigorous and the most relationally remote members of a congregation.

- Where closeness and fear arrive together (the "disorganized" moment). Closeness is felt as both longing and threat, most often where a loss or trauma remains unresolved. A believer in such a season may swing between intense spiritual experiences and withdrawal, react strongly to spiritual authority figures (idealization, then collapse), and find the same practice (prayer, worship, communion) steadying one week and overwhelming the next.

The Reformed/evangelical distinction between saving faith and psychological trust capacity matters here. Saving faith is the gracious work of the Spirit through the gospel (Eph 2:8–9; Rom 10:17); it is not the product of secure childhood attachment. A believer with profound attachment wounds is no less saved than a believer with secure history. But sanctification — the progressive renewal of the whole person in conformity to Christ — does include the gradual repatterning of attachment-derived relational habits. The Spirit can renovate nature: scripts of vigilance, withdrawal, or chaos can become patterns of wise openness through Scripture, sacrament, community, prayer, and time.

## 5. Love and Attachment

Biblical love is the telos of the triad — the destination toward which faith and hope move. It is also the operational form that mature faith takes (Gal 5:6) and the relational substance the church is called to embody (Heb 10:24–25; 1 Cor 13). Attachment theory's caregiving system — the parallel motivational system that perceives others' needs and responds — supplies a creaturely description of the substrate on which biblical love is practiced.

Felt security enables outward love. Mikulincer and Shaver's research demonstrates that securely attached adults show more empathic caregiving, more forgiveness, more prosocial behavior, and more sustained altruism than insecurely attached adults. The mechanism is not surprising: the energy and attention required for sensitive responsiveness to others is partly the energy not consumed by managing one's own felt insecurity. A person whose attachment system is in chronic hyperactivation (anxious) or chronic deactivation (avoidant) has less of the relational bandwidth that love-in-action requires.

This converges with the biblical observation that "we love because he first loved us" (1 Jn 4:19) and "perfect love casts out fear" (1 Jn 4:18). The gospel's announcement of the Father's prior, unconditional, electing love is exactly the secure-base experience the insecure-attachment patterns most need. As that love is encountered repeatedly — preached, sacramentally enacted, experienced in reliable Christian community, made felt by the Spirit's witness — the believer's capacity for outward, costly love grows. Faith works through love; faith is love-in-action made plausible by encountered grace.

How attachment struggles can shape the practice of love:

- Where trust comes easily, love can be given and received without fusion or fear. This is where a person can speak the truth in love (Eph 4:15), hold their own boundaries while honoring others', and offer compassion without losing themselves.

- Where closeness feels uncertain, love may be equated with constant reassurance; every conflict may feel like the start of abandonment; proximity-seeking may pressure others without meaning to; emotional intensity may be over-spiritualized. The gospel re-grounds worth in God's unearned love, inviting practices (lament, slow prayer, paced response) that widen the window of tolerance for relational uncertainty.

- Where closeness feels unsafe to need, love may be equated with self-sufficient service, willing to give and resistant to receive, and dependence treated as failure. Scripture reframes love as mutual self-giving that receives as well as gives (1 Cor 12:21–26); the formation work involves practices of receiving (intercession, blessing, confession, the Lord's Supper as gift) that disarm the defense of competence.

- Where closeness and fear arrive together, love may be felt as both longing and threat, swinging between closeness and withdrawal within the same relationship. Trauma-informed care, consistent boundaries, predictable pastoral structures, and frequently the partnership of a licensed Christian counselor create the safety that gradually makes love sustainable.

## 6. Hope and Attachment

Biblical hope is the forward-leaning anchor of the triad — the virtue that holds the believer through suffering toward the not-yet of resurrection and new creation. Snyder's hope theory in secular psychology decomposes the construct into two components: pathways (perceived routes from the present to the desired future) and agency (the motivational energy to pursue those routes). Supportive relationships scaffold both; secure attachment correlates with greater capacity for both.

The Romans 5 sequence (suffering → perseverance → character → hope) requires both. Pathways: the believer must be able to perceive that there are real routes from the current trial to the eventual good — not in this life necessarily, but in the kingdom God has promised. Agency: the believer must have the motivational energy to walk those routes — to pray when feeling unheard, to obey when results are not visible, to stay in community when wounded. Both are easier when the believer has the secure-base experience of God's reliable presence and the secure-base experience of a reliable Christian community.

How attachment struggles can shape the experience of hope:

- Where trust comes easily, expectancy can be kept through setbacks, goals flexed without despair, and help asked for wisely.

- Where closeness feels uncertain, the pull is toward catastrophizing and compulsive reassurance-seeking; spiritual dryness may become apocalyptic threat. Pastoral care emphasizes God's steadfastness and slow practices that widen the window of tolerance.

- Where closeness feels unsafe to need, the pull is toward defensive pessimism and self-reliance; detachment may be spiritualized as "maturity" and petitionary prayer downplayed. Pastoral care invites concrete dependence: asking, receiving, and waiting with others.

- Where closeness and fear arrive together, hope and threat are intermingled; grand expectations may alternate with complete withdrawal. Predictable rhythms, trauma-informed counseling, and gentle leadership create the safety required for sustainable expectancy.

Biblical hope is cruciform — shaped by the cross. It does not promise relief in this life; it promises God's faithfulness through whatever this life contains. Performative-positivity cultures undermine hope by suppressing lament and over-realizing the eschatology of comfort; biblical hope tells the truth in the minor key and trusts God for the final cadence.

## 7. The Triadic Spiral: How Trust, Love, and Hope Reinforce Each Other Through Attachment Dynamics

The triad operates as a mutually reinforcing cycle —"a triadic spiral, a grace-driven, relationally formed cycle that moves from encounter to enactment." The cycle has four phases:

Phase 1 — Encountered Love produces Growing Trust. God's initiating love, preached and sacramentally enacted and made felt by the Spirit, meets the person where they actually are. In attachment terms, the Father in Christ becomes the safe haven; the church, when reliable and boundaried, echoes that haven. As the person repeatedly experiences attuned presence — pastoral care, small-group reliability, healing prayer practiced humbly (Jas 5:13–16) — felt security rises, and trust in God's promises becomes more emotionally accessible. Faith is still God's gift, but the Spirit ordinarily works through real relationships.

Phase 2 — Stabilized Trust frees Love-in-Action. Faith works through love (Gal 5:6). Confidence in God's character and future grace releases the person from self-protection and turns them toward costly service. In attachment terms, secure-base conditions free the believer to explore outward in mission and to activate the caregiving system with sensitivity and endurance. Doctrinal assurance and congregational reliability make love plausible and sustainable.

Phase 3 — Enacted Love confirms Hope. As love is practiced amid suffering, the Romans 5 sequence unfolds: suffering produces perseverance, perseverance produces character, and character produces hope that does not disappoint because the love of God has been experienced. Concrete testimonies, answered prayers, reconciled relationships, and the steady solace of the Lord's Supper function — psychologically — as agency and pathway cues for hope, and — biblically — as foretastes of the coming kingdom.

Phase 4 — Strengthened Hope generates Fresh Trust and Deeper Love. Eschatological hope — "the hope laid up for you in heaven" (Col 1:4–5) — enlarges the heart's capacity to risk loving again, and faith leans forward more boldly. The spiral tightens: experienced love → enacted faith → confirmed hope → enlarged love → and so on.

Where each attachment struggle tends to enter and resist the spiral:

- Where closeness feels uncertain, the spiral is often entered at Phase 1 (encountered love) and resisted at Phase 2: the experience of love is intense, but trust does not stabilize, because the working model expects the next experience to be different. The formation work is patience and repetition: many cycles of encountered love before the working model updates.

- Where closeness feels unsafe to need, Phase 1 itself is often resisted: encountered love is interpreted as pressure, or as something not really happening. The formation work is embodied rather than primarily verbal: the Lord's Supper, structured small-group vulnerability, practices of receiving (blessing, intercession), which gradually disarm the deactivating defense.

- Where closeness and fear arrive together, Phases 1 and 3 may be engaged vividly (intense felt encounters, dramatic hope) while Phase 2 is skipped (stable trust does not last). The formation work is consistent boundaries, predictable structures, trauma-informed care, and almost always collaboration with a competent Christian counselor.

The spiral does not depend on any one phase succeeding all at once. Grace is patient with the substrate, and the Spirit's ordinary mode is incremental.

**A door marked extraordinary.** Ordinary is not the only mode. God is not bound to the incremental, and he sometimes comes unbidden: a sudden peace, a wound healed in a night, a showing such as Julian received. No practice schedules such a gift and no protocol earns it. The church receives these gifts with gratitude and discernment (1 Thess 5:19–21; 1 John 4:1). It does not demand them, and it does not treat their absence as failure. The practices in this paper are the ordinary means. They are never the boundary of what God may do.

## 8. The IJH-Specific Contribution: The Sower Soils as Attachment Profiles

The Heart Soil diagnostic in V2.Exp1 already maps the four Sower soils to the five Affective Taxonomy stages: hard path → pre-Level 1; rocky soil → Level 1; thorny soil → Level 2/3; good soil → Levels 3, 4, 5 (30-, 60-, 100-fold). The mapping is internally consistent and explanatorily powerful.

ATB proposes a parallel reading (not a replacement, an enrichment) between the Sower soils and the familiar attachment struggles. Each cell says how closely the parallel fits.

| Sower soil | Affective-Taxonomy mapping (V2.Exp1) | Possible attachment parallel (a loose link, not a cause) |
|---|---|---|
| Hard path (the word snatched away before it takes root) | Pre-Level 1 — not yet attending | *Plausible fit.* The parable names its own agent: the evil one comes and snatches the word away (Matt 13:19; Mark 4:15; Luke 8:12). An avoidant closing-down may be part of what leaves the path packed hard, the relational system shut before the Word can land ("I don't need this"). The enemy exploits what that closing leaves exposed (V2.Exp2B). The substrate never replaces the enemy. |
| Rocky soil (quick joy, no root, falls away in trouble) | Level 1 — Responding extrinsically | *The tightest fit.* The parable's own mechanism (joy at once, no root, falling away when tribulation comes) matches the anxious shape closely. Enthusiasm that rises fast on outside reinforcement may not hold when tribulation takes that reinforcement away. |
| Thorny soil (choked by the cares of the age, the deceitfulness of riches, and desires for other things) | Level 2 — competing values not yet resolved | *The weakest fit.* The parable's thorns are rival loves and riches (Mark 4:19), not trauma; a secure, wealthy man can be thoroughly thorny. Where closeness and fear arrive together, the turmoil may add to the cares that choke the word, but the thorns belong first to mammon. |
| Good soil (30, 60, 100-fold) | Levels 3, 4, 5 — Valuing, Organization, Characterization | *A loose fit, by this paper's own evidence.* Felt security often supports deeper integration. But good soil is defined by hearing, holding fast, and bearing fruit with patience (Luke 8:15), not by attachment history. Some believers bear much fruit while still carrying attachment wounds (Section 13). |

The claim is not that these mappings are one-to-one or causal. It is that in some regions of some hearts a soil condition and an attachment struggle may appear together, and that the work of clearing the soil may then include relational work on the substrate. The table offers vocabulary for noticing. It is not a tool for classifying anyone.

**Owning the looseness.** Section 13 says that some believers struggle with attachment wounds while growing in godliness and service for decades. That is good soil without a secure pattern, in this paper's own hand. It is the strongest evidence that the link between the soils and attachment is loose, and it should restrain any reading of the table that equates fruitfulness with security, or insecurity with failure. There is a pastoral edge here too. The soils describe responses to the Word. An attachment wound is usually something done to a person, often in childhood. To map the one onto the other's language of failure risks moralizing trauma. That is why the table is held loosely in a paper and never read aloud over a person.

Three operational implications follow, each held with the same caution:

1. The "thorny" Level-2-to-Level-3 transition, already identified in V2.Exp1 as the most critical and challenging threshold in the Affective Taxonomy, is where attachment work may often matter most. Where closeness feels uncertain, a person may stay at Level-1 Responding for a long time, because intrinsic motivation draws on felt security. Where closeness feels unsafe to need, a person may stay in Level-2 cognitive engagement for a long time, because vulnerable internalization asks for relational openness. Formation that addresses these transitions may need to address the attachment substrate as well as the cognitive and behavioral surface.

2. The same heart contains multiple soil conditions in different regions (V2.Exp1's Proposed Diagnostic Law). The same heart may also be secure with one set of people or topics, anxious with another, guarded with a third. Adult attachment research confirms that attachment varies by relationship and is dimensional rather than typed (Fraley et al. 2015). Formation may invite a person to notice, for themselves, where closeness comes easily and where it does not. That noticing belongs to the person. No one else records it.

3. The Foundational Laws are experienced differently through different attachment struggles. Drawing-Near Reciprocity (V1 FL-VII) feels different where closeness is uncertain (drawing near is hyperactivated and easily mistaken for the law in operation) than where closeness is unsafe to need (drawing near is suppressed, and theological precision can substitute for relational engagement). Confession and Restoration (V1 FL-II) feels different where closeness and fear arrive together (ruptured trust is a high risk) than where trust comes easily. The laws are universal; the relational pathway by which a particular heart engages them varies.

Open empirical question: does the parallel hold at all? Any test would need validated, dimensional self-report instruments, separate research consent, and the safeguarding architecture that Section 13 names as a gate. It cannot rest on a facilitator's impressions. The 8-week trackers in the Warrior Monk × TPM packet are organized by pattern cohort. Before any research use they would need to become practice logs that a person chooses and keeps for themselves.

## 10. Operational Practices: The FotH Whole-Person Integration

ATB's operational core is the work that the Fellowship of the Heart community have developed over many years, expressed most recently in the Warrior Monk × Attachment-Informed Christian / TPM packet. Four operational components map directly onto the theological claims of Sections 4–7:

Component 1 — TPM (Transformation Prayer Ministry) as attachment-aware mind renewal. TPM's MAP — EMOTION → MEMORY → BELIEF → TRUTH → TRANSFORMATION, with Helper Boxes for SOLUTION (resistance) and ANGER — is operationally a Spirit-led practice for surfacing the belief layer of attachment-derived working models and presenting it to the Lord for re-patterning. The Solution Box, in particular, surfaces protective defenses as the person meets them: holding on harder (hyperactivating), shutting down (deactivating), or both at once. TPM done well is attachment work done in the Spirit's presence. Because its memory-descent can surface more than a lay group can hold, it is also the practice most in need of the safeguarding gate named in Section 13.

Component 2 — Practice cards. The packet supplies three short, repeatable practices. Each is offered to anyone who recognizes the struggle it meets. None is assigned by a leader to a person the leader has typed.

- Reassurance Cooldown (for the urge to ask again) — notice the urge to seek reassurance; rate distress (SUDS 0–10); wait ten minutes using breath and grounding; journal the fear; ask once clearly; receive the answer without re-asking; thank the person; re-ground for one minute. Scripture anchor: Ps 56:3. *If you ask again anyway:* you have not failed the practice, and you have not lost God. Notice it without contempt, and begin again next time. The answer God gives a re-asking child is the same answer.

- Receiving Drill (for the difficulty of receiving) — choose a safe person; ask them to pray for you for 60–90 seconds; your only words are "Yes" and "Amen"; notice sensations and breathe; afterward say "Thank you" without immediate reciprocation. Scripture anchor: Matt 7:7. *If you deflect, joke, or pray back:* that is the old defense doing its job. Say "Amen" when you can. The gift was given whether or not it was received well.

- Exit/Return Plan (for being overwhelmed) — signal to step out when overwhelmed; exit to a pre-agreed spot; use grounding for 2–5 minutes; return when SUDS ≤ 5 if you wish; tell the leader "Ready to return." Scripture anchor: Heb 4:16. *Return is welcome, never required.* You may leave the work for a night, or for good, without spiritual penalty. If distress does not come down, the next step is care from someone trained to give it, not a return to the room.

As ATB offers them, every card carries the same watermark: *this practice cannot make you more kept than you already are.*

These are small, repeatable, embodied, scripturally anchored. They train the substrate one micro-moment at a time, and they are the ordinary means, never the limit of what God may do (Section 7, "A door marked extraordinary"). The 8-week trackers in the packet belong to the person who keeps them. Any research use of them needs separate consent and the data governance reserved for the Council (Section 13).

Component 3 — Whole-Person Integration matrix. The packet's Heart–Spirit–Soul–Mind–Body integration is grounded in Willard's anthropology (and through Willard in TA, the IJH Theological Anthropology Formation Document). The matrix helps a person notice where in themselves an attachment-shaped struggle is showing up and which formation practice (lament, intention prayer, rule-of-life check, PROAPT scripture engagement, embodied grounding) addresses it. ATB's contribution to the matrix is the explicit attachment-substrate dimension: the same Whole-Person practice does different work in different attachment substrates.

Component 4 — Group covenant, safety practices, and trauma-informed structure. The packet's group-covenant requirements (confidentiality within legal limits, consent for touch/prayer/questions, presence and time limits, prompt rupture repair, no unsolicited advice, cultural humility) and the SUDS scale + grounding menu + safety plan together constitute the reliable, boundaried, attuned community that attachment-theory research identifies as the condition under which security can grow. The packet's care to position itself as complementary to (not a replacement for) licensed mental-health care reflects the wise pastor/counselor collaboration the GPT-5 paper recommends. These are real safeguards. They also shape the habitat for everyone in the room, which is where most change happens (Section 12). They are practices, though, not yet an architecture; Section 13 names what is still missing.

## 11. Reading Attachment Styles as Integration, Not Replacement

The Affective Taxonomy is read across the IJH project as integration-not-replacement (canonical surfaces: V2.Exp7 pastoral; Vol 4 §2 construct-level). The same framing applies to attachment styles:

Mature attachment is not "always secure." It is a secure baseline that still holds two older sensitivities open.

- The mature believer retains anxious sensitivity — alertness to the loss of relational closeness, capacity to feel the cost of separation, the proper grief of distance from God. Without it, the heart hardens to the loss of those it should love.

- The mature believer retains avoidant discernment — the capacity to recognize when intimacy is being pressed before trust has been established, when boundary-maintenance is wisdom rather than defense. Without it, the heart fuses with what should be held at appropriate distance.

- The mature believer's secure baseline is the regulating ground from which those two sensitivities operate without overwhelming the whole. Secure-as-baseline is what makes them useful rather than disabling.

Research supports the claim that these tendencies are not only damage. Social defense theory argues that anxious and avoidant tendencies carry real advantages for a group: the anxious are quicker to sense threat, and the avoidant quicker to act on their own (Ein-Dor et al. 2010). Levine makes the same case for general readers (Hill 2026). In the studies he draws on, anxious people noticed changes in faces sooner and reported smoke in a room sooner, and avoidant people were the first to leave, with others following. He also takes secure people off the pedestal: they may simply not notice when someone has been hurt. Hill calls Levine's evolutionary explanation speculative, and ATB takes the observation, not the story. The observation fits this section's claim. A believer who is only secure is insulated, not mature.

Disorganization is not a third sensitivity to keep. In the research it names a breakdown: the moment when the one who should be safety is also the source of fear, and no strategy holds (Section 2). The church should not dress that collapse as a virtue. What the tradition does honor is something different: the capacity to hold longing and fear in the same prayer without being torn apart, as the lament psalms do ("Why are you cast down, O my soul? … Hope in God," Ps 42:5). A person who has known the breakdown may come to carry that capacity, but it is a fruit of healing and of being held, not a residue of the collapse. Where it appears, it belongs to the secure baseline, not to disorganization. (The case for the strengths of anxious and avoidant tendencies offers no help here: Levine says little about the fearful-avoidant region beyond that less is known.)

A believer whose secure baseline has eclipsed both sensitivities is not mature — they are insulated. They are the relational analog of the Stage-5 Pharisee diagnosed in V2.Exp7: formally well-formed, operationally closed. The pastoral work of ATB therefore includes an integration question: is the secure baseline holding those sensitivities open, or has it replaced them? This question is for a person's own self-examination and for wise pastoral conversation. It is not a check that a facilitator runs on someone else.

This is the same structural move the Cameron-and-Quinn-derived held-tensions reading makes for the Foundational Laws (V1 Supplemental on Held Tensions and Shadow Pairs) and the integration-not-replacement reading makes for the Affective Taxonomy. ATB extends the discipline to the attachment dimension: health is a secure baseline that keeps anxious sensitivity and avoidant discernment alive; pathology is the collapse to one, including the apparently good collapse to "always secure."

## 12. Pastoral / Companion Implications

Trauma-informed shepherding. Assume a non-trivial portion of any congregation or small group carries histories of attachment-relevant relational trauma. Train leaders in basic attachment-aware postures: consistency, attunement, non-defensive listening, clear boundaries, explicit consent, prompt rupture repair, normalized lament. Secure trust grows slowly; the formation work is patient.

Wise referral and collaboration. Pastors and companions are not psychotherapists. Build active collaborative relationships with licensed Christian counselors who understand attachment and trauma. With consent, coordinate care so therapy (emotion regulation, narrative repair, trauma processing) and discipleship (Scripture, prayer, community, sacrament) cohere. Therapy is a creaturely means God often uses; treating it as spiritual failure is itself a pastoral malformation.

Small-group life as the secure-base laboratory. Structure groups with clear covenants (confidentiality, presence, grace-truth balance, repair norms). Train facilitators to name and normalize attachment dynamics non-shamingly ("It makes sense you hesitate to ask for prayer; that's a familiar pattern for many of us"). Name the dynamic in the room, never a label for a person. Encourage steady rhythms (weekly meetings, predictable check-ins, named expectations) that embody God's reliability over time.

Shape the habitat, not the person. Change comes more through surroundings than through willpower. Some people are like orchids, who wilt in poor conditions and flourish in good ones; others are like dandelions, who grow almost anywhere (the research calls this differential susceptibility; Belsky and Pluess 2009). Levine's word for the people among whom anxious hearts do best is "CARRP": consistent, available, responsive, reliable, predictable (Hill 2026). That is a fair description of a well-led group and a faithful leader. It also confirms the direction of this revision: shape the habitat for everyone, rather than sort individuals and treat them differently. One concrete habit follows. Teach about dry seasons before the first one comes. A heart prone to read God's silence as rejection does better if it has already heard, in a steady room, that the psalmists knew that silence too and did not take it as the end (Ps 13; Ps 42).

Leave more than one way in. For some people the circle itself is the hard part. Hill tells of a woman in Alcoholics Anonymous who found the group dynamic difficult but kept the one-on-one bonds she formed there, and those bonds sustained her sobriety (Hill 2026). A formation group should offer other ways in: the freedom to pass, partner exercises, and one-on-one contact with a companion (FC). Belonging may begin in a pair before it reaches the circle.

Charismatic practice with attachment awareness. Healing prayer, prophecy, and the gifts more broadly can serve as embodied experiences of attuned divine and human presence. They can also retraumatize when done badly — pressuring anxious people to perform, demanding vulnerability from those who guard themselves, overwhelming those for whom closeness and fear arrive together through unpredictable intensity. The FotH practice tradition's attention to consent, predictable structure, safety language, and trauma-informed referral is exactly the kind of charismatic accountability ATB's claims require.

Leadership reliability as pastoral love. Be the consistent, available, responsive, reliable, predictable presence described above. Keep promises. Communicate early. Repair ruptures publicly when necessary. Personnel reviews and pastoral care plans should include attachment-aware training. The manner of ministry makes the doctrine plausible — or implausible — to attachment-strained members of the congregation.

Preaching adoption. Make the Fatherhood of God and union with Christ recurring themes, and let Scripture's mother-images stand beside them (Isa 49:15; 66:13; Matt 23:37; Ps 131:2) for those whose wound is the father. Preach perseverance and assurance to anchor anxious hearts; preach dependence and prayer to soften guarded ones; preach the unchanging character of God to give a stable reference point to those for whom closeness and fear arrive together. Preach the Psalms, which teach the whole congregation to protest, long, and rest without leaving the relationship.

## 13. Cautions and Limits

Scope of attachment theory. Attachment theory illumines significant dimensions of human relating; it does not exhaust the human person. Temperament, culture, sin, demonic oppression, spiritual warfare, and God's mysterious providence all shape behavior. ATB resists reducing every spiritual struggle to attachment dynamics; some struggles are sin requiring repentance, some are warfare requiring resistance, some are providence requiring trust.

The Creator-creature distinction. Attachment theory's metaphors (safe haven, secure base) resonate with biblical images of God; they are not equivalent to the biblical doctrine of God. God is not a bigger parent, father or mother; He is the covenant Lord revealed in Christ. The attachment metaphors are useful for naming creaturely correlates of biblical truth; they are not the truth itself.

Cultural variability. Attachment-research instruments were developed in particular cultural contexts (largely Western, largely emphasizing individual autonomy and emotional expressiveness). Non-Western cultures often prioritize interdependence and emotional restraint in ways that recalibrate the meaning of "secure" or "avoidant." Application of attachment categories to spiritual formation must respect cultural variability while maintaining biblical authority on what love, faith, and hope finally are.

Saving faith vs. psychological trust capacity. Saving faith is the gracious work of the Spirit through the gospel (Eph 2:8–9); it is not produced by secure attachment, and it is not prevented by insecure attachment. A profoundly insecurely-attached believer is no less saved than a securely-attached one. ATB names attachment as part of the substrate on which sanctification operates, not as part of the ground on which justification rests.

Avoid psychologizing revelation and proof-texting psychology. Do not force one-to-one mappings (e.g., "secure attachment causes strong faith"). Receive empirical insights as common-grace descriptions of creaturely means the Spirit often uses; let special revelation continue to norm doctrine and practice.

Right-sizing is not the gospel's rule. Levine's *Secure* recommends "wall tennis": match in intensity what another person gives, and right-size the bonds that give less (Hill 2026). As a description of how insecure hearts protect themselves, it has some truth. As a rule for Christian love, it runs against grace. God loves first (Rom 5:8; 1 John 4:19), and loving only those who love back earns no credit (Luke 6:32–35). The church is a family one does not choose. Some limiting of closeness has warrant: Jesus kept circles of three, twelve and seventy-two, and Proverbs warns against close friendship with a hot-tempered man (Prov 22:24). Boundaries are wise. But the call to love the hard-to-love stands. Hill herself warns that the approach can license self-absorption, and that some relationships are rewarding precisely because they are difficult.

Continuationism / cessationism charity. Continuationists should heed cessationist cautions about authority, order, and abuse. Cessationists can affirm the Spirit's ongoing experiential ministry in assurance and love even while differing on the contemporary operation of miraculous gifts. Charity protects the vulnerable.

Resist prosperity and performative healing narratives. Some attachment patterns will not fully heal in this life. Some believers will struggle with attachment wounds while growing in godliness and service for decades. That is also evidence: good soil without a secure pattern, which is why Section 8 holds the soils parallel loosely. God's power is perfected in weakness (2 Cor 12:9). ATB does not promise complete repatterning; it names the direction of the Spirit's patient work.

Patterns are vocabulary, not verdicts. Adult attachment is dimensional and varies by relationship, and there is no adult trait called "disorganized" (Section 2). ATB therefore uses the four names only as pastoral words for struggles a person may recognize in themselves. No one is typed, labeled, or recorded by pattern, in a group or in a file. Practices are offered, not assigned. A wound is not a failure, and the soils' language of failure is never applied to a person's history.

A gate before any group program: safeguarding architecture. ATB brings trauma histories into the room by design, so its safety must be built, not assumed. Real safety practices already exist (Section 10, Component 4): covenant, consent, the SUDS scale, grounding, rupture repair, and referral language. What does not yet exist is the architecture around them: screening before people enter; a competence floor and supervision for facilitators; named referral triggers and a named person who decides; a protocol for when prayer ministry surfaces more than a lay group can hold; data governance and separate consent for anything recorded; and a complaint path that works when the leader is the rupture. Designing that architecture is reserved for the IJH Council's Character-Prerequisites pass, where it joins the standing safeguarding item from the earlier reviews (LANGBERG-2). This paper names the gate and does not design it. Until the Council has passed it, ATB's practices should not be run as a structured group program, and nothing from them should be gathered for research. The ordinary pastoral care a church already gives is not affected.

## 14. Conclusion

Attachment theory describes the creaturely substrate through which human persons learn to expect closeness or distance, comfort or threat, presence or abandonment. Scripture reveals the God who is the perfect attachment figure — Father in Christ, indwelt by the Spirit, faithful through all generations, the safe haven and secure base from whom and to whom every right relationship derives. The biblical triad of faith, love, and hope names the graces by which the Spirit progressively repatterns the substrate, drawing the believer through encountered love into growing trust, through stabilized trust into love-in-action, through enacted love into confirmed hope, and through strengthened hope into ever-enlarging love.

The local church, when it is ordered by the Word and enlivened by the Spirit and structured with the consistency, attunement, and reliability under which attachment-theory research finds security can grow, becomes the relational ecology God ordinarily uses to do this work. The Fellowship of the Heart practice tradition supplies the operational practices — TPM as attachment-aware mind renewal, the practice cards, the Whole-Person integration matrix, the safety-conscious small-group structure — through which the gospel meets the substrate where it actually lives, once the safeguarding gate in Section 13 is passed. The integration-not-replacement reading applied to attachment keeps the wisdom of anxious sensitivity and avoidant discernment while establishing the secure baseline as the regulating ground. The attachment names remain words for struggles, never labels for people.

Hope, finally, does not rest on achieved secure attachment. It rests on the God who promises "I will never leave you nor forsake you" (Heb 13:5), and who will not forget us even if a nursing mother could forget her child (Isa 49:15). That promise transcends the substrate; it transforms the substrate; in the resurrection it will heal the substrate completely. Until then, the Spirit's patient work continues, sometimes by the ordinary means and sometimes by a door no one but God can open, and the church bears witness — sometimes haltingly, always graciously — to the love that casts out fear. Julian, who knew more of this than the theory does, was shown that in falling and rising we are ever preciously kept in one love.

4. Integration with the Vol 1 Foundational Laws. Systematic work on how anxious and avoidant struggles characteristically distort a given Foundational Law's operation, and what formation work re-aligns it. Drawing-Near Reciprocity (V1 FL-VII) is the most natural starting point.

5. Dryness and desolation. Reconcile the "dryness read as abandonment" thread (Sections 4 and 6) with the desolation-dynamics differential in the staged research, which distinguishes what this paper currently lumps together.

6. Cross-tradition application. The MSF tradition-plateaus analysis (Reformed at L2, Evangelical at L2-3, Charismatic at L3, Mystical at L3-4) suggests that each tradition may also have characteristic attachment-pattern affinities — tradition-attachment-style affinity is an open research question with practical implications for cross-tradition formation work.

## Bibliography

This bibliography draws together the works cited across ATB and the three source papers, organized by topic. Entries are in Turabian author-date short form with a brief annotation describing what each work contributes to ATB. Reformed-evangelical theological works are listed at the end; the empirical attachment-research works and the spiritual-formation works are listed in their respective sections. IJH's existing Formation Documents and the Cameron-and-Quinn entries already in Vol 5 Part I are cross-referenced rather than re-described here.

### Attachment Theory: Foundational Works

**Ainsworth, M. D. S., Blehar, M. C., Waters, E., & Wall, S.** (1978). *Patterns of attachment: A psychological study of the strange situation*. Lawrence Erlbaum. The empirical foundation of contemporary attachment research — the Baltimore Longitudinal Study and the Strange Situation paradigm that produced the secure / anxious / avoidant infant-classification system, later supplemented by the disorganized category (Main and Solomon 1990). Cited throughout ATB Section 2.

**Bartholomew, K., & Horowitz, L. M.** (1991). Attachment styles among young adults: A test of a four-category model. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 61*(2), 226–244. The source of the adult "fearful-avoidant" region (high anxiety, high avoidance); cited in Section 2 to distinguish it from infant disorganization.

**Belsky, J., & Pluess, M.** (2009). Beyond diathesis stress: Differential susceptibility to environmental influences. *Psychological Bulletin, 135*(6), 885–908. The research behind the "orchids and dandelions" picture: some people are more affected than others by their surroundings, for worse and for better. Cited in Section 12.

**Bowlby, J.** (1969/1982). *Attachment and loss, Vol. 1: Attachment* (2nd ed.). Basic Books. The originating theoretical statement: human infants possess an innate behavioral system for maintaining proximity to caregivers under threat, and the quality of that early experience produces durable "internal working models" that shape relational expectation across the lifespan. The substrate concept that runs throughout ATB.

**Bowlby, J.** (1988). *A secure base: Parent-child attachment and healthy human development*. Basic Books. Bowlby's late summary of the clinical implications of attachment theory, including the "secure base" concept that ATB's biblical-images mapping (Ps 23; Heb 4:14–16) leans on.

**Brennan, K. A., Clark, C. L., & Shaver, P. R.** (1998). Self-report measurement of adult attachment: An integrative overview. In J. A. Simpson & W. S. Rholes (Eds.), *Attachment theory and close relationships* (pp. 46–76). Guilford Press. The integration of adult self-report measures into two dimensions, attachment anxiety and attachment avoidance; the basis of the dimensional reading in ATB v2 Section 2.

**Ein-Dor, T., Mikulincer, M., Doron, G., & Shaver, P. R.** (2010). The attachment paradox: How can so many of us (the insecure ones) have no adaptive advantages? *Perspectives on Psychological Science, 5*(2), 123–141. Social defense theory: anxious and avoidant tendencies carry real advantages for a group (vigilance to threat; quick independent action). Cited in Section 11.

**Fraley, R. C., Hudson, N. W., Heffernan, M. E., & Segal, N.** (2015). Are adult attachment styles categorical or dimensional? A taxometric analysis of general and relationship-specific attachment orientations. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 109*(2), 354–368. Evidence that adult attachment is dimensional rather than typed, and that it varies by relationship; supports ATB v2's retirement of categorical assignment (Sections 2, 8, 13).

**Granqvist, P., Sroufe, L. A., Dozier, M., Hesse, E., Steele, M., van IJzendoorn, M., et al.** (2017). Disorganized attachment in infancy: A review of the phenomenon and its implications for clinicians and policy-makers. *Attachment & Human Development, 19*(6), 534–558. The field's consensus statement on disorganization, warning against using the category to classify people outside trained, validated assessment; the reason ATB v2 uses "disorganized" for a moment, not a person.

**Hesse, E.** (2016). The Adult Attachment Interview: Protocol, method of analysis, and selected empirical studies: 1985–2015. In J. Cassidy & P. R. Shaver (Eds.), *Handbook of attachment* (3rd ed., pp. 553–597). Guilford Press. The interview tradition of adult attachment, including the "unresolved" state of mind and the technical meaning of earned security.

**Hazan, C., & Shaver, P. R.** (1987). Romantic love conceptualized as an attachment process. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 52*(3), 511–524. The first extension of attachment theory beyond infant–caregiver dyads into adult romantic relationships — the work that made adult-attachment research possible and that supports ATB's claim that attachment dynamics shape the believer's adult experience of God and Christian community.

**Konrath, S. H., Chopik, W. J., Hsing, C. K., & O'Brien, E.** (2014). Changes in adult attachment styles in American college students over time: A meta-analysis. *Personality and Social Psychology Review, 18*(4), 326–348. A cross-temporal meta-analysis of 94 samples (1988–2011): the share of students describing themselves as secure fell from about 49 to about 42 percent, and the dismissing share rose from about 12 to about 19 percent. Cited in Section 1.

**Main, M., & Solomon, J.** (1986). Discovery of an insecure-disorganized/disoriented attachment pattern. In T. B. Brazelton & M. W. Yogman (Eds.), *Affective development in infancy* (pp. 95–124). Ablex. The first report of the disorganized pattern in infancy.

**Main, M., & Solomon, J.** (1990). Procedures for identifying infants as disorganized/disoriented during the Ainsworth Strange Situation. In M. T. Greenberg, D. Cicchetti, & E. M. Cummings (Eds.), *Attachment in the preschool years: Theory, research, and intervention* (pp. 121–160). University of Chicago Press. The coding system that added the disorganized category to Ainsworth's three, following Main and Solomon's 1986 report of its discovery. Corrects v1's attribution of four patterns to Ainsworth.

**Mikulincer, M., & Shaver, P. R.** (2016). *Attachment in adulthood: Structure, dynamics, and change* (2nd ed.). Guilford Press. The standard contemporary reference for adult-attachment research, summarizing three decades of work on how attachment patterns shape emotion regulation, caregiving capacity, stress response, helpseeking, and openness to exploration. ATB Sections 4–7 lean on this synthesis throughout.

**Mikulincer, M., & Shaver, P. R.** (2020). Enhancing the broaden and build cycle of attachment security in adulthood. *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 17*(6), 2054. The mechanism by which felt security widens the believer's capacity for outward love and exploration — the empirical correlate of ATB's Triadic Spiral (Section 7).

### Attachment and Religion / Spirituality

**Beck, R., & McDonald, A.** (2004). Attachment to God: The Attachment to God Inventory, tests of working model correspondence, and an exploration of faith group differences. *Journal of Psychology and Theology, 32*(2), 92–103. The most widely used empirical instrument for measuring attachment to God; supplies the operational tools the future ATB measurement extension (Section 15 future work) would adapt and integrate with the IJH SIP framework.

**Bradshaw, M., & Kent, B. V.** (2017). Prayer, attachment to God, and changes in psychological well-being in later life. *Journals of Gerontology, Series B, 73*(2), 212–222. Longitudinal evidence that prayer practices shape attachment-to-God over time — empirical support for ATB's claim that the formation work patiently repatterns the substrate.

**Davis, E. B., Granqvist, P., & Sharp, C.** (2018). Theistic relational spirituality: Development, dynamics, health, and transformation. *Psychology of Religion and Spirituality, 10*(3), 192–204. The leading contemporary synthesis of attachment research applied to Christian spirituality; ATB's theoretical frame is consonant with this synthesis while extending it into the IJH-specific Sower-mapping and FotH-practices applications.

**Granqvist, P.** (2020). *Attachment in religion and spirituality: A wider view*. Guilford Press. The most comprehensive single-volume treatment of attachment theory's application to religious experience. It documents compensation (God as a substitute attachment figure for people with insecure histories) and socialized correspondence. That compensation matures into durable earned security is a plausible extension the book does not claim to have demonstrated; ATB v2 Section 2 says so (v1 overstated it).

**Granqvist, P., & Hagekull, B.** (1999). Religiousness and perceived childhood attachment: Profiling socialized correspondence and emotional compensation. *Journal for the Scientific Study of Religion, 38*(2), 254–273. The refinement of correspondence into *socialized* correspondence; the source of ATB v2's "two doors into the same church."

**Granqvist, P., & Kirkpatrick, L. A.** (2004). Religious conversion and perceived childhood attachment: A meta-analysis. *International Journal for the Psychology of Religion, 14*(4), 223–250. Evidence linking insecure histories to sudden conversion; the empirical base of the compensation pathway in ATB Section 2.

**Granqvist, P., Mikulincer, M., & Shaver, P. R.** (2010). Religion as attachment: Normative processes and individual differences. *Personality and Social Psychology Review, 14*(1), 49–59. The clearest single statement of the correspondence-vs-compensation pathways by which attachment history shapes God-images, summarized in ATB Section 2.

**Kirkpatrick, L. A.** (1992). An attachment-theory approach to the psychology of religion. *International Journal for the Psychology of Religion, 2*(1), 3–28. The foundational paper introducing attachment theory as a framework for the psychology of religion; proposes the correspondence/compensation distinction that ATB Section 2 uses.

**Kirkpatrick, L. A.** (2005). *Attachment, evolution, and the psychology of religion*. Guilford Press. The book-length development of Kirkpatrick's attachment-and-religion program.

**Kirkpatrick, L. A., & Shaver, P. R.** (1990). Attachment theory and religion: Childhood attachments, religious beliefs, and conversion. *Journal for the Scientific Study of Religion, 29*(3), 315–334. The first empirical study connecting childhood attachment history to adult religious experience.

**Hill, F.** (2023). Attachment style isn't destiny. *The Atlantic*. A general-audience account of the same correction ATB v2 makes: attachment styles are a spectrum, vary by relationship, and can change. Listed for readers, not as research evidence.

**Hill, F.** (2026, May 1). The psychiatrist's case for downsizing a friendship. *The Atlantic*. A review of Amir Levine's *Secure: The Revolutionary Guide to Creating a Secure Life* (2026; Levine co-wrote *Attached*, 2010). It is a popular-level source, cited lightly in ATB v2 Sections 1, 11, 12 and 13; Hill herself calls Levine's evolutionary explanation speculative.

**Pew Research Center.** (2019, July 22). *Trust and distrust in America*. The survey in which 49 percent of Americans said trust in one another has fallen because people are not as reliable as they used to be; cited in Section 1.

**Pirutinsky, S., Rosmarin, D. H., & Kirkpatrick, L. A.** (2019). Is attachment to God a unique predictor of mental health? Test in a Jewish sample. *International Journal for the Psychology of Religion, 29*(3), 167–179. Cross-tradition evidence that attachment to God independently predicts mental health beyond human-attachment effects — supports ATB's theological claim that the believer's relationship with God is itself attachment-formative.

**Roisman, G. I., Padrón, E., Sroufe, L. A., & Egeland, B.** (2002). Earned-secure attachment status in retrospect and prospect. *Child Development, 73*(4), 1204–1219. The technical meaning of "earned security" in the interview tradition and its limits; cited in Section 2 to separate the technical term from the pastoral hope.

**Zahl, B. P., & Gibson, N. J. S.** (2012). God representations, attachment to God, and satisfaction with life: A comparison of doctrinal and experiential representations of God in Christian young adults. *Journal of Psychology and Theology, 40*(2), 113–124. Empirical demonstration that *experienced* God-images, more than *doctrinal* God-images, correlate with attachment-to-God measures — supports ATB's distinction between cognitive belief and felt trust.

### Hope Theory

**Snyder, C. R.** (2002). Hope theory: Rainbows in the mind. *Psychological Inquiry, 13*(4), 249–275. The pathways/agency decomposition of hope that ATB Section 6 uses to bridge psychological and biblical hope. Snyder's research-grade definition is creaturely; ATB's biblical-hope framing locates the agency and pathways under grace.

### Religion / Spirituality and Coping

**Gall, T. L., Charbonneau, C., Clarke, N. H., Grant, K., Joseph, A., & Shouldice, L.** (2005). ### Spiritual Formation

**Augustine.** (1991). *Confessions* (H. Chadwick, Trans.). Oxford University Press. (Original work written c. 397–400.) "Our heart is restless until it rests in you" (1.1): the church's oldest secure-base sentence, restored in ATB v2 Section 2.

**Edwards, J.** (1959). *Religious affections* (J. E. Smith, Ed.). Yale University Press. (Original work published 1746.) The foundational Reformed treatment of true religion as consisting in *holy affections* — the seat of which Edwards locates in the will/heart rather than the intellect. Foundational to ATB's claim that attachment-derived affective patterns are part of the substrate on which sanctification operates.

**Julian of Norwich.** (1978). *Showings* (E. Colledge & J. Walsh, Trans.). Paulist Press. (Original work written late fourteenth century.) The tradition's fullest account of being securely held: the hazelnut that lasts "for God loves it," God's pity rather than blame, Christ as Mother, and "in falling and rising we are ever preciously kept in one love." Restored in ATB v2 Sections 2, 7 and 14; ATB's quotations are modernized.

**Smith, J. K. A.** (2009). *Desiring the kingdom: Worship, worldview, and cultural formation*. Baker Academic.

**Smith, J. K. A.** (2013). *Imagining the kingdom: How worship works*. Baker Academic. Smith's two books together argue that humans are formed primarily by liturgical practices that shape *desire* (the affective domain) prior to cognition. Convergent with ATB's claim that the formation work meets the substrate where it actually lives — in embodied practice, not primarily in cognition.

**Wilhoit, J.** (2022). *Spiritual formation as if the church mattered* (2nd ed.). Baker Academic. The leading evangelical text on church-based spiritual formation; consonant with ATB's emphasis on the local church as the ecology in which security can grow.

**Willard, D.** (2002). *Renovation of the heart: Putting on the character of Christ*. NavPress. The Spirit/Heart/Mind/Will/Body anthropology that the IJH Theological Anthropology Formation Document (TA) builds on and that ATB's Whole-Person Integration section (Section 10) references.

**Willard, D.** (1998). Spiritual disciplines, spiritual formation, and the restoration of the soul. *Journal of Psychology and Theology, 26*(1), 101–109. Willard's clearest statement of how the spiritual disciplines repattern the soul over time — the discipline-side complement to ATB's relational-formation account.
