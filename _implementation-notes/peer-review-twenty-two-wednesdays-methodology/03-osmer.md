# Stage B Methodology Review

**Reviewer:** Richard R. Osmer, Thomas W. Synnott Professor of Christian Education emeritus, Princeton Theological Seminary
**Manuscript:** "Twenty-Two Wednesdays as a Fellowship of the Heart" (JSFSC protocol paper, draft v2.3)
**Frame of assessment:** the four tasks of practical theological interpretation — descriptive-empirical (*what is going on?*), interpretive (*why is this going on?*), normative (*what ought to be going on?*), pragmatic (*how might we respond?*)

A word before I begin, because it matters for how the rest should be read. I like this paper. It is the work of a practitioner who has actually done the thing, who reports his own failures, and who has thought harder about the ethics of measuring people than most of the empirical work that crosses my desk. My criticisms are not the criticisms of someone who thinks the project is misconceived. They are the criticisms of a teacher who has spent thirty years watching gifted practitioners perform three of the four tasks brilliantly and skip the fourth without noticing they skipped it. In this case the skipped one is the interpretive task, and the consequences show up in the normative task, which is where I will press hardest.

---

## 1. What is methodologically sound here

**The pragmatic task is performed at a level I rarely see in a practitioner paper.** Osmer's servant-leadership dimension asks whether the leader's own authority is being spent rather than accumulated, and this design spends it liturgically. The Leader Feedback Round, in which "the evening's leader, beginning with me, tells the whole room what went well and what they would do differently" [20], is not a humility gesture bolted onto a curriculum; it is a weekly, public, structural transfer of the right to evaluate. The rotation architecture, in which "every practice the cohort learns is run twice, and the second running is led by a teenager" [19], does the same with the right to lead. And the fifth design principle — "at the top, the curriculum recedes: the settled life ... cannot be produced by a syllabus, and a curriculum that tried would do harm" [12] — is a designer building his own obsolescence into his design. Keep all of this. It is the paper's spine.

**The reflexivity is genuine, not performed.** Three times the author reports changing his mind under field pressure, and reports what changed it: the separate-circles design abandoned after prototyping [16]; two self-read designs "redesigned twice in the field, and the failures are instructive, so I will report them" [40]; the permission conversation "adopted ... late, after watching my own instruments through a parent's eyes" [21]. In Schön's terms this is reflection-on-action with the record left in. Practitioner papers almost never publish the discarded versions. This one does, and the field is better for it.

**The voice rule at [9] is a methodological commitment, not a stylistic one.** "When the researcher is inside the room, the researcher is part of the instrument, and a paper that hid its author behind the passive voice would be concealing the very thing a reader needs." That sentence should be quoted in method seminars. The declared rule — first person for testimony, third for warrants, and the difference visible — is exactly the discipline action research requires and rarely enforces.

**The level-matched, longitudinally aging instrument is a real contribution.** The observation that "watched only in full form, a healthy young cohort reads as failing all autumn, which is false, and discouraging, and (worse) teaches a team to distrust its own instrument" [36] is the best single paragraph in the paper. It is practitioner wisdom that improves the instrument's *validity*, not merely its morale: an instrument that reads floor for four months is not measuring the room, it is measuring its own miscalibration. The rung structure at [37] — answering / offering / costly telling; settling / absorbing / bearing; prompted noticing / naming / seeking — is a genuinely usable developmental coding frame, and the decision to score event-contingent signs as "no occasion" rather than as failure ("an event-contingent sign with no event is missing data, not failure" [37]) is correct and is more than many published coding schemes manage.

**The breaks-as-instrument design is the cleverest measurement move in the paper.** "The year has three scheduled structure-stoppages, and the same three counts are taken at each" [56]. This is a within-design longitudinal contrast on a behavioral trace, repeated three times, requiring nothing from participants and nothing of the observers but counting. It is the one place in the architecture where a claim about internalization could be embarrassed by data that no one on the team rated.

**The ethical architecture is not decoration; it does methodological work.** "No target is ever announced, because the moment a mirror becomes a goal it stops being a mirror" [45], with Strathern correctly invoked, is a real defense against the corruption that destroys most church metrics. The rule at [46] — "Permission is required for pages, never for care" — with its physical test ("if it is paper a participant touches, it is asked for; if it is a shepherd's eye held under a signed covenant, it is care") is the single most useful sentence I have read on the ethics of pastoral observation in a decade. It draws a line where a line can actually be held.

**The instrument's self-critique at [41] is sophisticated.** "All three house words can be heard along a single dimension of felt warmth, so vocabulary by itself cannot separate a room that calculates from a room that predicts from a room that identifies." That is a common-method-variance objection, raised by the author against his own instrument, with a remedy (read stage first from scheduled behavior, vocabulary corroborating). Reviewers do not normally have to supply that argument; here it is already on the page.

**On the refusals — my direct answer to the question put to me.** The design refuses journals, home-practice cards, and verbatim transcripts to outsiders [34], [59]. My judgment is that these refusals cost the descriptive-empirical task *very little*. Collected journals in a formation setting are among the most reactive data in our field; inspected practice cards convert a practice into a compliance ritual; and the author's own argument — "a room that does not feel surveilled behaves like itself" [34] — is a validity argument, not merely a pastoral one, and I accept it. What starves the first task here is not any refusal. It is two omissions that the covenant does not require and would not forbid, and I take them up as Findings 1 and 2.

## 2. Findings

### Descriptive-empirical task: *what is going on?*

---

**Finding 1. The situation is never described. — HIGH**

**Text.** The entire description of the context is: "a twenty-two-week, family-integrated pilot now underway at a small Christian school" [8]; "the school where this curriculum now runs releases its classroom at four o'clock" [5]; "three to six families, six to fifteen participants in all, with a Companion team of two to four" [8].

**Problem.** This is a paper about a room, and I finished it not knowing what room it is. I do not know the school's tradition or the families' denominational locations; whether these families are churched, lightly churched, or church-saturated; their socioeconomic range; whether the school is a day school, a classical academy, or a co-op; what the families' prior experience of small groups has been; why *this* school said yes; what the surrounding congregational ecology looks like; or what the teenagers' lives are like on the other six days. The descriptive-empirical task is not satisfied by describing an *intervention* in detail. It is satisfied by attending to the situation the intervention enters. This paper describes its design with beautiful granularity — I know the minute the door opens and how long the debrief takes — and describes its context not at all. That is the classic inversion in practitioner research, and it has a consequence beyond aesthetics: without a described situation, a reader cannot judge what the pilot's results would generalize *from*, and cannot tell whether the design's commitments are answers to *this* room's condition or preferences the designer brought with him. Blind review explains the withheld proper nouns; it does not explain the withheld sociology, all of which can be given without identifying anyone.

**Remedy.** Two to three paragraphs of thick description before [10], written from what the companions already know: tradition and ecclesial location of the school and the families; family structure and range; the families' prior formation history and small-group experience; what a Wednesday looks like for these teenagers before four o'clock; and the school's own reasons for hosting. None of this requires a new instrument, a participant-facing page, or anything the covenant restricts. It requires the author to write down what he already sees.

**Finding 2. There is no occasion in the year on which one person is listened to at length. — MEDIUM**

**Text.** The family-facing corpus is "five pages" of short prompts [34]. The observational tier is a team "read" against pre-specified rungs [37]. The room's own voice is gathered "aloud" in a public circle where "the leader answers first, nobody is anonymous" [41]. The one moment of genuinely open attending is a margin: "The team notices, without a form, who arrived early to build unasked; the debrief sheet has a margin for exactly this kind of small event" [27].

**Problem.** Priestly listening, in my vocabulary, has three forms: informal attending (the everyday noticing this design does superbly), semi-formal attending (a focused conversation in which one person is asked an open question and answers at length), and formal attending (designed research instruments). This design is rich in the first and third and has *none* of the second. Every category the team brings to the room precedes the room: the three signs, the three rungs, the three house words. So the honest description of these instruments is neither "priestly listening" nor "inspection" but a third thing — *reading*. Nothing is extracted and nothing is graded, so it is not inspection; but the room is read against a scale it never sees and never helped write, so it is not listening either. The paper is right that the third self-read question ("what would you tell somebody else about what this group is like?" [40]) is the most revealing, and the reason it is revealing is precisely that it is the only question in the architecture that does not supply its own answer categories. That should have taught the design something it did not learn.

**Remedy, inside the constraints.** Two moves, both free. First, the permission conversation already happens family by family, in person, in the gap after the first session [21]. Extend it by fifteen minutes and ask one open question with no categories attached — *tell me about how God has spoken in this family before now* — and write one line, which the family sees. That conversation is care, not paper, under the paper's own rule at [46]; it supplies the baseline description Finding 1 needs; and it costs no page. Second, on the debrief sheet, promote the margin to a column with a name: *what I saw that I had no box for*. The margin is the most theologically serious thing on that page. Make it structural.

### Interpretive task: *why is this going on?*

---

**Finding 3. The paper never asks why. Its theory legitimates rather than explains. — HIGH**

**Text.** "I did not design the container from that literature, but the convergence is striking, practice for practice: our confession-and-restoration night is Edmondson's 'mistakes' item lived liturgically; our costly telling is her 'hard issues' item with God in the sentence; our standing welcome for the honest skeptic is her 'different' item given a chair" [17].

**Problem.** Read that sentence for what it does rather than what it says. It takes a practice already designed and shows that a respected literature independently arrived somewhere similar. That is *convergent validation of a design decision*. It is not interpretation. Interpretation asks why the thing under study is the way it is, and it draws on the arts and sciences to answer. This paper borrows eleven bodies of literature and asks almost none of them a *why* question. Krathwohl supplies a ladder to climb; Lewicki and Bunker supply a vocabulary to code with; Edmondson supplies items to converge with; Strathern supplies a warning to obey; Schön and Bargh supply after-the-fact warrants for a round already invented. In every case the theory arrives after the practice and confirms it.

The exception, and it is a real one, is [42]: "open disagreement tends to arrive before mature trust, not after it ... the first honestly-processed disagreement is usually a milestone, not a malfunction," and the counterfeit of pseudocommunity. Here theory tells the team something it did not already believe, and would misread without. That paragraph shows the author knows how to make theory work. It is one paragraph out of sixty-odd.

What is entirely absent is any account of *why these families are in the condition this curriculum addresses*. Why is it plausible that parents and teens in this population have never told each other anything real about hearing God? What in the households, the school, the surrounding church culture, the media ecology, the sports and homework calendar, or the developmental situation of adolescence produces the deficit the curriculum answers? A practical theology reviewer will notice that a paper about the discipleship of teenagers cites no literature on adolescent development, and that a paper whose central wager is that teenagers can be honest beside their own parents cites no family systems theory. The bibliography is heavily weighted toward organizational behavior — Edmondson, Mayer, De Jong, Lewicki, Rousseau, Shapiro — which is the literature of *teams*, and the one literature that could tell you why a fifteen-year-old goes quiet when her mother speaks is not there at all.

**Remedy.** An interpretive section of two to three pages between [23] and [25], asking one question: *why is the situation described in Finding 1 the way it is?* It should draw on at least (a) adolescent development, (b) family systems — differentiation, triangulation, the emotional process of a household under observation, and (c) the sociology of formation in American evangelical households, where the outsourcing of catechesis to programs is a documented pattern and is precisely what this design's first commitment [16] resists. This costs the pilot nothing operationally. It changes what the paper *is*.

**Finding 4. The room is theorized as a work team and as a stranger dyad. It is neither. — HIGH**

**Text.** Two imports. First: psychological safety, "a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking," with the design reading "the room as a whole" [17], [59]. Second: "Vulnerability is reciprocal; the disclosure research has said for fifty years that people deepen by matched steps, each opening the next level for the other" [16], warranting the parents-go-first rule.

**Problem.** I am building here on a limit the paper already names at [59] — that safety is a team-level construct never disaggregated by generation — and going one step further, because the problem is not only in the *measurement*. It is in the *theory*. Edmondson's construct was developed among adult colleagues who are rough status peers, who have no kinship tie, who can exit the team, and who go home to different houses. A circle in which a fifteen-year-old sits beside the mother she will ride home with satisfies none of those boundary conditions. Whatever "the room is safe" means there, it is not what the construct means, and the paper's convergence argument at [17] therefore proves less than it appears to.

The same is true of social penetration. Altman and Taylor theorize *voluntary dyadic relationship development between relative strangers with free exit*. Parent and adolescent are involuntary, asymmetric in power, already deeply penetrated at some layers and heavily defended at exactly the layers this curriculum wants opened, and the "matched steps" mechanism has no obvious analogue where one party has changed the other's diapers. The parents-go-first rule may well be right — the eighteen months of prototyping is real evidence — but its warrant is being drawn from a theory that does not cover the case. What would cover it is family systems: "parents go first with something true" is, in that language, a differentiated self-position taken in the presence of the system, which is a well-theorized move with well-theorized failure modes, including the one this design should most fear — a parent whose "something real" functions as an emotional demand on the child seated beside them.

**Remedy.** Two paragraphs, no field changes. State the boundary conditions of the imported constructs and say what the design is claiming across them; and re-warrant the parents-first rule from a literature that covers kin. Then, on the debrief sheet, one line the companions can already answer: *after a parent's telling, did their own teenager speak next, and was it their own words or an echo?* That is a family-systems reading, it costs one line, it names nobody, and it is the closest thing to a generational read available inside the constraints.

### Normative task: *what ought to be going on?*

---

**Finding 5. The normative horizon stops at the room's edge, and every instrument is bounded by it — so cohesion and maturity produce identical readings. — HIGH**

**Text.** "By spring the cohort will show the one outcome I actually designed for, which no instrument elicits but every instrument serves: people who have tasted what a room like this is and have become unwilling to go back to rooms without it. That self-recognition is not a side effect. It is the level-three transition" [51]. And the closing: "A room that ends its year able to look into that mirror and say 'these are my people, and I am unwilling to go back' ... has given the theory its data" [63].

**Problem.** This is the paper's crescendo, stated twice, and named as the outcome the design exists for. I want the editor to see what it is: a *sociological* description of successful in-group formation standing in the place of a theological account of maturity. And every instrument in the architecture is bounded the same way. Costly telling is telling *in the room*. Response to load is what *the room* does in ten seconds. The sought stray is a stray *from the room* [36]. The trust vocabulary's top rung is "these are my people, I could say anything" [41]. The design's success condition and the consolidation of a warm, closed, leader-formed in-group generate the *same readings on every instrument the paper has*. There is no observation anywhere in this architecture that could tell those two apart.

That is a normative failure before it is a measurement failure. On what basis does the paper judge what ought to be going on? Its warrants are almost entirely one-line scriptural proofs for design choices — Deut 6:6-7 and Eph 6:4 for family integration [16], 2 Tim 2:2 for rotation [19], 1 Cor 2:16 and Acts 15:28 for the corporate turn [12] — and the good it finally names is the good the curriculum was built to produce. Its normativity is *curricular*. Meanwhile the tradition's own marks of maturity run the other direction: love of the neighbor who is not in the circle, and a willingness to be sent into rooms that are nothing like this one. Bonhoeffer sits in the bibliography [70] and does no work in the text, which is a pity, because his warning about the community that loves its own image of itself is precisely the warning this design needs. The paper knows about pseudocommunity [42], but pseudocommunity is a diagnosis of *hidden difference*, not of *closure*, and closure is the failure mode here.

Note also that the curriculum's third quarter "turns outward" and the fourth "sends, and commissions" [23], and that not one instrument reads outward. The design's own theological arc has no measurement at its far end.

**Remedy, inside every constraint.** Add one outward-facing observation to the debrief page, which already carries "the week's specific counts" [38]: *did anything cross the edge of this room this week?* — a member told something to someone outside the cohort; a member went after someone who is not in the circle; a family brought someone in; a practice landed on a person the room does not contain. This asks nothing of a participant, touches no paper a family holds, names no one, and adds a line rather than a page. Then add a fourth-quarter disconfirmation condition to the set at [53]-[58]: *if the year's outward-crossing count is flat or absent while the trust vocabulary migrates to owned, the design has produced cohesion and has not demonstrated maturity.* That single condition would convert the paper's most vulnerable claim into a testable one.

**Finding 6. There is no route by which a young person can say a hard thing outside the parental circle. — HIGH (and I am arguing against a stated constraint)**

**Text.** "The first thing the curriculum builds is what we call the container ... with confidentiality promised out loud and its legal limits named honestly" [17]. "So the cohort is one band of whole families: families share circles, a family is never divided, and in every practice the parents start" [16]. And the paper's own admission: "a room can register as safe while the subgroup least positioned to say otherwise, the teenagers, seated beside their own parents in a room where parents speak first, is never heard on its own" [59].

**Problem.** The paper names this as a measurement limit. I am naming it as a pastoral one, which is a different and heavier charge, and it is the one place where I think a stated constraint has been drawn wider than it needs to be. The design's central claim is that the container is safe to tell in. For the adults, the evidence for that is good. For a teenager whose hardest true thing is *about the parent sitting beside her*, the design offers no configuration in which it can be said — not in the circle, not on the five pages (which are family-facing and permission-bound), not in the journal (never collected), not in the self-read (public, unanonymous, leader-first). The one architectural feature that would catch it, the sought stray, is triggered by *going quiet*, and the team's response to a quiet teenager is to go after her — into what conversation, held where, under what confidentiality, the paper does not say.

The brief tells me that remedies separating families or staging parent-child conflict are unacceptable, and I accept that fully; I am not proposing either. I am saying the constraint forbids a *division* and does not forbid a *door*. A design that has thought this carefully about permission and this carefully about covenant, and that has already named its legal limits aloud, should not leave the route to a companion undescribed. Its absence is also, incidentally, why the safety readings cannot be trusted for the population that matters most.

**Remedy.** Name the door and name it aloud in the container speech, once, in the same breath as the confidentiality limits: any person in this room, of any age, may ask any companion for a conversation, at any time, and it will be given. Publish, in the handbook, where that conversation happens, who else may be present, what is held and what must be passed on, and what happens if what is said concerns someone in the room. This separates no family, stages no conflict, adds no instrument, and is a condition of the safety the paper claims. I would also record on the debrief sheet only that such a conversation occurred — never its content, never a name — because a year in which no young person ever walks through that door is itself a reading.

**Finding 7. The ladder shapes care, so an unvalidated scale acts normatively on people who never see it. — MEDIUM**

**Text.** "Levels are a Companion's diagnostic lens for shaping care" [12]. "The team reads the rung the room is on, marks each sign seen, not yet, or no occasion ... and promotes a rung after three consecutive weeks of seen" [37].

**Problem.** Stage A has secured the declaration that Krathwohl classifies objectives and was never validated as a stage model, and I will not re-litigate that. The *normative* consequence is separate and is not yet addressed. Non-disclosure protects a participant from *knowing* the judgment; it does not protect them from *receiving its effects*. If care is shaped by level, then a person read low receives differently shaped care than a person read high, on the basis of an unvalidated ordinal scale, applied by a single unblinded rater, promoted by a ratchet that cannot descend. The person cannot appeal a judgment they are not told about. This is exactly the moment where a developmental description quietly becomes the thing a person ought to be climbing, and where the ought is enforced by the distribution of pastoral attention rather than by anything said aloud.

**Remedy.** A clause in the measurement covenant, which already exists and already binds [44]: *a level may shape how care is spoken, never who receives it.* And one line on the weekly call's "look at the flock" section, which already tracks follow-ups by name and owner [38]: *did this week's attention go to the people who most need it, or to the people who are easiest to read as growing?* Both fit the page. Both are the kind of question a shepherding team should be asking anyway.

**Finding 8. "Nothing is ever asked" is asserted as the deepest commitment and never weighed against the obedience the curriculum's own domain presupposes. — MEDIUM**

**Text.** "The deepest commitment is the hardest to see because it is an absence. Nothing in the year is demanded: practices are offered, leadership is offered, even the faith commitment at the curriculum's center is stated plainly once and then left standing as an open door" [21]. Against: "Everything runs through a single domain, hearing and obeying God" [18].

**Problem.** I think the commitment is largely right and I would keep it. But it is presented as self-evidently good, and it is not self-evident; it is a normative position that requires an argument, and the argument is not made. The tradition this project stands in does not describe discipleship as an offer. It describes a summons — follow me, take up the cross, go and do likewise — and the paper's own chosen domain contains the word *obeying*. There is a genuine tension between a formation whose subject matter is obedience and a formation that never asks anything, and the paper does not appear to notice it. Nor does it distinguish the two things it may be defending: refusing to *announce a target* (which is Strathern's point at [45] and is unarguable) and refusing to *issue a call* (which is a much larger claim about the pedagogy of the Christian life). Those are different, and only the first is defended.

There is also a formation cost worth weighing. When "leadership is offered" and never asked, the young people who lead are the ones already able to volunteer, and the young person who most needs to be summoned into a role is the one least likely to receive one. Non-coercion has a distributive shadow.

**Remedy.** A paragraph of normative reasoning at [21] that (a) distinguishes announced target from spoken call, (b) states plainly which of the two the commitment refuses and why, and (c) engages one counter-case from the tradition rather than none. I would use the Wesleyan class meeting, which asked a great deal, weekly, of everyone, and which is the nearest historical exemplar of what this design is doing; arguing with it would make the commitment far stronger than asserting it.

**Finding 9. The one pneumatological sentence is asked to do causal work it cannot bear. — MEDIUM**

**Text.** "The whole year is intended to bring the group to the threshold of the level two to level three transition, where intrinsic motivation begins to take over from external structure, with each level change considered a gift of the Holy Spirit" [12].

**Problem.** That subordinate clause is the paper's entire doctrine of divine agency, and it is load-bearing: it is what makes this formation rather than group dynamics. But every behavior the instruments read — settling, offering, absorbing, seeking, vocabulary migration, break survival — is a behavior the group development literature the paper already cites would predict from ordinary process, on ordinary timelines, in a well-run secular cohort. The paper therefore has no way to say what, if anything, it is claiming about the Spirit, and no way to say whether that claim could be wrong. Prophetic discernment, in my framework, is not the assertion that God is at work; it is the disciplined attempt to say *what* God is doing here and to hold that against Scripture and tradition. A single clause does not do it. The theologically honest position may well be that the Spirit works through ordinary process and the distinction is not observable — but that is a position, and it should be argued, not left implied by a phrase.

**Remedy.** One paragraph in the design commitments, saying explicitly what the design does and does not claim about divine agency, and how the author understands the relation between the Spirit's work and the ordinary group process his own instruments read. Two or three sentences of that argument would strengthen the paper more than any additional count.

**Finding 10. The fellowship has no ecclesial location. — MEDIUM**

**Text.** "The fourth consolidates, sends, and commissions" [23]. "It has given its families a fellowship of the heart" [63]. The successor question is named as "how such companions are selected and formed" [61], with covering mentioned only in passing: "every companion stands under a named covering of their own" [61].

**Problem.** Sends where? Commissions to what, and on whose authority? This pilot runs in a school, gathers families who presumably belong to congregations, forms them into a fellowship with its own covenant, its own liturgy, its own confession-and-restoration night, and its own commissioning — and the church appears nowhere in the article. A practical theology reviewer will read [51]'s "unwilling to go back to rooms without it" alongside the ecclesial silence and ask the obvious question: what happens on Sunday, in the congregation, to a family formed to find its ordinary church wanting? That is not a hostile question. It is the question a design like this must answer, and the paper's own theology of parents as primary teachers [16] supplies the pattern for answering it: a supporting work equips and does not replace. The article says that of the family. It never says it of the church.

**Remedy.** A paragraph locating the fellowship ecclesially: what relation the pilot understands itself to have to the families' congregations, whether pastors were informed or consulted, where the commissioning at year's end sends people, and what the design would count as evidence that it had drawn families away from their churches rather than deeper into them. Optionally, and at no cost, the outward-crossing line proposed in Finding 5 can note when a crossing landed inside a congregation.

### Pragmatic task: *how might we respond?*

---

**Finding 11. The room's counsel is coded rather than adopted. — MEDIUM-HIGH**

**Text.** The self-read asks "what could we do differently that would make things even better" [40]. What happens to the answer: "The co-companion scribes the answers verbatim. On Thursday the team will map them, on its own side of the paper, to the three signs and to the trust vocabulary; tonight nobody grades anything, and whatever the room concludes about itself is received without correction" [28].

**Problem.** Here is the price of the paper's central thesis, and I do not think the author has seen it. If forming and observing are the same act, then every act of participant voice is simultaneously an act of participant assessment — and the room cannot tell which is happening, nor, on the evidence of [28], can the team. The room is asked what should change; the room answers; the answers are scribed and *mapped to a developmental scale*. "Received without correction" is not the same as acted upon. Nowhere in the architecture is there a mechanism by which the room's counsel changes the year. That is a servant-leadership deficit at the exact point the design is proudest: the leader recedes in *visibility* but never in *authorship*. And it undercuts the design's own top ambition, since the corporate turn at level four — "a body learning to hear together" [12] — is precisely a body whose hearing has consequences.

**Remedy, free.** The weekly call already has a "look ahead" section [38]. Require it, once a quarter, to name which of the room's "do differently" answers are adopted for the coming quarter and which are not; and require the next session's opening to say so aloud — *you said this, so we are changing that; you said this other thing, and here is why we are not.* No target is announced, nothing is graded, no page is added. What changes is that the room's speech acquires consequence, which is the difference between an instrument and a fellowship.

**Finding 12. The outside reader has no feedback loop, so the partnership the paper says it needs is adopted in name. — MEDIUM-HIGH**

**Text.** "Theological action research as Cameron and colleagues set it out is a partnership between insiders and outsiders, not a practitioner reporting alone, and this pilot as described is the practitioner reporting alone. Two corrections follow ... once a quarter a reader from outside the team spends two hours with the published materials and one session and answers a single question: judging only by what this program does, what does it appear to believe? ... and the answer is recorded whether or not the team recognizes itself in it" [59].

**Problem.** The diagnosis is exactly right and the question put to the reader is well chosen — it is, in Cameron's terms, a probe for the *operant* theology, the belief the practice enacts as against the belief the team professes. But the correction implements the diagnosis thinly in two ways. First, the reader's answer is *recorded* and nothing further; there is no mechanism by which it can change anything, and recording a reading is not a partnership, it is an audit trail. Second, the reader is given no counterpart: the team never states, in advance and in writing, what it *believes* it believes, so there is nothing for the outsider's operant reading to be compared against, and the whole exercise reduces to an impression.

I should say plainly that the "weekly surprise line" [59] has the same shape: it is a good instrument with no digestion. One companion writes a surprise on one page; nothing collects them, nothing reads them together, nothing is obliged to notice a pattern.

**Remedy.** Three moves, all inside the constraints and none requiring confidential material to travel. (a) Before the first reader visit, the team writes one page — its own statement of what this program believes. The outside reader never sees it beforehand. Comparing the two is the finding. (b) The reader's answer becomes a standing item at the next weekly call, and the team records what it changed or, explicitly, why it changed nothing. (c) Surprises are read aloud as the first item of "look back" each week, and read consecutively once a quarter, looking for the pattern a single week cannot show. The surprise line is the best interpretive instrument in the paper; at present it is the only one with no analysis attached.

---

**Finding 13. Volunteer-only teen leadership weakens the leadership-absence test as a disconfirmation condition. — MEDIUM**

**Text.** "The second running is led by a teenager when they are a willing volunteer" [19]; "at least five sessions across the year are second runnings led by teens" [23]; and the condition: "If teen-led nights consistently require adult rescue into the spring, the entrust-early design is wrong, or the pace is" [57].

**Problem.** The absence test is supposed to show that "the structure has begun to live in the members rather than in the leader" [19]. But the member who leads is always self-selected, and with six to fifteen participants the same one or two capable teenagers may lead all five nights. In that case the test shows that the structure lives in the most formed teenager in the cohort, which is a much weaker claim, and one that survives even in a group where nothing has generalized at all. As written, the disconfirmation condition at [57] is nearly unfalsifiable in the direction that matters: a design that selects its best case cannot fail its own absence test for the right reason.

**Remedy, one line on the sheet.** Record for each second running who led and whether it was their first time. Then sharpen [57]: *if the same one or two teenagers lead all of the year's second runnings, the leadership-absence test has not been run, and the design's claim about structure residing in the members is not evidenced.* Breadth of leaders, not survival of nights, is the reading that carries the claim. This asks nothing of anyone and does not require a single teenager to be asked to lead against the "nothing is ever asked" rule — it only requires the paper to stop inferring more than a volunteer sample allows.

**Finding 14. The individual-movement desire has no matching disconfirmation condition. — MEDIUM-HIGH**

**Text.** "Each individual's progress from wherever they start will be tracked to see the actual change. The desire is that each individual will move, on average, one level or more" [12]. The published disconfirmation set [54]-[58] concerns sign progression, source disagreement, break survival, teen-led containers, and vocabulary migration. Not one of them concerns individual level movement.

**Problem.** The paper's most quantitative statement of intent — an average of one level or more per person — is the single claim in the article for which no failure condition is published, in an article whose whole rhetorical warrant is that it publishes its failure conditions in advance ("A protocol that cannot fail is a testimony, not a design" [53]). That asymmetry will be noticed, and it is the kind of thing that lets a friendly result be claimed later without the risk having been taken first. I am not charging the paper for the absence of blind coding or a second coder; it names those at [59]. I am charging it for stating a target-shaped outcome and publishing no condition under which the target would count as unmet. There is a second problem inside the first: averaging an ordinal, unvalidated, ratchet-only rating across six to fifteen people is not an operation the scale supports, and "on average one level" implies a precision the instrument does not have.

**Remedy.** Either drop the averaging language and state the desire qualitatively, or — better — add a sixth disconfirmation condition in the same register as the others: *if, by spring, a majority of individuals show no rung movement on any of the three signs, the individual side of the internalization claim is not supported by this pilot.* No new instrument; the rung readings already exist.

## 3. What my discipline would consider missing

**The four voices of theology.** The paper claims theological action research and cites Cameron and colleagues [71], [59], fn 15, and then does not use the framework's central analytic apparatus: the distinction between *normative* theology (Scripture, creed, official teaching), *formal* theology (the academy), *espoused* theology (what a community says it believes), and *operant* theology (what its practice actually enacts). This design is unusually well-placed to use it — the outside reader's question, "judging only by what this program does, what does it appear to believe?" [59], *is* a probe for operant theology, one step from the framework and never taking the step. Running the quarterly look-back through all four voices would cost one meeting per quarter and would supply the interpretive discipline Finding 3 says is missing.

**Good practice as a normative source.** In my account, the normative task has three modes: theological interpretation, ethical reflection, and *good practice* — learning from exemplars of the practice, past and present. The paper does the second superbly, the first thinly, and the third not at all. There is no engagement with any historical exemplar of exactly what this design is: an accountable fellowship meeting weekly in small numbers around a single question of obedience, with lay leadership and a rule of life. The Wesleyan class and band meetings are the obvious case and would argue productively with almost every commitment in section [15]; the catechumenate, the Puritan conference, and the Moravian choirs are others. A twenty-two-week accountable fellowship that cites Edmondson and not one historical exemplar of accountable fellowship has located itself in the wrong library.

**A theological account of what counts as evidence.** The architecture reads behavior and infers interior change. Somewhere the paper must say why behavior is theologically legible at all, given that it has already conceded the opposite about the individual heart (Jer 17:9 at [6]). The move from "the heart is opaque even to its owner" to "a room is not opaque to its members" [41] is doing enormous theological work in a single sentence and is defended sociologically ("group behavior is public behavior") rather than theologically. There is a rich tradition on discerning fruit, and none of it is here.

**A described context.** See Finding 1. In my field this is not a nicety; it is the first task.

**An account of the practitioner's own transformation.** The reflexivity here is about *design* changes, and it is good (see section 1). What is missing is the practitioner's own theological change: not "I revised the instrument," but "I believed X about formation when I began and the room taught me Y." The surprise line [59] is the seed of it. Nothing yet harvests it.

## 4. What I would have done differently

Given the same convictions, the same families, the same covenant, and the same one-page limit:

1. **I would have written the descriptive and interpretive article first, and let the protocol follow it.** The companion article settled the theory; this one designs the response; nowhere in the sequence does anyone describe the situation or ask why it is as it is. Failing that, within this article, I would trade about a third of the instrument prose — sections [31] through [46] are more granular than a protocol paper needs, since the instruments are published in full anyway [24] — for the context and interpretation of Findings 1 and 3.

2. **I would have made the permission conversation a listening conversation.** It already exists, it is already in person, and it is already family by family [21]. Fifteen more minutes and one open question about the family's own history of hearing God gives the pilot a baseline description of the actual situation, in the families' own words, at no cost to the page count and no violation of any constraint.

3. **I would have built one outward-facing sign.** As Finding 5 sets out. Without it the architecture cannot distinguish a formed fellowship from a warm closed circle, which is a claim the paper needs and does not have.

4. **I would have put the design in argument with one historical exemplar.** The Wesleyan class meeting asked a great deal and this design asks nothing; letting those two argue on the page for one page would make "nothing is ever asked" a *position* rather than a preference, and would locate the pilot in a tradition rather than in a literature review.

5. **I would have given the outside reader teeth and given the team a counterpart page.** A partnership requires two statements to compare and a mechanism for the comparison to matter. See Finding 12.

6. **I would have changed the liturgy rather than the instrument to probe the generational ceiling.** Once, in the fourth quarter, on one practice, a teenager goes first. This separates no family, stages no conflict, and asks nothing of anyone who does not volunteer. It also does something the design should want: it tests whether the parents-first rule, whose warrant is depth-licensing early in a group's life, has done its work and can now be retired. If the room goes flat when a teenager opens, that is the most informative single evening of the year.

7. **I would have named the genre honestly and mapped it.** This is the *pragmatic* task of a multi-article program. One short paragraph in the introduction, mapping the companion article, the design papers, and the successor article onto the tasks of practical theological interpretation, would let a reader see the whole spiral even though this article traverses one arc of it — and would disarm, in advance, the objection I am about to make in section 5.

**Would this be recognised as competent practical theology?** As a piece of practitioner research and as a protocol, yes: it is more rigorous, more candid, and more ethically serious than most of what practitioners publish, and a formation and soul-care journal should be pleased to have it. As *practical theology* in the full sense, not yet. The four tasks are not four topics; they are four movements of one interpretation, and this article performs one of them at length (pragmatic), one of them thinly and mostly as ethics (normative), one of them prospectively (descriptive), and one of them scarcely at all (interpretive). A paper may legitimately foreground one movement — but it must know which one it is foregrounding, and this one presents itself as a whole method.

**The single addition that would most raise it** is the interpretive section of Finding 3: two to three pages asking why these families, in this school, at this cultural moment, are in the condition the curriculum answers — with adolescent development, family systems, and the sociology of formation in evangelical households actually consulted for explanations rather than for warrants. I nominate that one above the others because the rest follow from it. Once the author has asked why the room is as it is, he will see that team theory does not fit a kinship circle (Finding 4), that the good he is pursuing has an edge (Finding 5), and that there is a door his teenagers need (Finding 6). The interpretive task is the hinge on which the other three turn, and it is the one this excellent practitioner has skipped.

## 5. My single biggest concern

To the editor: my concern is not with any instrument but with the paper's normative horizon, which stops at the edge of the room. The outcome the author names as the one he designed for is that participants become "unwilling to go back to rooms without it" [51], repeated at the close as "these are my people, and I am unwilling to go back" [63] — and every sign in the architecture is bounded by the same circle: telling in the room, load borne by the room, strays sought within the room, and a trust vocabulary whose highest rung is the language of belonging to it. The consequence is that this design's declared success and its most dangerous failure — a warm, closed, well-led in-group that has mistaken its own cohesion for the Spirit's work, precisely the failure the paper's own citation of pseudocommunity should have alerted it to — produce identical readings on every instrument it possesses, and no disconfirmation condition can separate them. Because the tradition's own marks of maturity point outward, and because the curriculum's own fourth quarter claims to send and commission, this is not a peripheral gap; it is the place where a measurement architecture built with real theological care has quietly adopted a sociological criterion of the good. The remedy is small and fits every constraint the author has set — one outward-facing line on the debrief page and one matching disconfirmation condition — but until it is in place I would not let the paper's concluding claim stand as written.

*R. R. Osmer*
