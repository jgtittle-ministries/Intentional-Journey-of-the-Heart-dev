# Methodology Review — Bonnie J. Miller-McLemore

**Paper:** "Twenty-Two Wednesdays as a Fellowship of the Heart" (JSFSC protocol paper, draft v2.3)
**Stage:** B — design and inference
**Reviewer stance:** practical theology; the living human web; children and families as theological
subjects; the practitioner-scholar position.

A word before the findings, since tone matters and reviews are read by people. I like this paper. I
like it in the specific way I like very few protocol papers, which is that it has dirt on it. A
classroom that empties at four o'clock, chairs that have to become a circle by four-fifteen, a
basket by the door that a family can walk past at no cost — this is a paper that knows where it is
standing. That is rarer in our journals than it should be, and most of what follows is written
because the paper is good enough to be worth pressing hard.

---

## 1. What is methodologically sound here

**The constraint became the curriculum, and the author noticed.** [5]: "families who build the room
together settle into it differently than families who walk into one already prepared. I did not plan
that." This is the practical-theological move in miniature — the ordinary, unglamorous, logistical
detail turning out to be where the formation lives. Keep it, and keep saying "I did not plan that."
The paper's credibility rests more on sentences like that one than on any of its instruments.

**Level-matched longitudinal observation.** [36]–[37] is, I think, the paper's real contribution and
the author should know it: "Watched only in full form, a healthy young cohort reads as failing all
autumn, which is false, and discouraging, and (worse) teaches a team to distrust its own instrument."
Then the aged forms — answering → offering → costly telling; settling → absorbing → bearing;
prompted noticing → naming → seeking. Developmental calibration of an observational instrument so
that a young group is not read as a failing group is genuinely useful and generalizes far past this
pilot. If one thing survives review, it should be this.

**Permission before pages, and the willingness to pay for it.** [34]: "we would hold back even if it
cost us data." And [21]: "a no costs yes, the family remains a full participant, and the room never
learns who answered which way," with the physical instantiation at [27]: "a family that said no in
September walks past the basket at no cost, and nobody knows." Designing a *material* mechanism for
declining, rather than an announced principle of declining, is better ethics and better method than
most consent procedures I read.

**The leader receives correction in public, first, every week.** [20]: "the evening's leader,
beginning with me, tells the whole room what went well and what they would do differently." This is
an actual redistribution of a small amount of real power, repeated fifty-plus times a year, in a
setting where the founder holds nearly all of it. It is worth more than a paragraph of reflexive
throat-clearing.

**Levels are never disclosed to the parent either.** [12]: "never disclosed to a participant or a
parent." I want to name this specifically because it is the one place the design protects the
adolescent *from* the household rather than only within it. That instinct is correct and I will ask
below for more of it.

**Pseudocommunity is built into the team's expectations.** [42]: "the sweetest-looking room can be
the lowest-trust room in the building." A discipleship program that has pre-committed to distrusting
its own harmony is doing something unusual and right.

**The full-strength limitation paragraph.** [59] does not flinch: "the covenant was written by the
designer and signed by a team he selected and formed... Nothing in the design is positioned to notice
what all of us take for granted." Everything I say below is said to a paper that has already said
that about itself, and I have tried to build past it rather than restate it.

**Voice-marking.** [9]: "A reader should be able to tell at a glance which sentences are a
practitioner's testimony and which are warrants." Keep this. It is what makes the paper reviewable.

## 2. Findings

### F1 — The design records the *shape* of hearing and never its *content*. **HIGH**

[29]: "The tell step follows in clusters: each person tells one thing they heard, a parent opening.
[...] the sheet will record whether telling happened, whether any of it cost anything, and what the
room did with the costliest one." [38] lists what the debrief page carries: "the three sign readings
at the room's current rung, the feedback round's echo, a participation read, the week's specific
counts, and follow-ups."

Here is a twenty-two-week curriculum whose entire domain is hearing and obeying God, whose measurement
architecture is built to see above a ceiling, and whose weekly record contains not one word of what
anybody said they heard. The design counts the act of telling and drops the telling. Everything is
frequency, breadth, cost, and rung; nothing is theology.

For a practical theology journal this is the missing centre. Cameron and colleagues, whom the paper
claims at [44] and [59], surface a community's *operant* theology — what the practice actually
believes, in the community's own words — as the primary datum. This design has an outsider ask once a
quarter what the *program* appears to believe [59], which is the espoused/operant question asked of
the leadership. It never asks what the *room* believes, and it has the room saying it out loud every
single week, in clusters, with a scribe already in the building.

The cost of the omission is not aesthetic. It is that the design cannot detect its most likely failure
mode. If a room grows fluent in the founder's idiom and reports hearing what the founder's vocabulary
has taught it to hear, every trace in this architecture rises: telling happens, cost is reported,
breadth is high, vocabulary migrates, rungs promote. The instrument reads success. The paper names the
vocabulary problem for the *team* at [59]; it does not notice that the same problem runs into the room
through the curriculum and comes back out as data.

**Remedy (fits the constraints).** One line on the existing debrief page: *one thing someone said they
heard tonight, in their own words, unnamed.* Verbatim, no interpretation, no coding. That is a
sentence, it costs a scribe ten seconds, it releases nothing (it stays under the covenant with
everything else), and at year's end it is a twenty-two-entry corpus of a room's operant theology of
hearing God — the single most publishable artifact this pilot could produce, and the only one that
could show convergence on the leader's idiom if convergence is what happened. If the page is truly
full, it should displace a count before it displaces this.

### F2 — The consent unit is "the family," and a minor's yes is absorbed into a parent's. **HIGH**

[21]: "I sit with each family and ask, in plain words, for permission... One yes covers the year's five
pages." [12]: "each individual's progress from wherever they start will be tracked to see the actual
change... levels are a Companion's diagnostic lens."

One yes. Whose? The paper's grammar throughout treats the family as a single consenting agent, which
is exactly what its theology of parental primacy [16] would predict, and exactly what research ethics
with minors does not permit. Parental permission and the young person's own assent are two different
things and they are meant to be sought separately, from the person, in words that person can decline.
Here a fourteen-year-old is enrolled in a school program by her parents, seated beside them, and made
the subject of a year of individually-tracked observation on the strength of a yes her mother gave in
a living room.

I want to be careful about what I am *not* saying. I am not saying separate the families — the brief
rules that out and I agree with the ruling. I am saying that asking each person present is not
separating anyone; it is what already happens at the table, done properly.

**Remedy.** In the same permission conversation, with parents present and their yes already given, ask
each young person directly, in the same plain words, and say out loud in front of the parents that a
young person's no costs nothing and will not be reported to them. Then instantiate it materially the
way the basket already does for families: every one of the five pages carries a line — *I'd rather
skip this one* — returned to the same unchased basket, so an individual decline is invisible in the
same way a family decline already is. This adds one line to five pages across a year, and it converts
a legal exposure into a piece of the curriculum's own theology of the un-asked.

### F3 — Participants are not told that a named, longitudinal written record exists about them. **HIGH**

[46]: "if it is paper a participant touches, it is asked for; if it is a shepherd's eye held under a
signed covenant, it is care." [38]: "Names appear on the team's side only, under a signed measurement
covenant... in a single secured location." [17]: "confidentiality promised out loud and its legal
limits named honestly."

The paper draws the line between care and data at *whose hands touch the paper*. That line is drawn
from the researcher's side of the desk. From the participant's side, the relevant question is not who
holds the pen but whether a file exists. And a file does exist: weekly dated sheets, names, followups
with owners and deadlines, individual level-tracking, verbatim scribing of the self-read [28], all in
"a single secured location." That is a record system. Calling it "a shepherd's eye" is a description
of the motive, not of the artifact.

I am not asking for the record to be released — the covenant forbids it and I accept the covenant. I
am saying that a room which has been told its confidentiality is protected, and has *not* been told
that written notes bearing its members' names accumulate weekly, has been told something less than the
truth by omission. The paper's own standard [17] is that limits are "named honestly." This is a limit.

**Remedy.** The container is spoken aloud every week and already names confidentiality and its legal
limits [17]. Add one clause to that existing script: *we keep notes so we can shepherd you well; no one
is scored; nothing goes outside this team; and you can ask any of us what is written about you.* Seven
seconds, no new paperwork, no release of anything. If the honest answer is that the team is unwilling
to say this out loud, that is itself a finding the team should sit with.

### F4 — Cross-generational reciprocity is counted as a marker of health, which converts teen disclosure into a target. **HIGH**

[50], among the six things a Companion should watch for: "vulnerability answered with vulnerability
across a generation line (a parent's costly tell answered by a teen's)." Against [21]: "Nothing in the
year is demanded" and [45]: "no target is ever announced, because the moment a mirror becomes a goal it
stops being a mirror."

The paper is scrupulous that nothing is *announced*. But announcement is not where the pressure lives.
The pressure lives in the fact that the room's most powerful adult has gone first, by rule, with
something costly; that the parent sitting beside the young person has just paid a price in public; and
that the design's ledger records a good week when the teenager matches it. The teenager does not need
to be told the target. The parent's disclosure *is* the target, delivered face to face, with the whole
room watching.

The paper cites Strathern correctly at [45] and then, four paragraphs earlier, makes exactly the
mistake Strathern describes — with the aggravating detail that the person whose behavior the measure
now shapes is a minor and the measure is administered by their own parent.

**Remedy inside the constraints.** Do not remove the marker; re-point it. The healthy thing to observe
is not that a teenager reciprocated but that *the room could hold it if one didn't*. Rewrite the sixth
marker as a pair on the same sheet line: *did anyone decline or pass, and did the room take the pass
without repair-work?* A room where a young person can say "I'll pass tonight" and the moment simply
closes is a safer room, by Edmondson's own construct, than a room where every parental tell is matched.
That is a one-line change and it is a better instrument.

### F5 — Social penetration does not transfer to a parent–minor dyad, and the design's central rule rests on it. **HIGH**

[16]: "Vulnerability is reciprocal; the disclosure research has said for fifty years that people deepen
by matched steps, each opening the next level for the other."

Altman and Taylor built social penetration on peer relationships that were symmetrical, voluntary, and
dissoluble — largely young adults who could stop reciprocating by declining the next meeting. None of
those three conditions holds between a fifteen-year-old and the parent who drives her home. The
relationship is asymmetrical in authority, non-voluntary, and non-exitable; she will be in the car with
this person in forty minutes and in the kitchen tomorrow. The reciprocity norm that operates benignly
between peers operates as obligation between unequals who cannot leave.

There is also a literature on parental self-disclosure to adolescents, and it is not the same
literature and it does not say the same thing. It worries — with evidence — about role reversal, about
adolescents recruited into carrying a parent's interior life, about the collapse of the developmental
privacy that is how a young person becomes a self. The paper's field evidence for the rule is one
family over eighteen months [16], which is a real finding and I do not dismiss it; but it is a finding
about that family, and the theoretical warrant offered to generalize it is borrowed from the wrong
relationship type.

**Remedy.** Two moves, both cheap. First, in the text: state the transfer explicitly the way the paper
already states the Krathwohl extension at [12] — *social penetration was established on symmetrical
peer dyads; this design extends it to an asymmetrical, non-exitable, intergenerational dyad, and that
extension is deliberate and could fail.* The paper has a good habit here; use it again. Second, add the
corresponding disconfirmation condition (see F13): the extension fails if teen disclosure appears
promptly and never varies, which is what obligation looks like from the outside.

### F6 — Break-survival cannot bear the inferential weight [56] puts on it. **HIGH**

[56]: "Flat or declining break-survival across the year, in a cohort that attends faithfully,
disconfirms the claim that the curriculum is moving anyone from compliance to valuing; it would suggest
we built an excellent weekly event and no interior change." [18]: "one practice per week makes practice
survival across breaks a clean, countable trace."

It is not a clean trace. It is a trace of internalization *multiplied by household capacity*, and the
design has no reading of the second term. A family that survives the January break has a January in
which surviving was possible. A family that does not may have had a job loss, a hospitalization, a
custody week, a new baby, a night-shift rotation, a mother doing this alone at ten o'clock after her
second job. The count reads all of that as compliance decaying. The qualifier "in a cohort that attends
faithfully" does not rescue the inference; attendance at four o'clock on a Wednesday is itself a
capacity measure, so the qualifier conditions on the very thing it needs to control.

This matters most because [56] is a *disconfirmation* condition. The paper has committed in advance to
reading a flat line as evidence against the whole framework. In a cohort of three to six families [8],
two hard winters would produce that flat line for reasons that have nothing to do with the theory.

**Remedy.** The re-entry check-in already asks how the break treated the practice and already welcomes
lapsed answers [28] — the material exists and costs nothing new. Add to the same sheet line where the
count is recorded: *and what the room said about why.* Then rewrite [56] so that flat break-survival
disconfirms the framework only when the room's own account of the lapse is about wanting to rather than
being able to. That is a stronger disconfirmation condition, not a weaker one, because it can no longer
be satisfied by a bad January.

### F7 — The home is a destination for practices and never a source of data. **HIGH**

[16]: "the practices that go home to kitchen tables, a family conversation night at the year's center."
[18]: "each week sends home exactly one practice, never two." [34]: "Journals are never collected; home
practice cards are never inspected."

I have spent a career arguing that the household is a site of theological production and not merely a
delivery address for what was produced at church, so let me say plainly what this design does. It
sends. It counts what came back. In the entire measurement architecture the home appears exactly once,
as a survival statistic. What happened at that table — who sat down, who wouldn't, the argument, the
father who found it excruciating, the daughter who did it alone because nobody else would, the mother
who is the only adult in the house and went first to an empty chair — is invisible to every instrument
in the pilot.

The paper's own theology says the parents are the primary teachers [16]. Its measurement says the
kitchen table is a compliance checkpoint. That gap is not a small one, and it is the gap a
living-human-web reading exists to see: the design has drawn its circle around the room and treated the
household as the room's periphery, when on the paper's own account the household is the centre and the
room is the periphery.

**Remedy.** The quarterly pulse already asks "what has surprised me" [34] — one of its three items.
Change one word so that the item is asked of the table rather than of the self: *what surprised you
about how this went at home.* No new page, no new permission, no new burden, and it opens the only
channel in the design through which the household can say something the curriculum did not ask for.

### F8 — The design's response to a young person's silence is pursuit, and there is no stopping rule. **HIGH**

[36]: "the sought stray (when someone goes quiet, who notices, and who goes after them)." [37]: the sign
"matures into seeking." [29]: "Participation breadth in the shared circle gets a one-line read:
everyone, most, or a few carried it."

The paper is candid that attrition is not read as data [59], and I am not re-filing that. This is a
different and, I think, sharper problem: within the room, going quiet is the only signal available to
the person with the least power, and this design has made responding to that signal a *marker of the
community's maturity*. The healthier the room becomes on its own instrument, the more reliably a quiet
teenager is followed. There is no rung at which the correct answer is to let her be quiet.

And the aggregate participation read cannot see her. "Everyone, most, or a few" is a room-level count;
a young person who is systematically in the "not a few" every week for twenty-two weeks leaves no trace
distinguishable from ordinary week-to-week variation.

**Remedy.** Give the sought stray a stopping rule and write it into the two calibration pages at the
front of the pack [38], where the worked examples already live: *one going-after; if the quiet holds,
it is honored, recorded as sought-declined, and read as a reading about the room, not about the
person.* This costs nothing, it makes the sign more honest, and it gives the design its first mechanism
by which a young person's withdrawal can mean no rather than mean go after her.

### F9 — Theological action research's insider half is quoted and then left unfilled. **HIGH**

[59]: "theological action research as Cameron and colleagues set it out is a partnership between
insiders and outsiders, not a practitioner reporting alone, and this pilot as described is the
practitioner reporting alone. Two corrections follow, and both are adopted for the pilot year."

Read the two corrections. The first is a surprise line on the leader's own debrief sheet. The second is
an outside reader. Both sit on the outsider side of the partnership, or inside the leader's own head.
The insiders in Cameron's sense are not the practitioner — they are the community whose practice is
being theologized. The paper diagnoses itself precisely and then treats only the half of the diagnosis
that does not require sharing interpretive authority with the families.

This is the finding I would most want the author to sit with, because it is not an oversight of
technique. It is the same reflex the paper has already caught in itself elsewhere, showing up in the
one place the paper thought it had been cured.

**Remedy, entirely inside the constraints.** Once a quarter, the Thursday call [38] seats one parent and
one teenager — rotating, volunteer, by invitation never rule — not as subjects but as co-readers of the
quarter. They see no confidential notes and no names; they are asked the same three questions the room
is asked, and then one more the room is never asked: *what do you think we, the team, are getting
wrong?* Their answers are recorded whether or not the team recognizes itself in them, on the same terms
already granted to the outside reader [59]. This does not separate a family, does not stage conflict,
does not release notes, does not grade anyone, and it fills the empty half of the method the paper has
already named.

### F10 — "A family is never divided" leaves a young person with no door. **HIGH — and this is an argument against a stated constraint**

[16]: "families share circles, a family is never divided, and in every practice the parents start."
[17]: "confidentiality promised out loud and its legal limits named honestly."

The brief tells me that remedies separating families are pastorally unacceptable, and as a default rule
for the *curriculum* I accept that and think the paper is right. I am arguing against the absolute, not
the default, and I am arguing it because of what "costly telling" means for a young person whose cost
is at home.

Consider the design from inside one plausible household. The costly thing this young person could tell
is about her father. The rule is that her father speaks first. The circle contains her father. The
sixth vital-sign marker rewards her for answering his disclosure with her own. There is no session, no
pairing, and no channel in twenty-two weeks in which she is in a room without him. The design has made
the one telling that matters most structurally unavailable, and it has done so while making telling the
central virtue of the year.

The paper acknowledges mandated-reporting limits [17], which is right and which I credit. But mandated
reporting is a rule about what a leader does *after* a disclosure. It is not a route to disclosure.
This design has no route.

**Remedy that does not divide a family.** Name one adult — a school counselor, a companion, a
pastor — who is not in the circle, announce that person by name in the container script from week one
in front of everyone including the parents, and say that any young person may talk to them about
anything at any time and does not need a reason. That is a door, not a separation. It costs one sentence
in a script that is already spoken aloud every week. I would consider it non-negotiable for publication
of any protocol that seats minors with their own parents and makes disclosure the year's central
practice, and I would say so to the editor.

### F11 — Leader-first is applied to the one instrument that could revise the design. **MEDIUM**

[40]: "the room answers them together, leader first as always." [41]: "The leader answers first, nobody
is anonymous, and every informant is also a subject."

The paper already concedes that the self-read is not independent, and I am not re-filing that. My point
is about its *second* function, which the paper does not treat as a measurement question at all: this is
the mechanism by which the curriculum learns what to change [40], the room's only route to revising the
year. Anchoring it means the design can be revised in directions the founder has already voiced and
essentially no others. Anchoring effects on a first-spoken answer are strongest precisely for the people
lowest in the room's status order, which here means the teenagers.

**Remedy.** Keep leader-first for questions one and two, where the modeling function is real. For the
third — "what would you tell somebody else about what this group is like," which the paper itself calls
the most revealing [41] — the leader answers *last*. One sentence of sequencing, no separation, no new
instrument, and it materially improves the paper's best qualitative datum.

### F12 — "Costly" is not indexed to who pays, and this cohort makes the costs wildly unequal. **MEDIUM**

[36]: "costly telling (does anyone tell what God said and what obeying cost)." [37]: promotion "after
three consecutive weeks of seen."

Cost is treated as a property of the disclosure. It is a property of the discloser's position. A parent
who tells something hard is telling it to peers he chose to sit with, and the social consequence is
mostly admiration. A sixteen-year-old telling something hard in a small Christian school is telling it
to a parent she lives with, to adults who will see her mother at pickup, and — through ordinary
adolescent social physics — potentially to a hallway on Monday morning. The container's promise of
confidentiality [17] is spoken, sincere, and, in a building where everyone sees everyone daily, only as
strong as the least discreet person in it.

So the same rung reading, "costly telling seen," denotes two very different acts depending on who
performed it, and the room-level sign aggregates them. A room can promote to the top rung on the
strength of adult disclosures alone.

**Remedy.** The debrief sheet already records "what the room did with the costliest one" [29]. Add two
characters to that line: *who told it — adult or young person.* Not a name, not a score. At year's end
the team can see whether the costly telling that carried the room was ever paid for by the people with
the least protection, which is a question the design currently cannot ask.

### F13 — "Self-selected" names the fact of selection and never the mechanism. **MEDIUM**

[59]: "One room, one year, one school, one founder. There is no control cohort; families self-selected."
[8]: "three to six families, six to fifteen participants in all."

Every serious limitation in this paper is stated except the one that determines what the knowledge is
about. This cohort is not merely self-selected; it is selected by a stack of specific filters, and each
filter is a description of the population the findings will describe:

- **Tuition at a private Christian school.** An income floor, and in most such schools a fairly narrow
  racial and denominational band.
- **Four o'clock on a Wednesday, weekly, for a school year.** This is the sharpest filter in the design
  and the paper does not mention it. It selects for households with an adult whose work is salaried,
  flexible, self-directed, or absent. Hourly and shift workers are excluded by the timetable, not by
  choice. So are households where the only adult works.
- **Whole-family enrollment with parents in the circle.** A household with one available adult, or with
  one parent who will not come, or with a parent who is not a believer, cannot satisfy "the parents
  start" as written.
- **Willingness to be observed and to disclose in front of one's own child.** A household in conflict,
  or in the aftermath of one, self-excludes at exactly the point where it would have most to teach.

Note what the denominator quietly confirms: three to six families producing six to fifteen participants
averages about two and a half people per family. "The parents," plural, is already not what most of
these households will look like.

**Remedy.** Two paragraphs, no new data. First, replace "families self-selected" with a named account of
the filters — including the Wednesday-at-four filter, which the author can state from his own
recruitment experience. Second, change what the paper claims the year is *about*: not family-integrated
formation, but formation in households already structured to succeed at it. That is a smaller claim,
and the paper says at [59] that it prefers the smaller claim; this is one it has not yet made.

### F14 — Which parent goes first is never recorded, and the design has not noticed it is a question. **MEDIUM**

[16]: "the parents tell first, and tell something real... in every practice the parents start." [50]:
"a parent's costly tell answered by a teen's."

Throughout, "the parents" is a single undifferentiated agent. In the actual room it will not be. In
evangelical family-discipleship settings, ambient headship norms mean that "the parent goes first"
resolves in practice either to *the father always*, in which case the room learns weekly that
theological speech is the father's office, or to *the mother always*, in which case the room learns that
emotional and disclosive labor is women's work while the father supervises. Both are formative
outcomes. Neither is measured. Both are exactly the sort of thing a year of weekly repetition installs
deeply and invisibly.

The one sentence in the paper that does specify a gender is worth reading slowly. [16]: "on the
reasonable fear that no teenager is honest in front of their mother." The mother is the figure before
whom truth is impossible; the parent who licenses truth by going first is generic. That asymmetry is
not argued, and I do not think the author noticed writing it.

While we are in the same paragraph: Ephesians 6:4 is cited as warrant for parental primacy. Its actual
address is to fathers specifically, and its actual first clause is a restraint on paternal power — do
not provoke your children. The design has taken the mandate and dropped the restraint, and the restraint
is the half that speaks to a rule requiring fathers to disclose first, at cost, in front of their
children, every week.

**Remedy.** One tally on the debrief page, in the same space as the participation read: *which parent
opened, per family pair.* It costs a tick mark. Twenty-two weeks of it is a genuine finding about the
gendered distribution of disclosure labor in family discipleship, and it is data no one in our field
currently has.

### F15 — The prototype family is undisclosed and may be in the cohort. **MEDIUM**

[16]: "A year and a half of prototyping with one faithful family taught me the opposite." [8]: "the
design was prototyped with one family across eighteen months."

The design's most consequential rule — the one that seats minors with their parents and orders the
speaking — rests on n = 1, over eighteen months, with a family whose relationship to the author is never
stated. That relationship is load-bearing. A family recruited from the school is one thing; the author's
own family, or a family from his leadership team, is quite another, and the reader cannot tell which.

The methodological consequence is concrete and not merely about disclosure: if that family is enrolled
in the pilot, they enter in year three of the practice while everyone else enters in week one. Their
fluency will register on every sign as the curriculum working, in a cohort of three to six families
where one family is a fifth to a third of the denominator.

**Remedy.** State the relationship in the text. If the family is enrolled, flag their readings on the
team's side of the sheet so that the year's traces can be read with and without them — no separation, no
disclosure to anyone, one column.

### F16 — The families' own congregations are absent from the ecology and from the ethics. **MEDIUM**

[16]: "A program that quietly takes a family's formation over has failed even where it appears to
succeed." [61]: "every companion stands under a named covering of their own."

The paper is vigilant about one displacement — a program supplanting the parents — and blind to its
twin. This is a school-based, non-congregational fellowship, twenty-two weeks long, deliberately
building the most trusting room its members have been in, aiming at people who "have become unwilling to
go back to rooms without it" [51]. Wuthnow is in the bibliography and is exactly the right witness here:
intense small groups reliably become the primary religious community of their members and their members'
congregations reliably become the thing they are unwilling to go back to.

The families in this pilot presumably attend several different churches, or none. None of those churches
is named, consulted, covenanted with, or given a role. Where does a costly telling go on Sunday? Whose
pastor knows this is happening? Companions have a named covering; the families do not.

**Remedy.** A paragraph in the design commitments applying the paper's own conviction symmetrically: the
program encourages and equips the family's congregation and never replaces it. Then a mechanism as light
as the conviction requires — the permission conversation [21], which is already happening in each home,
adds one question: *who is your church, and would you like us to tell them you're doing this?* Their no
costs nothing, on the same terms as everything else.

### F17 — Adolescents are figured throughout as adults-in-preparation, never as present theological agents. **MEDIUM**

[19]: "These teens are a few years from college dormitories, and one overall aim of the pilot is that
they leave able to lead other teens." [12], warranting the corporate turn: "the group learns to hear God
as a body, one member receiving, another testing, another seeing the application, a competence Scripture
treats as belonging to the gathered body itself (1 Cor 2:16; 14:26–29; Acts 15:28)."

Two things sit uneasily together here, and I think the second is the more serious.

First, the teenagers' value in this design is consistently deferred. They are apprenticed, entrusted,
prepared, aimed at a dormitory. The role available to them is to run a second running of a practice
adults designed. There is no channel by which a fifteen-year-old's theological insight changes the
curriculum — the self-read is the nearest thing and it is anchored by the leader (F11). A practical
theology of childhood and adolescence that I would recognize starts from the position that young people
are already bearers of the Spirit with something to teach the adults now, not in four years.

Second, and more pointedly: the proof text the paper chooses for its corporate turn is 1 Corinthians
14:26–29, whose whole logic is that the Spirit distributes without regard to standing — *each one has a
hymn, a lesson, a revelation* — and whose only rule about order is that it be orderly, not that it be
ranked. The design then imposes a fixed, permanent speaking order by generation and calls it a
commitment [16]. The one place in the year where the paper's central text would level the room is the
one place the design has hardened the hierarchy.

And "a few years from college dormitories" is a class script. It assumes these young people leave home
for residential college. Some will work. Some will stay. Some will commute, or enlist, or care for a
grandparent. Naming the aim that way tells those young people, weekly, whose future the curriculum was
built around.

**Remedy.** Two sentences and one session. In the text: state the aim as leading wherever they land, and
name the exegetical tension in [12] rather than leaving a reader to find it. In the year: reserve at
least one session in the fourth quarter — the consolidating quarter, where it fits — in which the young
people open, by invitation and never by rule, and the adults answer. No family is divided, no conflict
is staged, nothing is graded. It is the corporate turn applied to the one variable the design has held
fixed all year, and it would be the single most informative hour in twenty-two Wednesdays.

### F18 — The room never gets to contest the account written about it. **MEDIUM**

[63] describes what "the measurement all works" will have shown; [59] describes what will be claimed:
"this ran, here is everything it recorded against the predictions it published."

Recorded by whom, and checked with whom? The design has the room speak about itself quarterly and
receive its own words "without correction" [28], which is right. But nothing is ever read back. The
families will not see, in any form, the account of their year that goes to a journal. In a
participatory discipline this is the norm most reliably enforced and the one most reliably skipped by
practitioner-scholars, precisely because we love the people we are writing about and are certain we have
been fair.

**Remedy.** At the last session, or in a written page to each household, the room hears the narrative
account of its own year before it is submitted — story and specific moments, which is the register the
covenant already mandates [12], no levels, no rungs, no names. And they are invited to say what is
wrong with it, with the team's disagreements recorded rather than resolved. This releases nothing
confidential; it is the same account the journal will get.

### F19 — The outside reader is chosen by the author, sees one announced session, and is recorded rather than published. **LOW**

[59]: "once a quarter a reader from outside the team spends two hours with the published materials and
one session and answers a single question... the answer is recorded whether or not the team recognizes
itself in it."

Good instinct, weak implementation on three counts. The reader is recruited by the person being checked.
One announced session is the least representative hour of the quarter. And "recorded" is not
"published" — recorded means the author decides what the reader's answer meant.

**Remedy.** The school, not the author, supplies the name from a list of candidates. The visit is
unannounced within a named two-week window. And the four answers are reproduced verbatim in an appendix
to the results paper, unedited, whatever they say. The first two are free; the third is the only one
that converts the reader from a gesture into a check.

## 3. What my discipline would consider missing

**A thick description of the context.** There is no description of this school, this town, this
population — no racial composition, no economic range, no denominational mix, no sense of what these
households do for money or who else lives in them. Practical theology's first obligation is to say where
it is standing before it says what it saw. A reader cannot situate knowledge whose context has not been
described, and the paper cannot claim a limitation ("families self-selected") whose content it has not
supplied.

**Household-level description, and the household as an analytic unit.** Not a demographic table — a
portrait. Who is in each household, who works when, who else the young people answer to, which
congregation, who is missing from the circle and why. The paper's entire theology is about households
and its entire dataset is about a room.

**The operant theology of participants.** Named at F1 and it is the largest absence. A study of hearing
God that does not record what anyone heard is a study of a room's speech behavior.

**A theology of childhood and adolescence.** The paper has a fully developed theology of parenthood
(Deut 6, Eph 6) and no theology of the young people at all. There is no claim anywhere about what a
teenager is, theologically, that would justify or constrain the way this design treats them. In a paper
whose most contested design choice is about minors, that is the missing warrant.

**A participatory role for participants.** Insiders as co-interpreters, which the paper's own chosen
method requires and its own limitations paragraph identifies (F9).

**Member checking.** (F18.)

**Safeguarding architecture, as distinct from psychological safety.** The paper has a rich account of
whether a room feels safe for interpersonal risk and no account of what happens when a young person is
not safe at home. These are different literatures and different obligations, and the six markers at [50]
belong entirely to the first.

**Attention to the wider web.** Congregations (F16), the school's own authority over these families,
extended kin, employers whose schedules set who can attend. The unit of analysis here is a circle of
chairs. The unit of formation is a web, and the paper's own best sentence — [5], about who built the
room — is a web observation the design otherwise has no way to make.

## 4. What I would have done differently

Same convictions, same constraints, same four o'clock classroom.

1. **I would have made the household the unit of the study, and described it.** One page per family at
   enrollment, by permission, once: who lives here, who works when, who else this young person answers
   to, which church, what a Wednesday actually costs you. Every trace in the year — break survival,
   attendance, participation breadth — read against that page rather than against an average. This is
   the single change that would most improve the paper's inferences, and it costs one page per family
   for one year.

2. **I would have written what people heard.** One verbatim, unnamed line a week. (F1.)

3. **I would have seated a parent and a teenager on the Thursday call once a quarter,** as co-readers of
   the year with the standing question *what are we getting wrong* — the insider half of the method the
   paper already quotes. (F9.)

4. **I would have made the speaking order a variable rather than a doctrine.** Parents first through the
   first two quarters, where the licensing function is real and the field evidence supports it. Then, in
   the fourth quarter, one session where the young people open by invitation. Same room, same families,
   nothing staged, nothing separated — and the design gets to find out whether its central rule is a
   scaffold or a ceiling. As written, the pilot can never learn this about itself.

5. **I would have named a door.** One adult, outside the circle, announced by name every week in front
   of the parents, whom any young person may talk to about anything. (F10.) I would treat this as a
   condition of publication.

6. **I would have asked each young person for their own yes,** in the room, with the parents present and
   their no protected by the same basket that already protects a family's. (F2.)

7. **I would have tallied which parent goes first.** (F14.) It is a tick mark, and it is the most
   interesting unclaimed finding available to this pilot.

8. **I would have claimed less, and claimed it more precisely.** Not "a family-integrated formation
   pilot" but "formation among six households that could clear the Wednesday-at-four bar at a small
   Christian school, described." The paper says at [59] that the smaller claim is the faithful one. This
   is the smaller claim it has not yet made.

## 5. My single biggest concern

To the editor: this is a paper I would want to see in print, and my central worry is not any of its
stated limitations but a silence that runs underneath all of them. The design records with real care
*that* people spoke, *how much* it cost, and *who* answered, and it records nowhere at all *what anyone
said they heard from God* — so the one failure mode most likely in a founder-led, founder-designed,
founder-observed room is the one failure mode invisible to every instrument in it: a room becoming
fluent in its leader's idiom and reporting that fluency back as revelation, with every trace in the
architecture rising as it does. The family rule concentrates that risk rather than dispersing it,
because the design has arranged for the person most likely to hand a teenager the idiom to be the parent
who speaks first, by rule, having received it from the leader the week before — and then counts the
teenager's matching answer as a marker of the community's health. The remedies are small and they fit
inside every constraint the author has set: write down one thing someone said they heard, each week, in
their own words; seat a parent and a teenager among the readers rather than only among the read; ask the
young people for their own yes; and name one adult outside the circle they can talk to. What this paper
needs is not more rigor. It needs the people it is about to be able to talk back to it, and the room's
own theology to appear somewhere in its own record.
