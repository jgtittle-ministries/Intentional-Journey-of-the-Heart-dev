# Review — "Twenty-Two Wednesdays as a Fellowship of the Heart"

**Reviewer:** John H. Coe (Talbot School of Theology, Institute for Spiritual Formation)
**Stage:** C — spiritual theology desk
**Recommendation:** Major revision. The paper is publishable in this journal, but not in this form. Its defects are not methodological — Stages A and B have done that work — they are defects of *spiritual theology*, and they sit at the center rather than at the edges.

---

## Preliminary: what I am not going to complain about

I want to name what is genuinely good, because several of my findings will be hard and the author should be able to tell that they come from someone who read the whole thing.

The room-build is a real discovery and the author is right to have noticed it. "Care wins, and we count the cost" is the most honest paragraph I have read in a formation protocol in some years, and the specific enumeration of forfeited evidence is a model. "No target is ever announced... the moment a mirror becomes a goal it stops being a mirror" is correct, and citing Strathern for it rather than a devotional source is the right instinct. Reading an honest report of a lapsed practice as an offering-level sign is pastorally shrewd. The pseudocommunity warning is exactly right and most designs of this kind lack it. The founder-absence test is cheap, brave, and scheduled. And the self-limitation paragraph in "How This Design Could Be Shown Wrong" is more withering than anything I could write about the author's independence, which is why I will not write it.

What follows is not about rigor. It is about whether this is a spiritual formation design or a well-run group-process design with a theological attribution attached at the end.

---

## Finding 1 — The Spirit is credited but not operative. HIGH

**What is wrong.** The Holy Spirit appears exactly once in the article, in the second-to-last paragraph:

> "Nothing in the apparatus forms anyone. The Holy Spirit does that, and the instruments read only the traces He leaves in a room that has agreed to be looked at."

I checked this against the text rather than trusting the disclosure. In the body of the article, "Spirit" occurs once; "grace" once; "prayer" once and "prayed" once; "Christ" as a word, never. Meanwhile every *mechanism* the paper actually names is a natural mechanism, and each is properly sourced to a secular or quasi-secular literature: formation by habituated desire (Smith), the container (Edmondson), the migration held → known → owned (Lewicki/Bunker/Shapiro), disagreement before trust (Tuckman/Wheelan), reciprocal disclosure (Altman/Taylor), the teach-back (Bargh/Schul), internalization itself (Krathwohl). There is no third thing. The Spirit is the paper's stated cause and none of its described causes.

The sharpest form of the problem is in Table 3. Take the six disconfirmation conditions on their own terms: the ladder climbs, the sources disagree, practices survive the breaks, the container survives a teen leader, the vocabulary migrates from *held* toward *owned*, the covenant costs something. Every one of the six would be satisfied, at the stated thresholds, by a competently run secular mentoring cohort with a confidentiality ethic and a warm facilitator. The design therefore carries no condition under which the room's outcome would look different if the Spirit were not the agent. A protocol that cannot distinguish its own stated cause from its stated non-cause has not made that cause operative; it has made it an attribution.

I will grant immediately what the author will want to say: the practices themselves — Scripture, telling, confession, commissioning — are ordinary means, and their presence is the pneumatology. I do not think that answer survives contact with the article's own description of them, because in every case the article *warrants* the practice from the natural literature rather than from the tradition, and in one case says so outright (Finding 7).

**Why it matters.** This journal exists on the premise that spiritual formation is not a subfield of applied social psychology. The paper's contribution — forming and observing are one activity — is real and I want it published. But offered in this form it will be read, correctly, as a demonstration that a sufficiently careful group-process design produces the traces we call formation. That is the opposite of the author's intent and the paper gives a reader no purchase against it.

**Remedy.** Three moves, none of which touches a constraint.

1. Move the pneumatology from the conclusion to "Design Commitments and Their Warrants," and state it as a *commitment* with consequences: what the Spirit does, what the practices therefore are (places of receptivity, not causes of growth), and what follows for the reading of the instruments.
2. Schedule dependence. There is currently no moment in the seventy-five minutes at which the room asks God to do what the curriculum cannot. Add one, and add one line to the debrief page recording what the team asked for and did not receive. That line will be the single most theologically informative datum the year produces, and it costs a sentence on a page the author has already budgeted.
3. Either add a disconfirmation condition that a purely social account would not predict, or — better, and more in the paper's own spirit — state plainly in the limitations that the design cannot distinguish Spirit-wrought formation from well-formed group process, and that this is a limit on the *interpretation* of every reading in the year. The author's instinct for naming what he cannot see is excellent everywhere else in the paper. He has not turned it on the one thing that matters most.

## Finding 2 — "Internalization" is doing the work of sanctification, without the doctrine. HIGH

**What is wrong.** The paper uses internalization as its organizing account of growth and never says what relation it bears to sanctification. The two candidate relations — that internalization *is* sanctification measured, or that it is a natural substrate the Spirit may or may not be using — have entirely different implications for every reading in the year, and the paper leaves the question unasked.

The text pushes toward the first. The top of the ladder is described as:

> "The settled life in which hearing and obeying God has become simply who a person is"

and the pivot the year aims at is:

> "the group's level two to three threshold, where intrinsic motivation begins to take over from external structure"

That second sentence is not Krathwohl; it is self-determination language, and it is theologically loaded in a way the paper does not notice. On this account maturity is the migration of the locus of motivation from outside the self to inside it. But Christian maturity is not increasing self-regulation. It is increasing *dependence* — a man who at forty needs God more obviously than he did at twenty, and knows it. The paper's ladder has no rung for that, and its highest rung, "simply who a person is," names a stable and self-consistent religious identity. Philippians 3:4–6 is the curriculum vitae of a man at the top of exactly that ladder.

The decisive tell is where the paper does deploy grace. It appears once, and it appears here:

> "no reading of any instrument measures anyone's standing with God, which is complete by grace at every stage."

That is a good sentence and I am glad it is there. But look at what it does: grace is assigned to *standing*, and growth is left entirely to internalization. That is the precise division — justification by grace, sanctification by a natural developmental mechanism — that spiritual theology in this journal's tradition exists to refuse. The author has protected justification carefully and handed sanctification to Krathwohl.

**Why it matters.** Every promotion rule, every rung, and the whole hoped-for "average movement of about one level" inherits whichever answer the author would give. A reviewer, a replicating practitioner, and a Companion marking a sheet all need to know whether they are watching a work of grace or a psychological process that grace may be using. Right now they cannot know, and the design behaves as though it were the former while arguing as though it were the latter.

**Remedy.** One paragraph in Design Commitments, stating the relation explicitly. My own view, offered as a reviewer's suggestion and not a requirement: internalization is a *natural correlate* of formation, observable, sometimes produced by the Spirit and sometimes produced without Him, and therefore evidence that must always be read alongside the possibility of its counterfeit. If the author adopts that, then the counterfeit must be named in the paper: a fully internalized false self, for whom hearing and obeying God has indeed become "simply who a person is," and who is further from God at the top of the ladder than he was at the bottom. And the level 2–3 language should be rewritten so that the target is not intrinsic motivation displacing external structure but something that does not put the self at the center of its own maturity.

## Finding 3 — There is no doctrine of sin, no account of resistance, and no reading for the participant whose signs go backward because God is at work. HIGH

**What is wrong.** The words *sin*, *repentance*, *forgiveness*, and *flesh* do not occur in this article. I do not raise that as a word count; I raise it because the design's behavior matches the absence exactly.

There are three marks available to a Companion — "seen, not yet, or no occasion" — and one movement:

> "promotes a rung after three consecutive weeks of seen"

The author already concedes, in the limitations, that the rule "advances a rung after three consecutive readings of seen while providing no path back down." He treats this as a psychometric infelicity. It is a theological one. A formation instrument that can only ratchet upward has committed itself to the view that in the Christian life nothing is ever lost, nothing is ever hidden, and no one is ever led into a season where the visible fruit is withdrawn.

Now put the three vital signs beside the classical account of the dark night. Costly telling, response to load, the sought stray are all *outputs*, and all three are diminished — not corrupted, diminished — in a genuine night. Dryness in the things of God; the loss of the ability to pray as one used to; a felt distance from the community; and, most pertinently here, a reticence about the interior life for the honest reason that there is nothing consoling to report. St. John of the Cross's own signs of the night would register in this instrument as regression across the board, and would register most sharply on costly telling, which is the sign the whole liturgy is built to elicit. Table 3 then converts that regression into a disconfirmation of the design:

> "Any sign still marked not yet in its first form in February, or no growing form recorded all year"

And the practitioner's markers reward exactly what the night takes away:

> "the room's own words about itself will migrate from safety language toward belonging language... people who have tasted what a room like this is and have become unwilling to go back to rooms without it"

That is a design that reads consolation as progress. It is not that the author believes this; it is that nothing in the instrument can distinguish a person losing consolation because God is deconstructing his self-effort from a person who is simply not growing. And in a room where "in every practice the parents start," the most likely candidate for such a season is a parent, who must go first every week in front of his own children with nothing in hand.

The paper does have one counterfeit in it — the pseudocommunity warning, which is good. It has a counterfeit for *harmony*. It has none for *progress*, and no category at all for genuine regress, for resistance, or for a room whose sweetness is avoidance of repentance rather than avoidance of conflict.

**Why it matters.** Two harms, both pastoral. First, a team trained on this instrument will press exactly where God is asking for waiting; the follow-up rule ("follow-ups under a forty-eight-hour rule, each with a name, an owner, and a deadline") gives that pressing an operational form. Second, the design will teach the room that dryness is failure, which is the single most damaging thing a formation program can teach, and the one thing this journal's readership is best positioned to notice.

**Remedy.** All of these respect the constraints.

1. Add a fourth mark beside seen / not yet / no occasion: **withdrawn** — a sign previously seen that has ceased. It is not a demotion and does not move a rung; it is a pastoral flag, recorded with its eight-word occasion note like the others.
2. Give the handbook a named category for the dry season, with one rule attached: a participant or a room in it is not read as regression, is not made a follow-up, and is not pressed to tell. The author's own "Nothing is ever asked" commitment already contains this; it has simply never been applied to the case where it costs the instrument something.
3. Add to the covenant a companion clause to the weep test: *read nothing as failure that God may be doing in the dark.*
4. Add the corresponding disconfirmation condition, which the design currently lacks entirely — a condition under which the design is shown to have *harmed*: whether anyone was pressed to tell in a season they should have been left alone. The Thursday decisions log is already the right place to read it.

## Finding 4 — The design drops testing from the corporate turn, and testing is the only thing that distinguishes hearing God from hearing oneself. HIGH

**What is wrong.** The author names this as a deliberate loss and asks to have it weighed rather than discovered, so I will weigh it. My finding is not the one he is braced for. I am not going to ask him to collect journals.

The paper's fourth principle states the corporate turn:

> "The group learns to hear God as a body, one member receiving, another testing, another seeing the application, a competence Scripture treats as belonging to the gathered body itself (1 Cor 2:16; 14:26–29; Acts 15:28)."

*Another testing.* That is the paper's own theology, and 1 Cor 14:29 — "let the others weigh what is said" — is the strongest of the three texts cited, the one that actually says what the principle claims. Then follow the word "testing" through the rest of the article. It never returns. Every subsequent occurrence of *test* in the manuscript is methodological: the leadership absence test, the weep test, the founder-absence test. The words *discern* and *discernment* do not appear in the article at all. The measurement architecture schedules the receiving (the six-step practice) and schedules the telling (step six, made liturgical, counted) and drops the middle term.

The reason it drops out is structural: testing is about content, and content is not recorded, so the sign sheet records

> "whether telling happened, whether any of it cost anything, and what the room did with the costliest one"

— that is, the room's *response*, without any criterion for what a right response would be.

The consequence is that the entire ladder can be climbed on false hearing. A room can advance every rung — costly telling, load carried toward God, strays sought unasked, vocabulary migrating to *owned* — while what it hears becomes progressively more confident and less true, and every instrument in the design will read that room as a success. In a curriculum whose single domain is *hearing and obeying God*, that is not a peripheral gap.

I will state the counter-case fairly, because I think the author's constraint is right as far as it goes. Recording the content of God's speech to a family would make the telling performative, would turn the Spirit's address into a corpus, and would fail the weep test outright. I accept all of that. But it justifies not *collecting* content. It does not justify having no doctrine of discernment, and the paper has none. The author has silently converted "we will not record what was heard" into "we need not say how the room knows whether it heard."

There is a safeguarding edge to this as well. In a room where parents speak first and minors are present, "God told me" is a sentence that carries authority. The handbook's protections are for distress and abuse; there is nothing here for the ordinary case of a confidently mistaken hearing that a fifteen-year-old has no standing to question.

**Remedy.** Restore the middle term without collecting a word of content.

1. Make the testing act liturgical, as the tell step already is: after the tell, the room weighs — against Scripture first, then the body, then the fruit. This is the paper's own 1 Cor 14:29, and it happens aloud, in public, where the design already looks.
2. Record it the way the sheet already records the tell: *whether testing happened, and by what standard.* That is content-free at the record level and gives the year a trace of the one competence the corporate turn is named for.
3. Add a fourth vital sign, or a fourth column, for the room's weighing — it will run its own rungs (accepting what is said → asking a clarifying question → weighing against Scripture unprompted), and it is more diagnostic of Organization than the vocabulary migration is.
4. State the article's doctrine of discernment in a paragraph, including what the team does when a hearing is unwise or harmful and a minor is on the receiving end of it.

## Finding 5 — The prototype household sets the room's canon of what hearing God sounds like, and its relation to the author is never stated. MED-HIGH

**What is wrong.** Stage B will have handled the prototype family as prior exposure, selection, and a rival explanation. My concern is different and is not a validity concern.

> "Both parents and their four teenagers ran the practices through the prototype, three of the teens have already led sessions, including the opening of the container, and some of them are expected to enroll in the cohort described here. The prototype group also previews each session before the cohort meets it."

The design's own account of how formation transmits is modeling: "the teen watches the person they know best being formed, which no curriculum can fake." In the tell step, the first voice in a cluster establishes what counts as having heard from God — its length, its register, its certainty, its emotional temperature. If the practiced tellers are a single household with eighteen months of rehearsal, and its teens open the container, then that household is the room's operative canon of what the Spirit sounds like. Six to fifteen participants is a small enough room for one family to set that norm decisively in the first quarter, and it will be set before any instrument could register it, because the instruments record *whether* telling happened, never what it sounded like.

Second, and separable: the article never states the author's relationship to that family. "One faithful family," "the prototype family," "I sit with the families to ask permission," "watching my own instruments through a parent's eyes." Whatever the answer is, the reader needs it, and needs it in the body rather than inferred. If the author is a parent in that household, then the room's canon of hearing, the previewing of every session, the design, the covenant, the instruments, and the reading of the instruments all sit inside one home.

**Why it matters.** This is a formation risk before it is a research risk, and it is invisible to the whole architecture. It also bears directly on the paper's longest target — teens who can form a room in a college dormitory. A teen formed to one household's idiom of hearing has been given a dialect, not a competence.

**Remedy.** State the relationship plainly in the disclosure paragraph. Then stagger the opening voices: the constraint is that *parents* go first, not that the *practiced* go first. For the first quarter, the parent who opens the tell should be a parent new to the practice, and the prototype teens should not open the container until the second quarter. This costs the design nothing it has committed to and buys the room a canon of hearing formed in several unpracticed voices, which is the more honest picture of what a first year sounds like anyway.

## Finding 6 — "Parents go first" places spiritual direction inside family authority, and every safeguard listed addresses harm rather than the ceding of the interior life. MED-HIGH

**What is wrong.** Credit first: the handbook's protections are better than most family-discipleship designs carry — "a Companion watching each teenager during a parent's telling, a two-adult rule, mandatory reporting named in advance rather than discovered, and one adult outside the Companion team whom any participant can reach without going through a Companion or a parent." And the author already names in the limitations that teens are "seated beside their own parents in a room where parents speak first" and never heard on their own.

But notice what every one of those safeguards is *for*: distress, abuse, disclosure risk, reporting. None of them touches the ordinary, non-abusive, far more common case — a devout parent who, week after week, in a domain defined as hearing and obeying God, becomes for their teenager the standard of what God says. That is not a safeguarding failure. It is the normal way a borrowed faith is formed in a good home, and it is precisely what the design's longest target is meant to prevent:

> "These teens are a few years from college dormitories, and one overall aim of the pilot is that they leave able to lead other teens."

The author has seen the objection and answered it:

> "What separates training from echo is time and absence, and the year schedules both: practices that survive a break nobody supervises, a teen-led night no adult carries, and a stray sought by someone nobody pointed."

Each of those tests whether the *practice* survives the parent's absence. None tests whether the *content and authority* of hearing has been ceded. A seventeen-year-old can run the six-step practice flawlessly in an empty room and hear, at every step, her father's voice. The design has no occasion on which she would find that out, and no occasion on which anyone else would.

**Why it matters.** For this journal, spiritual direction inside a structure of authority the directee cannot leave is a standing question, and this design has it in its purest form: a minor, her own parent, a domain of divine speech, a weekly public liturgy, and an explicit rule that the parent speaks first every time.

**Remedy.** I am not asking that families be divided; I think the author is right about that and the prototype evidence is worth something. Two smaller moves:

1. Make the outside adult a *practice* rather than a safety valve. Once a quarter, each teen tells one thing they heard to a non-parent adult, with the parent present and not answering. That is inside the family circle, it divides nothing, and it is the only occasion in the year on which a teen's hearing stands on its own feet in front of someone.
2. Schedule at least one reversal per quarter, named and warranted rather than smuggled: the child hears and the elder receives it. 1 Samuel 3 is the canonical instance and sits at the center of the hearing-God literature. A design whose theology is that the body hears together, and whose liturgy has the elder speaking first every single time, is asserting something about the Spirit's distribution of gifts that its own fourth principle denies. This is the one place where I am asking the author to bend a stated constraint, and I am asking it on his own ground.

## Finding 7 — The room is commissioned and sent, and there is no church behind the sending; the design's stated success condition makes this worse, not neutral. MED-HIGH

**What is wrong.** The author names the gap:

> "The pilot also has no ecclesial location: it runs in a school rather than a church, the families belong to several congregations, none of which is party to the covenant or receives those this year commissions, and a formation program that sends without naming who receives has left something unsaid."

That is honest, and it is filed under what the year "cannot show" — that is, as a limit on generalizability. It is not a limit on generalizability. It is a defect in the formation, and the paper contains the evidence for that without noticing.

Week 22 is a *commissioning*. Commissioning is an ecclesial act; it presupposes a body that sends and a body that receives. Here it is performed by a fellowship whose leader is also its designer, its researcher, its covenant author, and the selector and former of everyone else with standing to object, on behalf of no church, toward no church.

Now set that beside the design's own success condition:

> "by spring the cohort will show the one outcome I actually designed for, which no instrument elicits but every instrument serves: people who have tasted what a room like this is and have become unwilling to go back to rooms without it."

Read that sentence with the ecclesial gap in front of it. The rooms these families will go back to are their congregations. The stated designed-for outcome of the year is that participants become unwilling to return to them. I do not think the author means this. But it is what the sentence says, and the design gives it a whole year of formation pressure behind it.

The pattern extends. Every occurrence of *church* in the body of this article is either a problem or an absence: "church metrics," "churches are not exempt from it," "church-programming culture," "church small groups" drifting toward comfortable support, and "no ecclesial location." Not once does the church appear as the body into which these families are being formed, or as the ordinary place where the hearing they are learning gets obeyed. The paper's account of the room is that it is the instrument and the context of formation. It does not ask what the room is *for*, and a fellowship of the heart with no answer to that question is on the well-worn road to becoming a person's real church without ever having said so.

**Why it matters.** This is the oldest failure mode of parachurch formation, and this journal's readers will see it immediately. It is also, unlike most of my findings, cheap to fix.

**Remedy.**

1. Reframe the ecclesial gap out of the generalizability section and into Design Commitments, as an acknowledged defect with a stated mitigation.
2. Name the receiver. Before week 22, each family names the congregation to which they are being sent, and the commissioning names it aloud. Nothing in the constraints prevents this; it needs no permission because it involves no page.
3. Rewrite the "unwilling to go back" sentence. What the author wants — I am fairly sure — is people unwilling to go back to *shallowness*, who therefore carry this into their congregations. Say that instead, because as written the design is measuring successful disaffection.

## Finding 8 — The construct is named vertically and read horizontally for the whole first year. MED-HIGH

**What is wrong.** The paper names its underlying construct:

> "The companion team watches three vital signs, three readings of one underlying construct, confidence in God under the load of ordinary weeks."

Now read Table 2's lower cells, which are the only cells the pilot year will substantially populate — the author expects full forms "at least occasionally by spring," and the first quarter's win is explicitly "the first forms of the three signs steady."

- First forms: "Answering: when invited, people say something real, however small" / "Settling: the room lands when the container opens" / "Prompted noticing: pointed toward someone, somebody goes."
- Growing forms: "Offering: something brought unprompted..." / "Absorbing: a late arrival or an awkward moment taken in stride..." / "Naming: the missing and the quiet named unprompted."

Six cells. God appears in none of them. Every one is a social behavior toward the room, readable in full by someone who does not believe in God, and indistinguishable from group cohesion. Only the three full-form cells contain the vertical relation ("Telling what God said and what obeying it cost"; "Real load carried toward God"), and those are the cells the design predicts will be scarce or absent all year.

So for the great majority of the pilot's readings, a construct named *confidence in God* is inferred entirely from behavior toward other people. There is an available warrant for that inference — the Johannine one, love of brother as evidence of love of God — but the paper does not make it, and the inference is defeasible in both directions: a person can be warm, present, and generous in a room while their prayer is dead, and a person in a dry season can be interiorly faithful and horizontally quiet.

**Why it matters.** This is the construct-validity question that a spiritual formation desk asks and a methods panel does not, and it compounds Finding 1: the reason the design cannot distinguish Spirit-formed from well-facilitated is partly that its readable indicators contain no reference to God.

**Remedy.** Either (a) rename the construct to what is actually read for three quarters — the room's practiced trust, read horizontally — and state the vertical construct as what the full forms alone reach; or (b) keep the name, state the theological warrant for the inference explicitly, and state its defeasibility. And add at least one God-referring cell at the first or growing rung. There is an obvious candidate the liturgy already produces: the room's response when someone says the practice went dry.

## Finding 9 — Confession is operationally defined by Edmondson, and the warrant runs backward. MEDIUM

**What is wrong.**

> "our confession-and-restoration night is Edmondson's 'mistakes' item lived liturgically; our costly telling is her 'hard issues' item with God in the sentence; our standing welcome for the honest skeptic is her 'different' item given a chair."

The author is careful to say "I did not design the container from that literature, but the convergence is striking." Convergence would be fine. But the convergence does not stay a convergence — in the practitioner's markers it becomes the criterion:

> "The first three sit close to the items by which psychological safety is actually measured"

So the middle-quarter wins are operationally Edmondson's items. Bonhoeffer is in the paper, but in a footnote, in a list, behind Strathern and Vazire.

The criterion of Christian confession is not whether mistakes are held against people. It is whether sin is named as sin before God and the brother, and whether forgiveness is spoken. The article's own vocabulary shows the substitution: "mistakes," "lapse," "failure," and the word *sin* nowhere in the piece. "Restoration" is the right word and it appears once, as the name of a session.

**Why it matters.** This is exactly the integration failure that transformational psychology exists to name: the social-science construct silently supplying the operational content of a theological practice, so that what is finally measured is the natural correlate and the practice's own criterion goes unread.

**Remedy.** State the ordering explicitly — Scripture and the tradition give the practice its content and criterion; Edmondson names a natural correlate that a secular team can also produce, whose presence is therefore not by itself evidence of formation. Then add to the markers the item Edmondson has no equivalent for and the design currently cannot see: whether anything was named as *sin* rather than as a mistake, and whether forgiveness was spoken to the person who named it.

## Finding 10 — The one-practice rule counts persistence in a discipline, and the incentive gradient runs against the discouraged. MEDIUM

**What is wrong.** The design's disconfirmation condition:

> "The practice-survival count at the third break no higher than at the first"

Persistence in a practice is thus a predicted and read outcome. But persistence in a discipline is the achievement most easily produced by the flesh and by ordinary conscientiousness, and it is the point at which a means of grace becomes a work — a subtler self-atonement performed under a devotional name. The paper never says what it thinks the practices *are*. Without that sentence, "one practice per week, never two" reads as technique, and its stated justification is candid about the pressure it creates: the rule "is also psychometric: one practice per week makes practice survival across breaks a clean, countable trace."

The author has partly seen this, and his solution is genuinely good: "an honest report of a lapsed practice is telling with a small price" — the lapse itself can score. But look at the resulting gradient. Persisting scores. Lapsing-and-saying-so scores. The only unrewarded state is lapsing and staying quiet — which is exactly the state of the person most ashamed, and the person the year most needs to reach. Compounding it, the debrief carries "follow-ups under a forty-eight-hour rule, each with a name, an owner, and a deadline," so a quiet lapse can become a named follow-up on a lead companion's page.

**Why it matters.** A curriculum whose measurement gradient bears down hardest on the discouraged has inverted its own pastoral commitments, and it will do so invisibly, because the mechanism is an incentive rather than a rule.

**Remedy.** Add the missing sentence: the practices are means of grace, places of receptivity, not achievements, and their persistence is a trace worth counting only because it is cheap, never because it is the thing. Then add the handbook rule: a season of not practicing is a legitimate report, is not a follow-up, and generates no owner or deadline.

## Finding 11 — "Nothing is ever asked" is contradicted by the liturgy the paper describes. MEDIUM

**What is wrong.**

> "Nothing in the year is demanded: practices are offered, leadership is offered, even the faith commitment at the curriculum's center is stated plainly once and then left standing as an open door."

Set against the session narration:

> "The tell step follows in clusters: each person tells one thing they heard, a parent opening."

*Each person tells.* In a sequential circle of six to fifteen, in front of one's own family, the ask is structural whether or not it is verbalized. The paper's protection of the freedom to decline is real but lives entirely on the paper side — "a young person who would rather not fill a page does not have to" — and pages are the one thing that never happens in the room. There is no described mechanism by which a participant declines the tell step, and no evidence in the article that anyone has ever seen one decline.

The same applies, more seriously, to the faith commitment. "Stated plainly once and then left standing as an open door" is the right instinct, but an open door in a small warm room, with a beloved leader, in front of one's parents who said yes in public, is not a neutral architecture for a minor.

**Why it matters.** The freedom of a response is the condition of its being a response to God rather than to a room. This is the paper's own deepest commitment, and its liturgy has not caught up with it.

**Remedy.** Use the paper's own best mechanism. The leader feedback round works because "receiving it looks like strength because it has only ever been modeled as strength." Apply that to declining: early in the first quarter, the leader passes at the tell step, once, without explanation and without apology. A modeled pass makes the freedom real in a way that a stated policy cannot, and it costs one turn.

## Finding 12 — The design has an account of the room's lapses and none of the room's sin against itself. MEDIUM

**What is wrong.** The only account of trouble in the room is developmental:

> "open disagreement tends to arrive before mature trust, not after it... the first honestly-processed disagreement is usually a milestone, not a malfunction."

Sometimes. And sometimes the trouble is a broken confidence, a family humiliated by a story told outside the circle, a teen mocked in a hallway on Thursday, a Companion who talks. The covenant governs the *team's* handling of data. Nothing in the article governs what happens when the container is broken by a participant, and there is no repair liturgy, no account of repentance between members, and — the author having already named the ecclesial gap — no discipline available. "Confession and restoration" is a night in the first quarter, not a standing capacity of the room.

**Why it matters.** The whole architecture rests on confidentiality holding. The one event that would end the year's usefulness has no protocol, and the year would have no way to record it if it happened, because Table 2 has no cell for it.

**Remedy.** Give the handbook a repair liturgy, and give the reading a place for it. A broken confidence repaired in the open is a higher-order sign than anything in Table 2 and should be recorded as such, at the room level, which keeps the constraint intact.

## Finding 13 — The weep test is an internal test of the measurer's affection. MEDIUM

**What is wrong.**

> "a closing personal test, measure nothing you will not weep over. This weep test may be the most radical suggestion of all."

It is a fine test and I have no wish to lose it. But note what it is: a test of the shepherd's own sentiment, applied by the shepherd, about people he loves. It is entirely internal, and the author's own limitations paragraph tells us why that is a problem here — "the covenant was written by the designer and signed by a team he selected and formed." A man can weep over what he should never have measured; affection is not a check on affectionate overreach, it is its engine.

The design already has the one external instrument it needs and does not use it for this. The quarterly outside reader is asked a single question — "judging only by what this program does, what does it appear to believe?" — which is a good question and the wrong one for this purpose.

**Why it matters.** The covenant is doing most of the ethical load-bearing in a design that concedes it has no other external check.

**Remedy.** Give the outside reader a second question, and record the answer as given, as the first one already is: *is there anything here you would not want done to your own family?* It costs nothing, violates no constraint, and gives the weep test the one thing it lacks — an edge that is not the author's own.

## Finding 14 — The leader's humility is a scheduled performance that immediately converts into demonstrated competence. LOW

**What is wrong.** The Leader Feedback Round has three movements, and the third is the teach-back:

> "'if I were to teach tonight's process to someone, here is what I would tell them,' a consolidation move the learning literature would recognize as a reflective practice and the room experiences as humility."

The sequence is: the leader is corrected, then the leader immediately demonstrates what he learned, in the form of instruction. That is a good pedagogical move and I do not doubt its cognitive warrant. But the affective shape of it is that being corrected always terminates in the leader being competent again, in public, within the same minute. Over twenty-two weeks that teaches the room that correction is survivable *because it can be converted*, which is not the same lesson as correction being survivable.

**Why it matters.** The author is explicit that this is the model from which teenagers learn to receive correction. It is worth being precise about what is being modeled.

**Remedy.** Once a quarter, the leader receives the round and does not do the teach-back. Says thank you, and sits down.

## Finding 15 — The article describes a formation architecture and almost no formation content. LOW

**What is wrong.** In an article on hearing and obeying God, the described content of the year is: a six-step Scripture-hearing practice, a morning question, Romans 10:17 for six minutes, Mark 1:14–20 walked together, a confession-and-restoration night, a mission quarter, a family night, and a commissioning. The word *Christ* does not appear in the article; *gospel* appears once, in a parenthesis explaining that Romans 10:17 is about something other than what the session uses it for; *worship* appears only in a book title in the bibliography.

I accept that this is a protocol article and the curriculum is published elsewhere — footnote 10 makes the materials available and that is to the author's credit. But a reviewer at this journal is being asked to assess a *formation* design, and what is on the page is an architecture that would accept nearly any content. The paper's own second principle says the unit is the practice and not the lesson; that is a good principle and it does not entail that the object of the practice can go unnamed.

**Remedy.** One paragraph in "The Shape of the Year" naming the christological and scriptural spine of the twenty-two weeks — what is heard, from where, and toward whom the obeying runs. Half a page buys the article the thing a reader currently has to take on trust.

## Summary of the recommendation

Findings 1–4 are one finding seen four ways: the design has a natural account of formation, a natural account of maturity, no account of sin, and no account of discernment, and it therefore cannot tell its own stated cause from its stated non-cause. Everything else on this list follows from that or sits beside it.

None of the remedies I have proposed requires abandoning a constraint except Finding 6's request for one scheduled reversal of the parents-first rule, which I have argued on the author's own theological ground. Most of them cost a paragraph, a column, or a question. The author's instinct — that a design should publish the ways it could be wrong — is the right instinct, and my central complaint is that he has applied it everywhere except to the one claim on which the whole thing rests.

I would be glad to see this in the journal after revision. There is a real contribution here.
