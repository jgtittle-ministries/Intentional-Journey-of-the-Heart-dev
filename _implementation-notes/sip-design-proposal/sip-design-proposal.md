# Stage Integration Profile (SIP) — Design Proposal

**Status:** brainstorm; not yet committed to the published site. Lives in `_implementation-notes/` so it stays out of `docs/` and doesn't break the strict build.

**Date:** 2026-05-25 (draft 1)

**Context:** the AT integration reframe pilot landed across Vols 1–5 markdown in five dev→mirror cycles. The §2 sub-section in Vol 4 names that *measured* AT level is honest only when earlier-stage openness remains active. The §3 calibration touches (Quick Reference footer, STI-A divergence flag, LotS-H3 clarification) and the §4b GATS closure shadow rating **flag** the integration check but do not **measure** it. Assessors currently have to infer integration-vs-closure qualitatively from PSFA + STI-A + NRP convergence. This proposal closes that operational gap with a forced-choice profile instrument modeled on the OCAI's *structural pattern* (not its copyrighted items).

---

## Why this instrument

The §2 reframe argues that mature formation is the *integration* of all five AT stages, not the replacement of earlier by later. The canonical failure mode is the Pharisee-as-stage-N-collapse pattern: surface behavior at a high stage while earlier-stage openness has died. The current PSFA classifies a participant at their highest consistent stage. Under the integration reading, that classification is honest only when earlier-stage openness is still active — and the PSFA does not check for that. A Stage 4 PSFA score that is in fact masking Stage 1 collapse looks identical to a Stage 4 score that is genuinely integrated. The instrument cannot tell them apart.

A profile instrument — one that asks not "what's your stage?" but "how alive is each kind of engagement in you right now?" — would surface the closure pattern directly. The OCAI's forced-choice distribution format is well-suited to this because it forces relative weighting: the respondent cannot mark themselves "high on everything." They must allocate finite attention across the five stages, which is exactly what the integration check needs.

## The OCAI structural pattern (what we're adapting)

Cameron & Quinn's OCAI in *Diagnosing and Changing Organizational Culture* uses a forced-choice profile architecture that is structurally well-suited to integration measurement. The specific items, dimension wording, and the four CVF quadrant content are copyrighted; the **structural pattern** is in the public domain and is what we adapt:

- **N observation dimensions.** Each names a context where the underlying values show up. (For organizations: dominant characteristics, leadership style, employee management, organizational glue, strategic emphases, success criteria — six dimensions in the original.)
- **K options per dimension, one per value/quadrant.** Each option describes what that value would look like in that specific context.
- **Forced-choice 100-point distribution.** The respondent distributes 100 points across the K options for each dimension, based on how strongly each describes their actual current situation.
- **Two passes — Current and Preferred.** Each dimension is scored twice: first as it actually is now, then as the respondent thinks it should be in five years (or some chosen horizon).
- **Profile output.** Results plotted as a kite/radar across the K values; current and preferred are overlaid; the gap between them marks the change work.

This pattern translates cleanly to AT integration measurement: replace the four CVF quadrants with five AT stages, replace organizational contexts with personal (or small-group) formation contexts. The forced-choice distribution does the integration work — a respondent who tries to mark themselves "high on everything" cannot, and a closed Stage 4 person who would have classified as Stage 4 on the PSFA will visibly profile as low-Receiving / high-Organization, which is the closure signature.

## SIP-Individual: design

**Six dimensions of personal spiritual engagement.** Each names a context in which the five kinds of AT engagement show up. For each dimension, five options describe what that engagement looks like at each stage. Respondent distributes 100 points across the five options based on how alive each is *right now*. Then repeats for *preferred*.

### Dimension 1: My engagement with scripture

How does scripture land when I encounter it — whether a new passage or a familiar one I'm reading again today?

- **A (Receiving).** I show up to scripture willing to hear it but without much engagement of my own yet. I read or listen; the passage doesn't yet reach me, but I'm here.
- **B (Responding).** I engage scripture with curiosity. I ask questions of the text, notice what's interesting, and follow up on what catches my attention.
- **C (Valuing).** I find myself applying what I read to my own life as I read. The passage costs me something — a recognition, a conviction, a behavioral shift I begin to plan.
- **D (Organization).** Scripture is part of how I structure my life. The passages I've engaged with feed into how I make decisions across multiple domains, not just the immediate one.
- **E (Characterization).** Scripture has shaped who I am to the point that I no longer notice the integration. Others would describe me primarily in terms of how I live what I have read.

*Distribute 100 points across A–E for Current. Then repeat for Preferred.*

### Dimension 2: My response to challenge or correction

When someone disagrees with me on a matter of faith, shows me something I missed, or points out a pattern in me I hadn't seen — what happens in me?

- **A (Receiving).** I notice myself willing to listen. The challenge reaches me, even if I don't know what to do with it yet.
- **B (Responding).** I engage actively — ask follow-up questions, try to understand the other person's reasoning, look at the evidence they're pointing to.
- **C (Valuing).** I find myself willing to be wrong. If the challenge holds up, I let it cost me — apologize, change a behavior, revise a position.
- **D (Organization).** I treat correction as part of the ongoing formation work. It feeds back into how I make future decisions in adjacent areas, not just the immediate one.
- **E (Characterization).** Receptivity to correction is part of who I am. Others would describe me as someone who can be told the truth.

### Dimension 3: How scripture shapes my decisions

When a real decision intersects with what I've been reading or hearing — at work, with money, in relationships, with how I spend my time — what role does scripture actually play?

- **A (Receiving).** I'm aware that scripture has something to say in this area, even if I don't yet know what.
- **B (Responding).** I look for what scripture says about decisions like this one. I read, ask, study.
- **C (Valuing).** I let what I find from scripture cost me. I make the decision the way the passage actually points to, even when it's hard.
- **D (Organization).** Decisions across multiple life domains consistently reflect what scripture has formed in me. I don't think about it; the formation is already in the way I weigh options.
- **E (Characterization).** The way I make decisions has become a defining mark. People who know me would say my decisions look like a person whose life is governed by what God has said.

### Dimension 4: My presence in community

In the small group room, in church, with other believers — what kind of presence do I bring?

- **A (Receiving).** I show up. I am there. I listen to what others say.
- **B (Responding).** I participate. I share what I'm thinking, ask genuine questions, build on what others bring.
- **C (Valuing).** I take real personal risks in community — share a conviction, name a hard thing about myself, let the group see what is actually happening in me.
- **D (Organization).** I am consistently the kind of person in community whom others can rely on for honest engagement. The character of my presence holds across sessions.
- **E (Characterization).** My presence has shaped what is normal in the community I am part of. Others have grown into deeper community partly because of how I have been with them.

### Dimension 5: My response to suffering or unexpected difficulty

When something hard happens — loss, conflict, illness, betrayal, public failure, the slow grind of an unresolved situation — what happens in my engagement with God?

- **A (Receiving).** I stay in the conversation with God through it. I do not close.
- **B (Responding).** I bring the suffering actively into prayer and scripture. I ask questions; I look at what is happening.
- **C (Valuing).** I let the suffering do its formation work. I make the costly choices the situation requires, including the spiritual ones.
- **D (Organization).** I draw on what previous suffering has formed in me. The integration of past trials feeds into how I navigate this one.
- **E (Characterization).** Suffering does not change who I am with God; it deepens it. Others see in me what Brueggemann calls reorientation, what Job describes as moving from hearing about God to seeing him.

### Dimension 6: The Tell — what I do with what I have received

After I have heard from God or from scripture, what crosses the threshold from interior reception to spoken word and outward action?

- **A (Receiving).** I notice what I have received. I hold it.
- **B (Responding).** I speak about what I have received in safe contexts — to spouse, friend, small group.
- **C (Valuing).** I tell people who specifically need to hear it, including people outside my close circle, including when it costs me something to say.
- **D (Organization).** Telling is part of how I live. The Romans 10 chain — what I have received becomes word that becomes formation in others — runs naturally through my daily life.
- **E (Characterization).** My life carries the word outward as a defining mark. The Tell is not a discipline I practice but the shape of who I am.

### Scoring and output

Each dimension produces:
- A current profile (5 numbers summing to 100)
- A preferred profile (5 numbers summing to 100)
- A current/preferred gap per stage

Aggregating across the six dimensions produces:
- A **whole-person current profile** (5 numbers, sum to 600 — or normalized back to 100)
- A **whole-person preferred profile** (same)
- A **stage gap vector** showing which stages have the largest current-to-preferred gap

Visualization: a five-point radar chart with current and preferred overlaid. The shape of the kite tells the story at a glance.

### Closure detection (the integration check operationalized)

The Pharisee-as-stage-N-collapse pattern produces a specific signature on the SIP. A canonical closed Stage 4 person would show, on the current profile:

- **A (Receiving):** very low — e.g., 5–10 out of 100 per dimension
- **B (Responding):** low — 10–15
- **C (Valuing):** moderate — 15–20
- **D (Organization):** very high — 45–55
- **E (Characterization):** moderate — 15–20

The shape is unmistakable: a profile dominated by D with A starved. A genuinely integrated Stage 4 person would show high D but also healthy A and B (e.g., A: 15, B: 20, C: 20, D: 30, E: 15). The presence of A and B is the integration check.

The simplest rule: **any stage scoring below 10 points on a dimension is flagged as collapse-suspect.** Multiple low-A dimensions in the same profile constitute the closure signature. The assessor reads the flagged dimensions back to the participant as the starting point for the integration conversation: "Where has the kind of openness Stage 1 names gone quiet in you?"

This is the single operational move the SIP makes that the PSFA cannot: it surfaces the closure pattern automatically rather than requiring qualitative inference.

## SIP-Group: design

Same structural pattern, six group-level dimensions, five options per dimension representing the group culture at each stage. Administered to each group member individually (assessing the group's culture, not their own); aggregated to produce the group's profile. The facilitator completes one separately; gaps between facilitator's reading and member aggregate are diagnostic.

### Dimension 1: How the group approaches a scripture passage

At the start of a session, when the group encounters the passage for the day — what is the group's collective engagement?

- **A (Receiving).** The group shows up and listens together.
- **B (Responding).** The group engages actively — questions, follow-ups, building on each other.
- **C (Valuing).** The group lets the passage cost something — personal conviction shared, application named at personal cost, real repentance possible.
- **D (Organization).** The group's collective decisions and culture consistently reflect what scripture has formed in this group together.
- **E (Characterization).** The group has become known — to itself, to others — as the kind of community where scripture takes root and bears fruit. Other communities are formed by what was formed here.

### Dimension 2: How the group handles a hard or unfamiliar passage

When the text resists the group's existing framings — when it says something none of them want to hear, or names something none of them know how to integrate — what does the group do?

- **A (Receiving).** The group sits with the discomfort without immediately resolving it.
- **B (Responding).** The group engages the difficulty actively — multiple perspectives, honest wrestling, willingness to be confused together.
- **C (Valuing).** The group lets the hard passage change something in how it operates. The discomfort does formation work, not avoidance work.
- **D (Organization).** The group has internalized that the hard passages are part of the formation, not interruptions to it. The cultural pattern of facing the hard is consistent.
- **E (Characterization).** The group's willingness to be confronted by scripture is part of what defines it. New members are formed by it.

### Dimension 3: How the group welcomes a new voice

When a new member, a visitor, or someone with a different background or framework enters — what happens?

- **A (Receiving).** The group is genuinely open to hearing what the new person brings.
- **B (Responding).** The group engages the new voice — asks them questions, integrates their contributions into the conversation.
- **C (Valuing).** The group is willing to let a new voice change the conversation. Costly accommodation, not surface welcome.
- **D (Organization).** Welcoming new voices is part of how the group functions. The pattern is consistent across visits and new members.
- **E (Characterization).** The group's openness has become part of its identity. Outsiders experience it without being told.

### Dimension 4: How the group's prayer life functions

What kind of prayer happens? Who initiates? What is the content? Is the group a praying community or a community that prays?

- **A (Receiving).** The group prays together. People are willing to be in prayer with each other.
- **B (Responding).** The group prays actively — naming specific things, building on what is named, varying voices.
- **C (Valuing).** Prayer in the group reaches into the real life of the members. People bring what is actually at stake. Conviction, repentance, real intercession.
- **D (Organization).** Prayer is consistently part of how the group functions across sessions, not just an opening or closing ritual. The prayer life shapes the rest of the session.
- **E (Characterization).** The group is recognizable as a praying community. Prayer is the medium through which the group lives, not a practice the group does.

### Dimension 5: How the group handles conflict, correction, or disagreement

When two members disagree, when someone needs to be told a hard truth, when an interpretation is contested — what does the group do?

- **A (Receiving).** The group can hear that conflict is present without suppressing it.
- **B (Responding).** The group engages the disagreement — both sides heard, the substance worked through, not just managed politely.
- **C (Valuing).** The group lets the conflict do its work. Real correction happens. Real apologies. Real change.
- **D (Organization).** The group has consistent patterns for navigating disagreement that work. It is not a one-off; the culture handles it.
- **E (Characterization).** The group's capacity for honest conflict is part of its identity. New members and visitors notice it and are formed by it.

### Dimension 6: The group's Tell — what the group does with what it has heard

What crosses the threshold from the group room into the world the members go back to?

- **A (Receiving).** Members take what they heard with them. They notice it later.
- **B (Responding).** Members speak about what the group heard with people outside the group.
- **C (Valuing).** Members let what they heard cost them outside the group room. Behavior, decisions, conversations change.
- **D (Organization).** The Tell pattern is consistent across members and across weeks. The group's hearing reliably crosses the threshold.
- **E (Characterization).** The group is recognizable to outsiders by the fruit its members bear in the world. The Romans 10 chain runs through the group as a normal feature of its life.

### Scoring and output

Same as SIP-Individual: per-dimension current and preferred profiles, aggregated whole-group profile, stage gap vector, radar visualization.

The same closure detection rule applies: any stage scoring below 10 on a dimension is flagged. A group whose aggregate profile is high-D / high-E but starved on A and B is the **closed Stage-4 group culture** the §4b shadow rating names — what looks like advanced group formation is in fact closure.

### Group SIP relationship to existing group instruments

- **GSFP (Group Spiritual Formation Profile).** GSFP plots the distribution of individual stages within the group. Group SIP measures the group's own culture profile across the stages. These are different measures: GSFP says *which members are at which stages*; Group SIP says *what the group's collective formation environment looks like*. Complementary, not redundant.
- **CSFA (Collective Spiritual Formation Assessment).** CSFA has 12 items in three domains (corporate scripture engagement, corporate trust/safety, outward orientation). Group SIP overlaps with all three of those domains but organized around the five AT stages rather than three domains. There is real redundancy here. CSFA could potentially be folded into Group SIP, or Group SIP could supplement CSFA with the integration check that CSFA does not produce. Open design question — see below.
- **GCS (Group Cohesion Scale).** GCS measures psychological safety in the group; quite distinct from Group SIP. Complementary.
- **SOP (Session Observation Protocol).** SOP is a facilitator-side session-by-session checklist; distinct from Group SIP. Complementary.

## Replace/simplify analysis: where SIP fits in Vol 4's instrument suite

The user's question: could SIP replace or simplify one of the existing tools?

**Recommendation: SIP replaces the AT-stage portion of the PSFA.** The PSFA's 4Rs portion is preserved. The PSFA either becomes a smaller two-section instrument (SIP for AT, retained 4Rs section for Image of God) or splits into two named instruments (SIP + IoG-Profile).

**Reasoning:**

1. The PSFA's AT-stage section was designed under the stage-replacement reading. It produces a single highest-consistent-stage classification. Under the integration reading, that classification is **necessary but not sufficient** — and the §3 calibration touches now have to add interpretive caveats to compensate for what the instrument structurally cannot tell you. The SIP produces both a stage profile and an integration check natively; the caveats become unnecessary because the instrument answers the question directly.
2. Item count is comparable. PSFA AT-stage section is roughly half of the 25-item total (~12 items). SIP-Individual is six dimensions, each scored twice (current + preferred), totaling 12 forced-choice distributions. Time-on-task is similar; cognitive demand may actually be lower because forced-choice distribution is a more focused task than rating 12 separate Likert items.
3. The 4Rs section of the PSFA covers a different construct (Image of God reflection) that the SIP does not address. Keep it.
4. The §3 calibration touches (Quick Reference footer, STI-A divergence flag, LotS-H3 clarification) all become **less load-bearing** when the instrument itself surfaces the integration pattern. They can be tightened or in some cases removed.

**At the group level:** Group SIP could replace the CSFA, but the case is weaker. CSFA's three-domain structure (corporate engagement / trust / outward) is a different cut than Group SIP's six-dimension structure. They measure overlapping but distinct things. Possible options:

- **Option A:** Group SIP replaces CSFA. The three CSFA domains are reorganized as three of the Group SIP dimensions (rather than the six proposed above). Simpler instrument suite, some information loss.
- **Option B:** Group SIP supplements CSFA. Both administered at baseline / Month 6 / Month 12. Heavier participant load but richer data.
- **Option C:** Group SIP and CSFA become alternating quarterly administrations rather than both at every assessment point. Reduces load without losing either measure.

I'd recommend **Option C** as the default — preserves both instruments' contributions while reducing total survey load.

## Open design questions

1. **How many dimensions?** I proposed six (matching the OCAI's six-dimension original). The argument for fewer: simpler instrument, lower respondent load. The argument for six: each dimension catches a different context where stage-N collapse can hide. (A Pharisee can have high Tell capacity — Dimension 6 — and very low Receiving — Dimension 1 — and the asymmetry across dimensions is itself diagnostic.) I lean toward keeping six.
2. **Anchor description length.** I wrote relatively full one-to-two-sentence anchors. They could be tighter — single phrases per option. The argument for tighter: faster administration. The argument for fuller: pastoral specificity, especially for the C/Valuing and E/Characterization stages where the difference between "external compliance" and "internal commitment" is the whole game. I lean toward keeping the fuller anchors for the published instrument and offering a single-phrase "express version" for repeated administrations.
3. **Forced-choice vs Likert fallback.** OCAI users sometimes find the forced-choice 100-point distribution cognitively demanding. A Likert fallback (rate each option 1–5 independently) loses the integration constraint that makes the SIP work — but it might be necessary for some participants. Open question whether to offer a fallback or hold firm on the forced-choice format.
4. **Current/Preferred horizon.** OCAI uses "five years from now." For spiritual formation, "one year from now" matches the Vol 4 testing protocol's twelve-month arc. Use that.
5. **Visualization standard.** Radar chart is the OCAI default. For a print/PDF context the radar works. For a markdown-rendered context, an ASCII bar chart or a simple table may be cleaner. Open.
6. **How does SIP interact with the NRP narrative data?** The NRP's qualitative entries should illuminate the SIP's quantitative profile — particularly the closure-flagged dimensions. NRP prompts could be adapted to ask specifically about the lowest-scoring SIP stages.
7. **Group SIP aggregation rule.** Mean across members? Median? Some other aggregation? OCAI typically uses mean; we should follow unless there's a reason not to.
8. **Naming.** "Stage Integration Profile" (SIP) is a working name. Other candidates: "AT Integration Profile" (ATIP), "Five-Stage Profile" (FSP), "Integration Check Instrument" (ICI). Open.
